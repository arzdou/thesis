\subsection*{Single-shot readout of a $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ nuclear spin in diamond}

\begin{marginfigure}

In its negatively charged state, the system has $S=1$ and can be controlled with microwave pulses (in blue). Single-shot readout is performed by measuring the spin-selective optical fluorescence (in red). A green laser is used to readout and prepare the state of the NV center (in green). The figure was taken from \cite{neumann_singleshot_2010}.}
    \labfig{sota_nv2}
\end{marginfigure}

Nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers are point defects consisting of a substitutional nitrogen atom adjacent to a vacancy in the diamond lattice, as shown in \reffig{sota_nv2}. This defect forms a localized electronic state within the diamond band gap. In its negatively charged state, the NV center possesses a paramagnetic ground state with total spin $S=1$. The corresponding energy levels as a function of magnetic field are shown in \reffig{sota_nv1}a. The electron spin levels are further split by the hyperfine coupling to the $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ nuclear spin.

The optical transitions exhibit spin-dependent fluorescence intensity, a property that enabled the detection of the spin state of an individual NV center using optical excitation~\sidecite{jelezko_single_2002}, followed shortly by the detection of nearby single $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ nuclear spins~\sidecite{jelezko_observation_2004a}. 

%However, the nuclear spin readout scheme was not single-shot; multiple repetitions were required to accumulate a detectable signal, thereby obscuring the intrinsic quantum nature of the spin dynamics. The primary limitation was a reduced effective nuclear spin lifetime $T_1$, caused by cross-relaxation events induced by the transverse hyperfine coupling during optical interrogation with a green laser~\sidecite{robledo_spin_2011}.

Single-shot readout of the $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ nuclear spin was achieved by Neumann \textit{et al.} in 2010~\sidecite{neumann_singleshot_2010}. In the experiment, the electron spin is initialized in the $m_S=0$ state. A nuclear-spin-selective microwave $\pi$-pulse is then applied, transferring the electron population to the $m_S=-1$ state conditional on the nuclear spin state. This selective mapping is possible because the hyperfine splitting of the EPR-allowed transitions is stronger than the decoherence rate of the electron spin. Then, the electron spin is read-out optically by measuring the photoluminescence under a green laser pulsed excitation. Therefore, if the $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ is in state $m_I=-1$ the PL should be low, whereas it should be high if the $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ is in state $m_I=0$ or $m_I=+1$. The fluorescence trace shown in \reffig{sota_nv1}b reveals real-time quantum-jumps of the nuclear spin state.

The time trace shows that the nuclear spin can be interrogated repeatedly before a state change occurs. Under laser excitation, the electron spin is reset in the ground state. Nevertheless, the nuclear spin state remains stable for many cycles. Therefore, the protocol is a projective, single-shot, Quantum Non-Demolition (QND) measurement of the nuclear spin state~\sidecite{imoto_quantum_1985}. The average number of readout cycles before a nuclear spin flip defines an effective nuclear $T_1$ under measurement.

This effective lifetime ultimately limits both the readout fidelity and the degree to which the measurement can be considered truly QND. Nuclear spin flips are induced by cross-relaxation through the ZQ/DQ transitions, which occur during optical excitation when the electron spin state is scrambled. As discussed in \refsec{driving_nuclear_spin_half}, the probability of such cross-relaxation events scales inversely with the nuclear Larmor frequency. Consequently, increasing the applied magnetic field enhances the nuclear spin lifetime. \reffig{sota_nv1}c shows the measured effective nuclear $T_1$ as a function of magnetic field and is fit to a model that accounts for cross-relaxation via ZQ/DQ transitions. This confirmed that these processes dominate the nuclear spin relaxation under optical interrogation.

%Over the years, several strategies have been developed to further mitigate this limitation. Replacing off-resonant green excitation with resonant red laser excitation suppresses back-action on the nuclear spin~\sidecite{robledo_highfidelity_2011}. Additionally, incorporating solid immersion lenses or optical cavities significantly enhances photon collection efficiency, which reduces the number of required laser pulses~\sidecite{schroder_ultrabright_2011}.

In contrast to optical fluorescence-based readout, electrical detection schemes exploit spin-to-charge conversion mechanisms. One such technique couples a neutral donor in silicon, such as a phosphorus impurity, to a single-electron transistor (SET). The SET current is finite only within specific electrochemical potential windows, and the tunneling of a single electron is sufficient to switch the current. By applying a strong magnetic field $B_0$, spin-dependent tunneling can be engineered, as the Zeeman effect lifts the degeneracy of the electron spin states. Therefore, current flowing through the device signals the state of the electron spin, enabling single-shot electron spin readout~\sidecite{morello_singleshot_2010}.

The first detection of an individual nuclear spin using this architecture was demonstrated by J. Pla \textit{et al.} in 2013~\sidecite{pla_highfidelity_2013}. The device used in their work is shown in \reffig{sota_p}a. Four electrostatic gates control the electrochemical potential of the donor. The electron spin is driven using a microwave magnetic field $B_1$ generated by an on-chip transmission line.

In its neutral state, the donor hosts both an electron and a nuclear spin, coupled via the Fermi contact interaction. The energy level diagram of the coupled system is shown in \reffig{sota_p}b. Compared to the regime discussed in \refsec{w_er_hamiltonian}, the hyperfine interaction strength exceeds the nuclear Zeeman splitting, such that $\omega_S > A > \omega_I$. When the donor is ionized, the electron is removed and only the nuclear spin remains.

Figure \reffig{sota_p}c shows electron spin resonance spectroscopy. After applying a microwave $\pi$-pulse, two well-resolved peaks in the excited-state population $f_\uparrow$ are observed. The resonance frequency depends on the state of the coupled nuclear spin. These peaks correspond to the two EPR-allowed transitions and are separated by the hyperfine coupling strength, $A \approx 120$ MHz.

By monitoring the difference in excited-state population between the two resonance frequencies, $\Delta f_\uparrow = f_\uparrow(\nu_{e2}) - f_\uparrow(\nu_{e1})$, the nuclear spin state can be discriminated with a fidelity exceeding $0.998$. Observation of the nuclear spin dynamics (shown in \reffig{sota_p}d) reveals the presence of a shorter-lived spin state. This effect is attributed to electron-nuclear flip-flop processes occurring during electron spin readout. The authors mitigate this mechanism by increasing the donor ionization rate, thereby suppressing unwanted spin exchange during measurement.

\subsection*{Conclusion}

The two experiments, authors rely on the hyperfine interaction between a nuclear spin and a proximal electron spin to enable single-shot nuclear spin readout. Fundamentally, they resolve the hyperfine splitting of the EPR-allowed transitions. In both approaches, successful discrimination of the nuclear spin state requires that the hyperfine coupling exceed the electron spin linewidth, ensuring that the EPR transitions are spectrally resolvable.

Such strong hyperfine interactions are typically achieved when the electron and nuclear spins are coupled through the Fermi contact interaction, leading to coupling strengths on the order of several megahertz. In contrast, dipolar interactions with more distant nuclear spins are significantly weaker and therefore more challenging to resolve spectroscopically. Nevertheless, advances in dynamical decoupling have enabled the detection of large ensembles of weakly coupled distant $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ spins using a single NV center~\sidecite{vandestolpe_mapping_2024}.

Using magnetically silent substrates is a promising path towards single nuclear spin detection. In this context, \Ca has demonstrated exceptionally long electron spin coherence times in ensembles~\sidecite{ledantec_twentythreemillisecond_2021}. Such narrow intrinsic linewidths relax the requirement for large hyperfine coupling strengths. Consequently, \W nuclear spins that are coupled through magnetic dipole interaction to an \Er impurity should, in principle, be spectroscopically resolvable even without resorting to dynamical decoupling techniques. This is the topic of the present chapter.

After identifying and characterizing individual \Er ions, we perform high-resolution spectroscopy by exciting the spin with an 80 \textmu s long Gaussian-shaped microwave $\pi$-pulse, with frequency $\omega$ followed by microwave fluorescence detection, sweeping $\delta = \omega - \omega_0$ over $2\pi \times 100$\,kHz. Each sweep is averaged 200 times to obtain the ensemble-averaged number of counts $\langle C \rangle (\omega)$. \reffig{quantum_jumps}a shows such spectra measured repeatedly during 150 minutes for $\mathrm{Er}_1 - \mathrm{Er}_5$. Ion $\mathrm{Er}_1$ displays a single line, whose center frequency fluctuates on a timescale of several minutes, and on a frequency scale of $\sim 20$\,kHz. This spectral diffusion is attributed to a combination of $B_0$ drift, charge noise, and re-arrangements of the weakly coupled nuclear spin bath, and was observed for all measured ions although with a different intensity (see discussion below). Because the drift is slow, it can be compensated by various frequency-tracking strategies (see discussion below) which are used in all the measurements reported later in this chapter. In contrast, the electron spin frequencies of ions $\mathrm{Er}_2$, $\mathrm{Er}_3$, and $\mathrm{Er}_5$ display a clear telegraphic noise between two frequencies, four in the case of $\mathrm{Er}_4$. Similar to the systems discussed in \refsec{sota_ch3}, these jumps occur when one nearby $^{183}\mathrm{W}$ nuclear spin changes state. Quantum jumps are a hallmark of the measurement of individual quantum systems, and aside of nuclear spins, they have been observed for trapped ions~\sidecite{nagourney_shelved_1986,bergquist_observation_1986,sauter_observation_1986}, photons in a high-Q microwave cavity~\sidecite{gleyzes_quantum_2007,guerlin_progressive_2007}, superconducting circuits~\sidecite{vijay_observation_2011}. Here, we report their observation on individual $^{183}\mathrm{W}$ nuclear spins by microwave fluorescence detection.

The transition frequency of each spectrum $\tilde{\delta}(t)$ is obtained from a Lorentzian fit, shown as a histogram in \reffig{quantum_jumps}b for the five ions. Each histogram presents a different number of resolved distributions. For $\mathrm{Er}_2$, $\mathrm{Er}_3$ and $\mathrm{Er}_5$, the two resolved distributions indicate the presence of a strongly coupled neighboring nuclear spin. In contrast, $\mathrm{Er}_4$, shows four resolved distributions which correspond to two strongly coupled nuclear spins. The pair will be referred as $\mathrm{Er}_4^{(1)}$ and $\mathrm{Er}_4^{(2)}$ in the following. We note that the standard deviation of the individual distributions is significantly different for each ion, indicating that part of the drift originates from their local environment, likely from unresolved nuclear spin jumps.

\subsection{Time trace analysis}

The energy level structure presented in \refsec{w_er_hamiltonian} dictates that the difference in frequency between the two EPR-allowed transitions is a good estimate of the parallel hyperfine coupling $|A_\parallel|$ in the high-field regime. To extract this value we measure the difference in frequency between each time window 

\begin{equation}
    \Delta \tilde \delta (\tau) = \tilde \delta (\tau) - \tilde \delta (\tau - \Delta t), 
\end{equation}

which is plot in \reffig{trace_analysis}a as a function of time for Er$_2$. Depending on the number of strongly coupled nuclear spins, the value distribution of $\Delta \tilde \delta$ will show different number of peaks (see \reffig{trace_analysis}b).  The peak centered at $0$ arises when no resolved jump occurs in-between two consecutive traces. A Gaussian fit yields a Full-Width-Half-Maximum varying from 4.5 to 10 kHz, indicating that the short-term noise (at the minute scale) also varies significantly from ion to ion. The extra peaks observed for $\mathrm{Er}_{2-5}$ arise from resolved nuclear spin quantum jumps. Gaussian fits to these peaks yield the mean frequency jump size which corresponds to $|A_\parallel|$. The measured values are shown in \reftab{hyperfine_parallel_meas}. We note that the standard deviation of the satellite peaks is similar in value to the central peak, as expected.

We define the cross-relaxation probability as

\begin{equation}
    p_\text{ZQ, DQ} = \frac{N_{ZQ, DQ}}{N_\text{ex}}, 
\end{equation}

Because detuned $\pi$-pulses still drive the electron spin when they fall within the linewidth, every pulse contributes with a weight set by the lineshape. Thus, the total excitation count per sweep can be estimated by summing over all applied pulses, each weighted by a Lorentzian profile with the fitted linewidth.

\subsection{Tracking procedure}

\begin{marginfigure}
    \includegraphics{chapter3/figures/rough_tracking.pdf}
    \caption[Rudimentary tracking scheme]{Rudimentary tracking scheme. Ensemble average number of counts $\langle C \rangle$ as a function of the pulse frequency $\omega_r + \delta$ after polarization of the neighboring \W spins (see text). The two traces are separated by $\Delta t \sim 2$ minutes and the data (dots) are fit to a Lorentzian (line). The red arrow indicates the drift in the EPR-allowed transition after the waiting time.}
    \labfig{rough_tracking}
\end{marginfigure}

As discussed in the previous section, the frequency drift on the EPR-allowed frequencies is on the same order of magnitude as the linewidth of the transition. Without active tracking and compensation of the drift, the fluorescent intensity of the transition will not be constant, resulting in a reduction of SNR. However, the noise has a characteristic timescale of minutes, which is slow when compared to the duration of our pulse sequences. Therefore, it is possible to track the drift in between averages, which allows to compensate it during the next sequence. 

A rudimentary but straightforward tracking protocol can be implemented by performing high-resolution spectroscopy with a small sweeping range around the electron transition frequency\sidenote{To account for nearby nuclear spins, we perform dynamical nuclear polarization before tracking (see \refsec{dnp_half})}. The ensemble averaged number of counts $\langle C \rangle (\delta)$ is then fitted to a Lorentzian, as shown in \reffig{rough_tracking} The center of the fit yields the new electron spin transition frequency and the pulse frequencies are updated for the next measurement. This algorithm is straightforward and reliable, but the main drawback is the time it takes to obtain a reasonable fit, approximately 1 minute. Therefore, it can only be realistically run a limited number of times, resulting in poor tracking.

Due to the limitations of the direct tracking procedure, we envisioned a second algorithm focused on speed rather than fine control. This method exploits the properties of a Ramsey measurement as frequency sensor. Let's begin by considering a Ramsey measurement, where the spin is sent to the equatorial plane of the Bloch sphere by a $\pi/2$-pusle where it freely evolves during a time $\tau$ before another $\pi/2$-pulse is applied and the population of the spin state is measured. In our case, the measurement is performed via the SMPD and results in a number of counts $C$. 

During the free evolution period, the electron spin accumulates a phase proportional to the transition frequency and the evolution time. Any drift $\delta \omega$ in the transition frequency therefore appears as an additional phase shift\sidenote{As measured before, the drift in our system has a timescale of minutes, while the coherence of the electron is on the hundreds of \textmu s. It is therefore reasonable to assume that the transition frequency is constant during the evolution time of the Ramsey sequence.} $\phi = (\omega_S + \delta \omega) \tau$. Since $\omega_S \gg \delta\omega$, directly resolving small changes in $\phi$ is difficult. To address this, the second Ramsey pulse includes an artificial phase that effectively compensates most of the rapid oscillation due to $\omega_S$, leaving only the slower drift-dependent component.

One possible strategy for tracking the drift would be to measure the full Ramsey fringe and fit the oscillation frequency, but this method will again be limited by the data acquisition time. Instead, the tracking can be greatly accelerated by probing only a single time point on the Ramsey curve, chosen for maximal sensitivity. This point corresponds to the zero crossing of the Ramsey oscillation, where the population of the electron spin is linear to $\delta \omega$. For this single-point Ramsey sensor, sensitivity is maximized when $\tau\sim T_2^*$ as it balances long phase accumulation time with low information loss due to decoherence.

\begin{figure*}
    \includegraphics{chapter3/figures/tracking.pdf}
    \caption[Ramsey tracking scheme]{Ramsey tracking scheme. 
    \textbf{a.} \textit{(top)} Simulated Ramsey measurement under phase cycling. The signal was generated with a dephasing time of 50~\textmu s and artificial detuning of 20~kHz and 21~kHz (red and gray respectively). The resulting quantity, $C - \bar C$ is plot as a function of the interpulse delay $\tau$. 
    \textit{(bottom)} A single point Ramsey at $\tau = \tau_0$ as a function of $\delta \omega$. This quantity has a stable setpoint at zero (dashed horizontal line) and is linear with the detuning for small values of $\delta \omega$
    \textbf{b.} Feedback loop diagram. The PI loop is run after every measurement to correct any shift in the frequencies of the system.}
    \labfig{tracking_ramsey}
\end{figure*}


In practice, fluctuating dark counts introduce a varying offset in the measured counts $C$. We remove this offset by phase cycling: the Ramsey sequence is repeated with an added $\pi$ phase shift on the second pulse, producing a second measurement $\bar C$. Subtracting the two, $C - \bar C$, cancels the offset and yields a signal that is linear in $\delta\omega$, provided that $\tau \ll 2\pi / \Delta\omega$ (see \reffig{tracking_ramsey}a).

The quantity $C - \bar C$ can be used as the process variable of a Proportional-Integral (PI) loop with a setpoint at zero, integrated within the FPGA logic. In practice, we define the variable $Y_i$ which carries a short memory by filtering the values of the sensor output for every iteration $i$ of the PI loop,

\begin{equation}
Y_i = \sum_{j=0}^{i} (C_{j} - \bar{C}_{j})\, e^{-(j-i)/f} \approx (1 - 1/f)\, Y_{i-1} + (C_{i} - \bar{C}_{i}),
\end{equation}

where $f = 2000$ is the memory of the system. Finally, the PI controller calculates the frequency correction to be applied on the pulses through a proportional and an integral channel,

\begin{equation}
\delta\omega_{i} = P\, Y_{i} + I \sum_{j=0}^{i} Y_{j}.
\end{equation}

The values for P and I were set by manually optimizing the convergence time after artificially detuning the pulse frequency. \reffig{tracking_ramsey}b shows a block diagram with the control loop.

The second tracking procedure is used to compensate for the drift in all measurements presented in this chapter and the next, unless explicitly stated.

\paragraph{SNR of the measurement}

We consider a nuclear spin prepared in $\ket{\downarrow}$. Since the spin is completely polarized, the measured counts $C_\uparrow$ will arise solely from detector dark-counts and can be modelled as a Poissonian random variable with rate $N_\mathrm{RO} \, \Gamma_\mathrm{DC}\, t_\mathrm{d}$ where $\Gamma_\mathrm{DC}$ is the detector  dark-count rate and $t_\mathrm{d}$ is the duration of each detection window. 

In contrast, the measured counts $C_\downarrow$ contain contributions from both detector dark counts and real fluorescence events. We model the fluorescence contribution as a binomial random variable with sample size $N_\mathrm{RO}$ and success probability $\eta$, where $\eta$ denotes the spin-to-click detection efficiency. The total distribution of $C_\downarrow$ is therefore given by the sum of a Poisson-distributed dark-count process and a binomially distributed fluorescence process. Direct addition of these two distributions does not have a simple form, but it can be significantly simplified in the experimentally relevant regime.

When the Poisson rate and the binomial sample size are sufficiently large, both distributions can be well approximated by Gaussian distributions according to the central limit theorem. In this limit, their sum is also Gaussian, with mean and variance equal to the sum of the respective means and variances of the individual contributions.

The distributions for $C_\uparrow$ and $C_\downarrow$ are then

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    &C_\uparrow \sim \mathcal{N} (\mu_{\uparrow}, \sigma^2_{\uparrow}) \\
    &\mu_{\uparrow} = \sigma^2_{\uparrow} = N_\mathrm{RO} \, \Gamma_\mathrm{DC} \, t_\mathrm{d}
\end{split} 
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    &C_\downarrow \sim \mathcal{N} (\mu_{\downarrow}, \sigma_{\downarrow}^2) \\
    &\mu_{\downarrow} = N_\mathrm{RO} \left(\eta + \Gamma_\mathrm{DC}\, t_\mathrm{d} \right) \\
    &\sigma^2_{\downarrow} = N_\mathrm{RO} \left(\eta \, (1 - \eta) +  \Gamma_\mathrm{DC} \, t_\mathrm{d} \right)
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The quantity used for single-shot readout corresponds to the difference in counts $\delta C = C_\downarrow - C_\uparrow$, which is given by 

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    &\delta C \sim \mathcal{N} (\mu_{\delta}, \sigma_{\delta}^2) \\
    &\mu_{\delta} = \mu_{\downarrow} - \mu_{\uparrow} =   N_\mathrm{RO} \eta \\
    &\sigma^2_{\delta} = \sigma^2_{\downarrow} + \sigma^2_{\uparrow} = N_\mathrm{RO} \left(\eta \, (1 - \eta) + 2 \Gamma_\mathrm{DC} \, t_\mathrm{d} \right)
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The threshold-based nuclear spin state assignment translates to an integral of the probability density function of the $\delta C$ distribution from the threshold and $\infty$. As the model does not include detector bias, we take the threshold at zero. The probability of measuring the prepared state is

\begin{equation}
    p_\mathrm{SNR}(\downarrow | \downarrow, N_\mathrm{RO}) = \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{\sigma_\delta \sqrt{2\pi}} \, e^{-\frac{(x - \mu_\delta)^2}{2\sigma^2_\delta}} dx = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 - \text{Erf}\left[\frac{\text{SNR}}{\sqrt{2}}\right]\right)
\end{equation}

where SNR corresponds to the Signal-to-Noise Ratio,

\begin{equation}
    \text{SNR} = \frac{\mu_\delta}{\sigma_\delta} = \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\eta \cdot (1 - \eta) +  2\Gamma_{DC}\, t_D}} \cdot \sqrt{N_{RO}}.
\end{equation}

The SNR is proportional to $\sqrt{N_\mathrm{RO}}$ and the prefactor contains detector specific parameters which can be measured from the fluorescence curve of the spin as detailed in \refsec{erbium_spin_detection}. For the measurement shown in \reffig{single_shot_vs_NRO} we estimate $\eta = 0.18$, $\Gamma_\mathrm{DC} = 150$ s$^{-1}$, $t_\mathrm{d} = 2.6$ ms.

\paragraph{Population evolution due to cross-relaxation}

The cross-relaxation events introduce a dynamic change in the population of the nuclear spin states. The population evolution can be modeled through a system of discrete rate equations by considering the finite probabilities of cross-relaxation $p_\mathrm{ZQ,DQ}$,

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \frac{\Delta P_\downarrow}{\Delta N_\mathrm{RO}} \: = \: &p_\text{DQ} P_\uparrow - p_\text{ZQ} P_\downarrow, \\
    \frac{\Delta P_\uparrow}{\Delta N_\mathrm{RO}}  \: = \: &p_\text{ZQ} P_\downarrow - p_\text{DQ} P_\uparrow, \\
    P_\uparrow + &P_\downarrow = 1.
\end{split}
\end{equation}

In our specific case, $p_\mathrm{ZQ,DQ} \ll 1$ and the dynamics of the system are much slower than the step size. Therefore we can approximate the discrete evolution as a continuous derivative, $\frac{\Delta P_\downarrow}{\Delta N_\mathrm{RO}} \approx \frac{d P_\downarrow}{d N_\mathrm{RO}}$. We solve the equations by considering the initial condition $P_\downarrow(0) = P_0 < 1$ which yields

\begin{equation}
    P_\downarrow(N_\mathrm{RO}) \: = \: (P_0 - \frac{p_\text{DQ}}{p_\text{DQ} + p_\text{ZQ}})\cdot e^{-(p_\text{DQ} + p_\text{ZQ})\cdot N_\mathrm{RO}} + \frac{p_\text{DQ}}{p_\text{DQ} + p_\text{ZQ}}.
\end{equation}

The populations exponentially converge to a stable state which depends on the relative cross-relaxation probabilities. In the following, we consider that both cross-relaxation rates are equal $p_\text{ZQ} \approx p_\text{DQ} = p_\text{x}$, and complete polarization of the nuclear spin state at the beginning of the sequence $P_0=1$ and the equation can be simplified to

\begin{equation}
    P_\downarrow(N_\mathrm{RO}) \: \approx \: \frac{1}{2}\left( 1 + e^{- 2 p_\text{x} \, N_\mathrm{RO}}\right), 
\end{equation}

In conclusion, cross-relaxation drives the system a balanced mixed state when $N_\mathrm{RO} \gg p_\text{x}$; larger $p_x$ gives faster deterioration of the prepared nuclear population and thus limits the number of usable readout pulses.

\paragraph{Total fidelity of the measurement} The final readout fidelity is set by a combination of the two processes described above, which have different scaling on $N_\mathrm{RO}$. %On one hand the SNR of the measurement improves with repetitions and larger values of $N_\mathrm{RO}$ result in better discrimination between the two states ($\mathrm{SNR} \propto \sqrt{N_{\mathrm{RO}}}$). On the other hand, each pulse carries a small probability to flip the nuclear spin through a cross-relaxation event, which accumulates and drives the nuclear population towards a mixed state with an exponential dependence on $N_{\mathrm{RO}}$.

As shown in \reffig{single_shot_vs_NRO}, the two effects operate in different timescales. The assignment probability rapidly converges after approximately $N_{RO}\approx10^2$, while, the effective nuclear spin lifetime due to cross-relaxation events is $N_{RO}\approx10^3$. Therefore, we consider both processes independent, and we model the total assignment probability by weighting the discrimination success by the population remaining in the target level:

\begin{equation}
    p(\downarrow \; \mid \; \downarrow, N_{\mathrm{RO}}) \approx p_{\mathrm{SNR}}(\downarrow \; \mid \; \downarrow, N_{\mathrm{RO}})\; P_{\downarrow}(N_{\mathrm{RO}}),
\end{equation}

This model contains a single free parameter, namely the cross-relaxation probability $p_x$. An analogous expression can be derived for the complementary sequence in which the system is initialized in $\ket{\uparrow}$. Fitting the model to the experimental data shown in \reffig{single_shot_vs_NRO} yields an average value of $p_x \approx 2.9(3)\times 10^{-4}$. However, the dataset presented in \reffig{single_shot_vs_NRO} does not extend to the full decay of the exponential dependence. As a result, the extracted value of $p_x$ should be taken as an estimate. The quoted uncertainty likely underestimates the true error, since fitting an exponential decay without access to the exponential tail is inherently ill-conditioned and sensitive to noise.

Using Eq.~\ref{eq:cross_relaxation_probability_half}, we convert this value into a perpendicular hyperfine coupling of
$A_\perp/2\pi \approx 71(7),\text{kHz}$.

From the assignment probability, we can define readout fidelity as 

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_\mathrm{RO} =\frac{1}{2} \left[p\left(\downarrow \; \mid \; \downarrow, N_{\mathrm{RO}}\right) + p\left(\uparrow \; \mid \; \uparrow, N_{\mathrm{RO}}\right) \right].
\end{equation}

The upper bound for the fidelity can be obtained by optimizing $N_{\mathrm{RO}}$ to balance SNR gain and cross-relaxation loss. For the parameters of our detector and the fitted $p_x$, this optimal fidelity is given by

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_\mathrm{RO} =\frac{1}{2} \left[p\left(\downarrow \; \mid \; \downarrow, N^{(\max)}_{\mathrm{RO}}\right) + p\left(\uparrow \; \mid \; \uparrow, N^{(\max)}_{\mathrm{RO}}\right) \right] = 0.93.
\end{equation}

Similar to the initial detection of $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ discussed in \refsec{sota_ch3}, the fidelity of the readout is ultimately limited by the nuclear spin cross-relaxation rate. To increase the readout fidelity, two strategies are possible: increasing detection SNR and suppressing the cross-relaxation probability.

Enhancing the SMPD performance will yield an increase of detection SNR. In the ideal limit of unit efficiency and vanishing dark counts ($\eta=1$, $\Gamma_{DC}=0$), a single electron excitation would suffice for nuclear spin single shot readout. In this regime, the only remaining source of infidelity is the probability of a cross-relaxation event occurring during the measurement, $p_x$, which therefore sets the fundamental upper bound on the achievable fidelity.

Alternatively, the cross-relaxation probability itself can be reduced. By decreasing the resonator linewidth $\kappa$, one can suppress the Purcell enhancement of the ZQ/DQ transitions, thereby reducing the cross-relaxation probability. Consequently, the fabrication of higher-quality resonators provides a direct route towards improved readout fidelity.

\section{Sideband driving and nuclear spin identification}
\labsec{sideband_driving_and_identification}

Nuclear spin spectroscopy is achieved by driving the forbidden transitions, in a single-spin version of the Electron-Electron-Double-Resonance (ELDOR) detected NMR (EDNMR) experiment ~\sidecite{schosseler_pulsed_1994}. Following preparation in state $\ket{\downarrow}$ (resp. $\ket{\uparrow}$) a flattop-shaped pulse is applied at a frequency $\omega_S + \delta$ and maximum amplitude $\Omega$. A reduction (resp. increase) in the down-state population $P_\downarrow$ is observed when $\omega_S + \delta$ matches the forbidden DQ and ZQ transitions. Spectroscopy data are shown in \reffig{sb_spectroscopy}a \& b for $3$ values of $\Omega$, showing a frequency shift and power-broadening.

The fitted frequencies for the ZQ and DQ transitions,  $\delta_\text{ZQ, DQ}$, are shown in \reffig{sb_spectroscopy}c as a function of $\Omega$. As discussed in \refsec{ac-zeeman_shifts}, $\delta_\text{ZQ, DQ}$ depend quadratically on the driving amplitude. The shift originates in the off-resonant driving of the allowed transitions, which leads to AC-Zeeman shifts of the energy levels. The measured data is fit to the expression derived in \refsec{ac-zeeman_shifts}, which we remind here,

\begin{equation}
\delta_{ZQ, DQ}(\Omega) = \mp \frac{1}{2} \left(\delta\omega^{(\uparrow)}_\mathrm{AC} + \delta\omega^{(\downarrow)}_\mathrm{AC}\right) \approx \mp \frac{1}{2} \left(\frac{\Omega^{2}}{2\omega_I^{(\Uparrow)}} + \frac{\Omega^{2}}{2\omega_I^{(\Downarrow)}}\right).
\end{equation}

The fit yields the nuclear spin Larmor frequency, $\omega_I/2\pi = - 788.1(4)$ kHz. The corresponding gyromagnetic factor of $1.767(1)$~MHz/T is close to the value expected for $^{183}\mathrm{W}$ in \Ca, $\gamma_W/2\pi = 1.774$~MHz/T \sidecite{knight_solidstate_1986}, thus confirming the nature of the nuclear spin. 

Driving the forbidden transitions provides an alternative method to determine $A_\perp$, as discussed in \refsec{driving_nuclear_spin_half}. Indeed, the ZQ and DQ Rabi frequency $\Omega_\text{ZQ, DQ}$ are related to the allowed-transition Rabi frequency as 

\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{\text{ZQ}} \approx \Omega_{\text{DQ}} \approx \Omega \, \frac{A_\perp}{2\omega_I}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+4\omega_I^2/\kappa^2}}.
\end{equation}

The DQ and ZQ Rabi oscillations are plot in \reffig{sb_rabi} and the oscillation frequencies are fit to the above equation, from which we obtain  $A_\perp/2\pi=103(7)$~kHz. This value is significantly larger than the one obtained using the readout analysis in the previous section. As discussed before, the estimation of $p_x$ is ill-condition by the nature of the dataset. We take the value of $A_\perp$ measured in this section as a more precise estimation of the hyperfine coupling.

The nuclear spin state can be initialized via solid-effect Dynamic Nuclear Polarization (DNP) \cite{a.abragham_nouvelle_1958, abragam_principles_1978, wenckebach_solid_2008}. To initialize the nuclear spin in $\ket{\uparrow}$, we apply a pulse on the zero-quantum transition, wait for a time $\tau_\mathrm{w}$ (taken to be close to $\Gamma_R^{-1}$), and repeat $N_{\mathrm{prep}}$ times. \reffig{dnp}a shows $P_{\downarrow}$ as a function of the number of ZQ pulses. The population in the down-state increases rapidly, reaching $\approx 0.8$ for $2$ pulses (\reffig{dnp}b). This demonstrates single-atom solid-effect nuclear spin polarization. The maximum value is limited by nuclear spin read-out errors and out-of-equilibrium excitations of the electron spin, and can likely be improved in future experiments.

State preparation is also confirmed spectroscopically. After polarizing the state of the nuclear spin to $\ket{\downarrow}$, a pulse is applied at a frequency $\omega_S + \delta$, with the amplitude corresponding to a $\pi$-pulse for the forbidden transition at resonance, followed by nuclear spin state readout. \reffig{dnp}c shows The probability $P_\downarrow$ as a function of $\delta$, which shows a clear dip at the frequency corresponding to the double-quantum resonance, but no dip on the zero-quantum transition, which is further evidence for nuclear spin polarization.

The solid-effect DNP is also effective when multiple spins are present, as seen in \reffig{dnp_trace} where it is applied to $\mathrm{Er}_4$. The polarization protocol into $\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}$, involves sending pulses at each of the zero-quantum transition frequencies. A $20$-minute time trace of the allowed-transitions spectrum is shown in \reffig{dnp_trace} in absence of (bottom), with polarization into $\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}$ (middle), and into $\ket{\uparrow \uparrow}$ (top). The polarization sequence is applied at the beginning of every spectrum. Whereas the trace with no polarization shows nuclear spin quantum jumps as reported in \reffig{dnp_trace}, the two other traces show dominantly the one EPR-allowed transition corresponding to the polarized state. 

\section{Conclusion and outlook}

A logical next step will be to extend these techniques methods to nuclear spins more weakly coupled to the electron spin, using methods demonstrated with NV centers in diamond~\sidecite{taminiau_detection_2012,cujia_tracking_2019,du_singlemolecule_2024}. This would open the way to $^{183}\mathrm{W}$-nuclear spin-based quantum registers operated and read-out via the $\mathrm{Er}^{3+}$ spin, generalizing recent $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ quantum registers demonstrations operated by a NV center in diamond~\sidecite{bradley_tenqubit_2019,abobeih_faulttolerant_2022}. Perhaps the most interesting aspect of the methods reported here is their applicability to a large range of nuclear spin systems in a large variety of samples, crystalline or not. Indeed, individual paramagnetic centers can be detected by microwave fluorescence provided their Purcell relaxation rate $\Gamma_R$ can be increased above $\sim 10^3 \mathrm{s}^{-1}$, and above their non-radiative relaxation rate at $10$\,mK. These conditions can likely be met in a variety of paramagnetic systems, ranging from organic radicals to transition-metal ions in metallo-enzymes, which could be deposited on top of the resonator constriction in the form of small molecular crystals or even frozen solutions. Applying the techniques demonstrated here may then yield their hyperfine couplings to individual ligand nuclear spins with a higher spectral resolution than reached with ensemble measurements.
