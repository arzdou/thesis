\begin{quote}

\section{State-of-the-art}
\labsec{sota_ch5}

We begin this chapter by highlighting two state-of-the-art experiments in which the spectra of individual quadrupolar nuclei were measured. As in the previous chapters, the systems of interest are NV centers in diamond and donor impurities in silicon.

\subsection*{Quadrupolar spectroscopy of a $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ nuclear spin in diamond}

The NV center has $C_{3v}$ point-symmetry. This implies that, in principle, the $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ quadrupole is axial around the NV symmetry axis. As discussed in \refsec{nuclear_spins_ca}, the quadrupolar Hamiltonian in this case takes the following form 

\begin{equation}
    \cH{Q} = Q_{zz} \left[\hat{I}_z^2 - I(I+1)/3\right]
\end{equation} 

Only one parameter is needed to describe the interaction. Recent work by S. Breitweiser \textit{et al.} tests this hypothesis, and shows by precision spectroscopy that $C_{3v}$ symmetry is in fact broken for individual NVs~\sidecite{breitweiser_quadrupolar_2024}. This is manifested by the appearance of a small but non-zero biaxiality parameter $\eta$.

As shown in \reffig{sota_nv1}a, the sample is located inside a solid immersion lens, which enhances optical photon collection efficiency. The energy level structure of the coupled electron-nuclear system is shown in \reffig{sota_nv1}b. The spectrum is progressively split by the different interaction terms. Since the magnetic field is applied along the quadrupole axis, the quadrupole interaction is diagonal and splits the levels proportional to $m_I^2$.

They measure, with high precision, the hyperfine spectrum of the quadrupolar nucleus using a DD pulse sequence. This sequence follows the same principle of electron decoupling as the DDrf sequences discussed in \refsec{sota_ch4}. However, instead of using rf pulses, they leverage the hyperfine coupling to create a constructive rotation of the nuclear spin state~\sidecite{taminiau_detection_2012}. \reffig{sota_nv1}c shows both the pulse sequence and the measured spectra as a function of the interpulse delay. The transitions highlighted in purple and red correspond to $\Delta m_I = \pm 1$. These transitions are enabled by $S_z I_x$ terms in the Hamiltonian, engineered by introducing a small off-axis component of the magnetic field.

In contrast, the green transitions correspond to $\Delta m_I = \pm 2$, which are enabled by quadrupolar terms perpendicular to the magnetic field axis, namely $I_x^2$ and $I_y^2$. The observation of these transitions indicates a non-axial component in the Hamiltonian. Using the DD spectroscopy, the authors can extract the small biaxiality parameter with four-digit precision.

Notably, the $\Delta m_I = \pm 2$ transitions are no longer observed, suggesting that strain induced by the fabricated microstructure is responsible for the anomalous biaxiality parameter.

\reffig{sota_nv1}d shows two $\Delta m_I = \pm 1$ transitions for different NV centers, which do not overlap. This reflects significant variations in the $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ quadrupolar Hamiltonian, attributed to different local strain environments. By measuring the hyperfine spectra of multiple NV centers and fitting the results to a Hamiltonian model, the authors extract the relevant parameters of the system. \reffig{sota_nv2} plots the quadrupolar interaction strength along the $z$-axis versus the zero-field splitting parameters, revealing clear correlations. They are attributed to local strain modifying the crystal field experienced by each impurity. These results demonstrate how single-spin quadrupole measurements can be used to probe local strain and microscopic structural variations.

\subsection*{Quadrupole spectroscopy of a $^{123}\mathrm{Sb}$ high-nuclear spin in silicon}

Building upon the \textit{state-of-the-art} in Chapters \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w} and \ref{ch:individual_nuclear_spins_quantum_information}, we now highlight the work by S. Asaad, V. Mourik \textit{et al.}, in which they measure the spectrum of a single $^{123}\mathrm{Sb}$ nuclear spin, using the electron spin of the neutral donor as a probe~\sidecite{asaad_coherent_2020}.

Like phosphorus, antimony is a group-V donor. When implanted in silicon, it forms a defect state that binds a single electron in its neutral configuration at low temperatures. Consequently, the methods described in \refsec{sota_ch4} to read out the donor spin by spin-to-charge conversion apply here as well. A notable difference is that $^{123}\mathrm{Sb}$ has nuclear spin $I=7/2$, rendering its spectrum sensitive to quadrupolar interactions.

The donor is measured in its ionized state, where the electron is removed from the system and reintroduced only for nuclear spin readout. The energy level structure of the $^{123}\mathrm{Sb}$ nuclear spin is shown in \reffig{sota_sb2}a. The quadrupole interaction is observed to be much weaker than the Zeeman interaction. Under these conditions, the magnetic quantum numbers $m_I$ remain good quantum numbers, and the Zeeman levels acquire shifts proportional to $Q_{zz} m_I^2$.


\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics{chapter5/figures/sota_sb2.pdf}
    \caption[State-of-the-art: Nuclear spectrum of a $^{123}\mathrm{Sb}$ spin]{
        \textbf{a.} Energy level structure of the spin-7/2 nucleus of an ionized $^{123}\mathrm{Sb}$ donor. The Zeeman effect (green) linearly splits the energy levels and the quadrupole interaction causes a further energy split (blue). The transition between $7/2 \leftrightarrow 3/2$ has been highlighted with an arrow.
        \textbf{b.} \& \textbf{c.} Nuclear electric resonance spectrum of the $\Delta m_I = \pm1$ \& $\Delta m_I = \pm2$ transitions as a function of the AC voltage of the driving gate.
    }
    \labfig{sota_sb2}
\end{figure}

Electric fields can modulate the quadrupole interaction of nuclear spins at non-centrosymmetric sites. This mechanism was first proposed by Bloembergen in 1961, who noted that strong electric fields can induce slight lattice distortions, thereby modifying the electric field gradient at the nuclear site~\sidecite{bloembergen_linear_1961}. The resulting linear electric Stark shift has since been observed in bulk NMR experiments across various systems~\sidecite{dixon_electrically_1964, ono_coherent_2013, macfarlane_optical_2014}. This effect will also play an important role in the analysis of the results in this chapter.

By applying an AC electric field, the authors modulate the off-diagonal quadrupole tensor elements and drive the $m_I=\pm1$ and $\pm2$ transitions. Figures \reffig{sota_sb2}b and c show the corresponding nuclear electric resonance spectra. Notably, the $-1/2 \leftrightarrow 1/2$ transition is absent, as it is first-order insensitive to quadrupolar effects. From the spectral separations, they estimate $Q_{zz}/2\pi \approx 66$~kHz.

\begin{marginfigure}
    \includegraphics{chapter5/figures/sota_sb1.pdf}
    \caption[State-of-the-art: Strain map on silicon]{Shear strain in the silicon substrate, calculated on a vertical cross-section under the orange dashed line}
    \labfig{sota_sb1}
\end{marginfigure}

An important aspect emphasized by the authors is that silicon sites are, in fact, centrosymmetric, which should suppress both quadrupole interactions and linear electric Stark shifts. However, aluminum gates induce significant lattice strain at low temperatures due to the mismatch in thermal contraction between the aluminum and the silicon~\sidecite{dehollain_nanoscale_2012, pla_straininduced_2018}. For impurities located near the surface, this strain can break the site symmetry, thereby generating a finite quadrupole interaction. They calculate the strain map around the donor position (see \reffig{sota_sb1}), which shows significant values of shear strain. From the strain simulations and the Hamiltonian parameters, they are able to locate the approximate position of the impurity by correlating the strain-induced EFG with the measured data.

\subsection*{Precision spectroscopy}

A driving motivation of high-resolution spectroscopy is to measure interaction terms beyond the magnetic dipole and electric quadrupole coupling. These are the octupole and hexadecapole interactions, which, at first order, take the following form~\sidecite{schwartz_theory_1955}

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \cH{octupole} &= \frac{C_3}{I(2I-1)(I-1)}\,I_z^3 , \\
    \cH{hexadecapole} &= \frac{C_4}{I(2I-1)(I-1)(2 I -3)}\,  I_z^4,
\end{split}
\end{align}

where $C_3$ and $C_4$ are the octupole and hexadecapole coupling strengths. These interactions correspond to the third and fourth order terms of the multipole expansion (see \refsec{nuclear_spins_ca}) and appear due to the non-sphericity of the nuclear spin wavefunctions. The octupole is a magnetic interaction that couples spins $I > 1$ to the magnetic field second derivatives. The hexadecapole is an electric interaction that couples spins $I > 3/2$ with the electric field third derivative.

These higher-order interactions are $>10^5$ times weaker than the leading-order terms, and precision measurements of the NMR spectrum are required to resolve their contribution. The octupole moment has been measured using molecular beam spectroscopy and single-ion experiments~\sidecite{gerginov_observation_2003,lewty_spectroscopy_2012}. A statistically-significant non-zero hexadecapole moment was claimed using molecular beam measurements~\sidecite{cederberg_evidence_1999}, but it was three orders of magnitude larger than predicted by theory~\sidecite{thyssen_quadrupole_2001}. This implies that the interaction likely originates from higher-order magnetic interactions (pseudo-hexadecapole).

These measurements are able to achieve exceptional precision by isolating the target from the environment. In contrast, ensemble magnetic resonance measurements of coupled electron-nuclear spin systems in the solid state generally offer a lower spectral resolution (kilohertz at best~\sidecite{sielaff_pulsed_2025}). The broadening originates from spectral drift and inhomogeneous broadening. In addition, high-nuclear spins are subject to further broadening, as local electric field differences will induce shifts in the quadrupolar interaction. This has so far precluded the observation of higher-order terms in the coupled spin Hamiltonian by magnetic resonance techniques, despite active research~\sidecite{segel_nuclear_1978,doering_search_1986}.

The tighter bound on the hexadecapole interaction is reported in ref.~\sidecite{liao_nuclear_1994}, where they measured a hexadecapolar coupling constant of $C_4=60\pm140$~Hz.


%In addition, precision measurements of the hyperfine spectrum of atoms and simple molecules probe electron-nuclear interactions beyond the leading-order magnetic dipolar and electric quadrupolar terms~\sidecite{schwartz_theory_1955}. Higher-order interactions such as the magnetic octuple are $\sim 10^5$ times weaker; hence, high spectral resolution in the order of hertz is required to resolve their contribution. This is readily achieved in atomic and molecular beams or in single-ion experiments, using combined microwave and laser excitations~\sidecite{gerginov_observation_2003,lewty_spectroscopy_2012}. In the solid state, ensemble magnetic resonance measurements of coupled electron-nuclear spin systems generally offer a lower spectral resolution (kilohertz at best~\sidecite{sielaff_pulsed_2025}), particularly when high-spin nuclei are involved. The origin of the broadening lies in a combination of spin-spin interactions and inhomogeneous distribution of electron gyromagnetic tensor and/or quadrupolar couplings. This precluded so far the observation of higher-order terms in the coupled spin Hamiltonian by magnetic resonance techniques, despite active research~\sidecite{segel_nuclear_1978,doering_search_1986,liao_nuclear_1994}.

%Because the magnetic moment of $I > 1$ nuclei is not point-like but has a spatial extension, they have a magnetic octupole moment that couples to magnetic field second derivatives. Such interaction has been measured in the hyperfine spectrum of neutral atoms~\sidecite{gerginov_observation_2003} and ions~\sidecite{lewty_spectroscopy_2012} using atomic beams but never in the solid-state, and could be used to extract information about the nuclear structure. \subsection*{Discussion}

The works on NV centers and donors in silicon illustrate how measurements of quadrupolar interactions at the single-spin level provide access to local structural information and strain-induced lattice distortions. In both cases, high-resolution spectroscopy is used to identify deviations from the expected Hamiltonians; these deviations are then used to understand the interactions in the system. The next step would be to reconstruct the full quadrupole tensor of an individual nuclear spin based on the measured spectroscopic information.

Single nuclear spins in the solid state also provide a platform to investigate higher-order interactions. Compared to ensemble measurements that are limited by inhomogeneous and quadrupolar broadening, individual nuclear spins demonstrate long coherence times, as discussed in the \textit{state-of-the-art} section of Chapter \ref{ch:individual_nuclear_spins_quantum_information}. This resolution can be used to test assumptions about the Hamiltonian.

One such example is the dependence of the nuclear quadrupole on the state of neighbouring spins. The nuclear quadrupolar interaction stems from the EFG at the nuclear spin position and should, in principle, not depend on the electron spin state. Such dependence is indeed not described in the standard spin Hamiltonian (see \refch{electron_nuclear_interaction}). This is tested and confirmed for the case of a $^{14}$N nuclear spin coupled to the NV electron spin in ref.~\sidecite{bartling_universal_2025}, where they measure the Hamiltonian parameters with hertz-level precision in both states of the NV center. 

Given the long coherence times of \W nuclear spins demonstrated in the previous chapter, \Ca is a promising substrate for precision spectroscopy of individual nuclear spins. In this chapter, we identify an impurity in the crystal and use precision spectroscopy to determine its nature and interactions with the \Er spin.

In the NV platform, such measurements are particularly challenging, as high-fidelity readout and control typically require precise alignment of the magnetic field along the NV axis.

To characterize the \Er spin, we first set $B_0$ to match the resonance condition. Unless otherwise specified, the measurements of this chapter were taken at $B_0=446.27$~mT and $\theta = -0.37^\circ$,  marked in \reffig{spectra_2d}a as a red star. The results of the basic electron spin characterization are detailed in \reftab{er_characterization_nb}.

As discussed in \refsec{individual_er_ion_characterization}, the top of the curve for this spin does not match with $\theta=0$. This implies that the direction of minimal effective gyromagnetic tensor is not aligned with the bulk's $c$-axis. By fitting the curve to a parabola, we measure a difference of $0.20^\circ$. In practice, we define the angle with respect to this spin's $c$-axis as $\theta_S \equiv \theta - 0.20^\circ$, which we use during the magnetic dipole coupling calculations described in \refsec{assignment}.

We then measure a spectroscopic time trace, similar to the ones presented in \refsec{nuclear_spin_quantum_jumps}. We repeatedly measure the \Er spectrum by sweeping the excitation pulse frequency and monitoring the fluorescence after the pulse. Note that the span of the scan is much larger than the operating bandwidth of the SMPD of $\sim300$~kHz. To measure this trace, we actively tune the frequency of the detector to stay in resonance with the excitation frequency $\omega$.

As shown in Chapter \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w}, strongly coupled nuclear spins are revealed by sudden jumps of the \Er EPR-allowed transition frequency. In \reffig{spectra_2d}b, ten evenly-spaced resonances are observed. They are separated by $\sim$130~kHz and have vastly different cross-relaxation lifetimes, indicating the coupling to a spin-9/2 nuclear spin impurity.  

As discussed in \refsec{nb_er_hamiltonian}, the secular Hamiltonian of this system is

\begin{equation}
    \cH{}/\hbar = \omega_S \hat{S}_z + \omega_I \hat{I}_z + A_\parallel \hat{S}_z \hat{I}_z + A_\perp \hat{S}_z \hat{I}_x + \hat{\mathbf{I}} \cdot \bar{\bar{Q}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{I}}.
    \label{eq:hamiltonian_ninehalf_full}
\end{equation}

where the terms correspond, in order, to the \Er spin Zeeman energy, the high-nuclear spin Zeeman energy, the parallel and perpendicular hyperfine interactions, and the quadrupolar term (see \refsec{interaction_between_to_spins} and \refsec{nuclear_spins_ca} respectively). 

The Hamiltonian can be separated in two manifolds, depending on the state of the electron spin. Therefore, it can be rewritten as

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \cH{} = &\ket{\Downarrow}\bra{\Downarrow}\otimes \cH{}^{(\Downarrow)} + \ket{\Uparrow}\bra{\Uparrow}\otimes \cH{}^{(\Uparrow)} \\
    \cH{}^{(\Downarrow)}/\hbar &= -\omega_S / 2 + \omega_I^{(\Downarrow)} \hat{I}_z^{(\Downarrow)} + \hat{\mathbf{I}}\cdot \bar{\bar{Q}}^{(\Downarrow)}\cdot \hat{\mathbf{I}} \\ 
    \cH{}^{(\Uparrow)}/\hbar &= +\omega_S / 2 + \omega_I^{(\Uparrow)}\hat{I}_z^{(\Uparrow)} + \hat{\mathbf{I}}\cdot \bar{\bar{Q}}^{(\Uparrow)} \cdot\hat{\mathbf{I}}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:up_down_hamiltonian}
\end{align}


\noindent  $\cH{}^{(m_S)}$ is the nuclear spin Hamiltonian when the \Er spin is in $m_S = \Downarrow$ or $m_S = \Uparrow$. As a result of the hyperfine interaction, the nuclear spin quantization axis $z^{(m_S)}$ and Larmor frequency $\omega_I^{(m_S)} \simeq \omega_I + m_S A_\parallel/2 $ depend on the \Er spin state. In the usual coupled spin description, the quadrupolar tensor should not depend on $m_S$, as confirmed with NV centers in diamond (see \refsec{sota_ch5}). In this chapter we focus on testing this property by separately measuring the tensors $\bar{\bar{Q}}^{(\Downarrow)}$ and $\bar{\bar{Q}}^{(\Uparrow)}$ for each \Er spin orientation.

We now recall the energy level system defined by this Hamiltonian, which was already described in Section \refsec{nb_er_hamiltonian}. \reffig{energy_levels_ninehalf_ch5} shows the $20$ energy levels of the coupled spins, grouped in the two $m_S = \Downarrow$ or $m_S = \Uparrow$ manifolds. The nuclear spin eigenstates are mixed by the quadrupolar interaction and are therefore labeled as $\ket{n}$ in increasing order of energy, with $n$ varying between $0$ and $9$. Ten transitions between $\ket{\Downarrow, n}$ and $\ket{\Uparrow, n}$ at $\omega_n$ are EPR-allowed. Transitions between $\ket{m_S, n}$ and $\ket{m_S, n+1}$ at frequencies $\omega_{n,n+1}^{m_S} $ are NMR-allowed. As described in \refsec{zero_double_quantum_ninehalf}, double- or zero-quantum transitions between $\ket{\Downarrow, n}$ and $\ket{\Uparrow, n \pm 1}$ are weakly allowed because of the slight hyperfine-induced mixing between the nuclear spin states.

Figures \ref{fig:matrix_elements}b \& c show the calculated matrix elements and corresponding cross-relaxation probabilities for the parameters of this system using the analytical formulae derived in \refsec{high_spin_generalization}. Although these parameters will be determined quantitatively in \refsec{final_hamiltonian_fit}, we use their anticipated values here to provide physical intuition for the observed behavior in \reffig{spectra_2d}b.

The calculations reveal that both the matrix elements and the associated cross-relaxation probabilities increase with the energy level index $n$. In particular, the cross-relaxation probability of the state $\ket{\Uparrow,9}$ is several orders of magnitude larger than that of $\ket{\Uparrow,0}$.

The effective lifetime of the $\ket{0}$ state is on the order of tens of minutes, indicating strongly suppressed cross-relaxation. In contrast, the $\ket{9}$ state exhibits a substantially shorter effective lifetime and is barely visible in the spectrum, consistent with its much larger calculated cross-relaxation probability.

\section{Readout, preparation and control}

We proceed to extend the nuclear spin single-shot readout, DNP and coherent control techniques developed in the previous chapters for the characterization and control of \W nuclear spins.

During the experiments performed in Chapters \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w} and \ref{ch:individual_nuclear_spins_quantum_information} with \W, the frequency separation between the EPR-allowed transitions was in the order of $\sim50$~kHz, well within the SMPD and the resonator bandwidth. This allowed us to monitor the population of each of the nuclear spin states independently by exciting the appropriate EPR-allowed transition and measuring the subsequent fluorescence (see \refsec{single_shot_ro_singlespin}). However, the ten distinct electron spin frequencies of this system are each separated by $\sim130$~kHz, spanning more than 1~MHz, considerably larger than the SMPD and the resonator bandwidth. Additionally, the cross-relaxation probability of the different nuclear states is vastly different, as discussed in the previous section. In fact, the cross-relaxation for large $n$ states is too large to achieve high enough SNR for high-fidelity readout using the previous scheme. Consequently, we perform readout on state $\ket{0}$, as it has the lowest cross-relaxation probability (see \reffig{matrix_elements}c). Later, when we achieve nuclear spin coherent control, the population of any arbitrary state can be coherently transferred to $\ket{0}$ and subsequently read out. We also maximize the Purcell effect in the relevant transition by tuning the magnetic field such that $\omega_r = \omega_0$.

Readout on state $\ket{0}$ is achieved by applying $\sim 200$ successive $\pi$-pulses at $\omega_0$ and counting the number of clicks, $C$. \reffig{readout_singleshot} plots the count histograms after preparing in $\ket{0}$ or $\ket{1}$, which show two well-resolved histograms. By defining a threshold at $C=75$, we can discriminate whether the nuclear spin is in state $\ket{0}$ or not.

We note that the value of $A_\parallel$ of this spin is considerably larger than those in Chapters \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w} and \ref{ch:individual_nuclear_spins_quantum_information}. As a result, we use shorter readout pulses, $40$~ms compared to $80$~ms used before. Consequently, the readout outcome is not as sensitive to spectral diffusion and frequency tracking is not needed in this experiment.

In the previous chapters we demonstrated how to perform solid-effect DNP to deterministically prepare the state of strongly coupled \W nuclear spins. The generalization to a high-nuclear spin $I=9/2$ is straightforward, as it only requires pumping all the $9$ DQ transitions to prepare $\ket{0}$. A crude approach would be to send a chirped pulse that covers a large frequency band of $10$~MHz under the EPR-allowed transitions, this pulse would eventually excite one of the DQ transitions, bringing the nuclear spin state closer to $\ket{0}$. However, this technique is not time-efficient. As it chirps, the pulse is mostly not resonant with any transition, and direct driving of the ZQ transitions is preferable. Therefore, we begin by characterizing all ZQ and DQ transitions through EDNMR spectroscopy.

We begin by preparing the nuclear spin into $\ket{0}$\sidenote{Naturally, the first characterization was performed using the "crude" method described in the previous paragraph. Subsequent measurements (as shown here) use the known frequencies of the ZQ and DQ transitions to gather well-averaged data more efficiently.}. We scan the frequency of the driving microwave pulse and monitor the population of state $\ket{0}$. As shown in \reffig{zq_single_transition}, we measure a depletion of the population in $\ket{0}$ centered around $\omega_0 + 7.418~$MHz, which indicates successful excitation of the ZQ transition.

We measure all nine ZQ transitions by extending the protocol to the rest of the nuclear energy levels. As before, the first step is the preparation of $\ket{0}$, then the population is transferred to state $\ket{n}$ by concatenating $\pi$-pulses between pairs of adjacent nuclear spin states (see next section). After the excitation pulse, the population is transferred back to $\ket{0}$ by a reverse train of $\pi$-pulses and readout. The resulting spectra for the ZQ transitions are displayed in \reffig{zq_transitions}a. 

We can drive Rabi oscillations of the ZQ transitions by modifying the duration of the pulse, as shown in \reffig{zq_transitions}b. We note two things. First, the contrast of the signal is limited by the coherence of the electron spin, as in the previous chapters. Second, the Rabi frequency for the same pulse amplitude increases with $n$. This is due to the combined effect of reduced resonator filtering as the resonant ZQ transition frequency gets closer to the center of the resonator, and increased matrix element, as shown in \reffig{matrix_elements}. 

As discussed in \refsec{zero_double_quantum_ninehalf}, the ZQ Rabi frequency is proportional to the perpendicular hyperfine coupling term $A_\perp$. We use Eq. \ref{eq:zero_double_quantum_ninehalf_rabi} to estimate the value of the perpendicular hyperfine coupling independently for each transition, which is shown as a function of $n$ in \reffig{A_perp_sb}. The values average to $A_\perp/2\pi=46(9)$~kHz and are compatible with each other. We note that the uncertainty of the measurement is mainly dominated by the uncertainty on the resonator bandwidth $\kappa$. Fitting the quality factor of a resonator to a high degree of accuracy is not straightforward as it depends on the probing power due to saturable losses caused by two-level-systems.

We now define the pulse sequence used to polarize to state $\ket{0}$ in all measurements reported below. It is shown in \reffig{polarization_seq}. We sequentially drive all double-quantum transitions, starting from $n=8$ down to $n=0$, using 1~ms microwave pulses. Each pulse is followed by a 1~\textmu s delay to allow the electron spin to relax to its ground state. Because of their small matrix elements and large detuning relative to the allowed transitions, the zero- and double-quantum transitions experience large AC-Zeeman shifts, causing their resonance frequencies to be sensitive to the applied microwave amplitude. To compensate for microwave-power fluctuations and spectral drift, all pulses are chirped by 100~kHz around each transition frequency. Due to the low Rabi frequencies of most sideband transitions, a single pulse is insufficient to achieve full population transfer. We compensate by applying 15 repetitions of the pumping sequence to ensure reliable preparation of the \Nb nuclear spin. An additional 15 pulses are applied to the $n=0$ transition, which has the smallest matrix element (see \reffig{matrix_elements}b) and was experimentally found to be particularly difficult to polarize. Polarization into $\ket{0}$ is achieved with a probability close to $1$ at the beginning of each sequence. In addition, the $^{183}\mathrm{W}$ environment is also polarized at the beginning of every sequence to reduce the magnetic noise from the neighboring \W nuclear spins (as discussed in \refsec{precision_nmr_spec}).

\subsubsection{Stimulated Raman driving}


We coherently drive the \Nb NMR transitions using microwave stimulated Raman pulses, as discussed in \refsec{stimulated_raman_driving_ninehalf}. The first transition we characterize is $\ket{\Downarrow,0} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Downarrow,1}$. Spectroscopy of the transition is shown in \reffig{raman_spectroscopy_nb}a. The choice to begin with this transition is natural, as both the preparation and readout have been defined with respect to $\ket{0}$. 

Once the transition frequency and the duration of a $\pi$-pulse have been measured, we can reliably transfer the population between this pair of states. As such, we can define a preparation and readout protocol in $\ket{1}$. This is achieved by including a $\pi$-pulse between $\ket{\Downarrow,0} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Downarrow,1}$ after the original DNP protocol and before the original readout pulse sequence. We can then use the preparation and readout in $\ket{1}$ to measure the next NMR transition between $\ket{\Downarrow,1} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Downarrow,2}$, and so on.

This train of $\pi$-pulses used to transfer the population from state $\ket{\Downarrow, 0}$ to $\ket{\Downarrow, n}$ is also known as a Givens rotation~\sidecite{cybenko_reducing_2001} and has been successfully used previously to control the state of an individual $^{123}\mathrm{Sb}$ nuclear spin in silicon~\sidecite{yu_creation_2024}.


%Spectroscopy of the $\ket{\Downarrow,0} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Downarrow,1}$ transition is shown in \reffig{raman_spectroscopy_nb}. To measure the $\ket{\Downarrow,n} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Downarrow,n+1}$ transitions with $n>0$, we first prepare the system in $\ket{\Downarrow,n}$ by successive application of stimulated Raman $\pi$ pulses from state $\ket{\Downarrow,0}$, also known as a Givens rotation \sidecite{cybenko_reducing_2001}. We then sweep the Raman detuning $\delta$ and measure the probability to find the system in $\ket{\Downarrow,n}$, by successive applications an inverse Givens rotation, followed by the measurement of $P_{\ket{0}}$.

\subsubsection{Ramsey measurements}

We first measure the ground-state-manifold frequencies $\omega^{(\Downarrow)}_{n,n+1}$. We prepare the system in $\ket{\Downarrow,n}$, apply two Raman $\pi/2$-pulses on the $\ket{\Downarrow,n} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Downarrow,n+1}$ transition separated by a time $\tau$, and measure the resulting probability to find the \Nb in $\ket{n}$. The phase of the second $\pi$-pulse has an artificial detuning to compensate for the rapid natural oscillations in the order of MHz. The data for all transitions are shown in \reffig{ramsey_ninehalf}a, together with an exponential cosine fit. 

To increase the accuracy of the measurement, we choose to interleave the measurement of all transitions, to avoid relative shifts induced by magnetic field drift over time. As discussed in \refsec{effect_environment}, this comes at the cost of precision in the measured transition frequencies, since longer measurement time increases the decoherence rate of the Ramsey.

The excited-state manifold frequencies $\omega^{(\Uparrow)}_{n,n+1}$ are then measured by including two \Er $\pi$-pulses separated by $\tau$, applied between two Raman $\pi/2$ pulses kept at a constant time delay of $1$\,ms. The data are shown in \reffig{ramsey_ninehalf}b. By keeping the time separation between the two \Nb pulses constant, any phase the state accumulates is proportional to the difference in frequency between the NMR transitions in the ground and excited state of the \Er spin. Consequently, the oscillation frequency of the measured data directly yields $\omega^{(\Downarrow)}_{n,n+1}-\omega^{(\Uparrow)}_{n,n+1}$. 

\reffig{ramsey_ninehalf}c plots the fitted NMR frequencies as a function of the transition index $n$, both for the ground and excited state of the \Er spin (green and orange resp.). The ground state transitions are consistently larger than their excited state counterparts, which indicates a positive value of $A_\parallel/2\pi\approx130$~kHz.

\subsubsection{The effect of the \W bath}

\begin{figure}{b}
    \includegraphics{chapter5/figures/polarized_vs_unpolarized.pdf}
    \caption[Effect of \W polarization]{Ramsey sequence with and without \W polarization pulses. Measurement data (dots) are fitted to an exponentially decaying curve (line), which yields $T_2^*=22$~ms and $T_2^*=331$~ms for the unpolarized and the polarized case, respectively.}

The nuclear spin bath, primarily consisting of neighboring \W nuclear spins, significantly contributes to the spectral drift of the \Nb transition frequencies. To mitigate this effect, we polarize the spin bath using solid-effect DNP by applying microwave pulses resonant with the double-quantum transitions of the coupled \W spins (see \reffig{polarization_seq}). To be able to effectively drive weakly coupled \W nuclear spins, we employ 40~ms pulses separated by 10~ms, and repeat this sequence 15 times. The strong pulse amplitudes induce a substantial AC-Zeeman shift, and we account for this by chirping the pulse 350~kHz around $\omega_{\text{\W}}^{\text(DQ)}/2\pi = \omega_r - 725$~kHz. Note that it is crucial that the \W polarization sequence is applied \emph{after} the \Nb preparation, since the electron-spin allowed transition depends on the \Nb state, and the \W double-quantum transition is correspondingly detuned relative to this transition.

To demonstrate the impact of the \W polarization, \reffig{polarized_vs_unpolarized} shows a \Nb Ramsey measurement with and without \W polarization. The \Nb coherence time $T_2^*$ without \W polarization is $26$\,ms, and it increases up to 331~ms upon polarizing the \W bath. Since the measurement time of the curve in \reffig{polarized_vs_unpolarized} is much shorter than those in \reffig{ramsey_ninehalf}a, ($\sim$3 hours compared to $\sim$32 hours), the $T_2^*$ is considerably larger for this measurement.

\subsubsection{Hahn echo measurements}

In addition, the large nuclear spin of \Nb offers interesting perspectives for quantum sensing. Indeed, the magnetic moment of states $0$ and $n$ differ by $\sim n \, \mu_\text{\Nb}$, implying that the state $\ket{0} + \ket{n}$ is $\sim n$ times more sensitive to a small magnetic signal than $\ket{0} + \ket{1}$. Such "Schrödinger spin-cat" states have been studied in a Sb donor in silicon~\sidecite{yu_creation_2024}, and used in magnetometry with Rydberg atoms sensing \sidecite{dietsche_highsensitivity_2019}. We perform a preliminary characterization of the magnetic sensitivity of these states by measuring the echo coherence of the state $\ket{\Downarrow \, 0} + \ket{\Downarrow \, n+1}$. \reffig{echo_nb}a \& b show a schematic of the pulse sequence along with the signal, fitted to an exponentially decaying cosine which yields the coherence time $T_{2}$. The decoherence rate $1/T_2$ is seen to increase linearly with $n$ (see \reffig{echo_nb}c), indicating that decoherence occurs mainly because of magnetic noise. Due to the accumulation of coherent errors on the pulses, we only measure these states until $n=6$. These measurements were taken in a different magnetic field configuration with an in-plane angle $\theta_S=-0.3^\circ$.

\subsection{Fitting $\mathcal{H}^{(\Downarrow)}$ and $\mathcal{H}^{(\Uparrow)}$}

We now analyze the frequencies shown in \reffig{ramsey_ninehalf}c to extract the nuclear spin parameters. As discussed in \refsec{spin_system_ch5}, the objectives of this fit are to reconstruct the quadrupolar tensor and to test the assumption that the quadrupole interaction does not depend on the \Er spin state. In order to do so, we separate the Hamiltonian in two manifolds, as shown in Eq. \ref{eq:up_down_hamiltonian}. In this picture, we have two independent nuclear spin systems which experience slightly different magnetic fields, both magnitude and orientation. The origin of this difference lies in the parallel and perpendicular components of the hyperfine interaction which take opposite signs depending on the state of the \Er spin. 

Each of the manifolds can be independently fit to obtain an effective magnetic field and the \Nb quadrupole tensor. However, they correspond to a "single-axis measurement" as the magnetic field is only applied along one direction. Before proceeding to the fit, we note an intrinsic degeneracy of the quadrupole spectrum under these conditions.

\subsubsection{Rotational invariance of the quadrupolar energy spectrum}
\labsec{rot_invariance}

The quadrupolar interaction is mathematically modeled with a rank-2 tensor that is real, traceless and symmetric. Therefore, 5 variables are needed to fully describe it. As discussed in \refsec{nuclear_spins_ca}, a possible set of variables are the quadrupolar interaction strength, $C_q$, the biaxiality parameter, $\eta$, and the three Euler angles that define the rotation from the laboratory frame to the quadrupole principal axes frame. Likewise, the quadrupole can be described in Cartesian coordinates for an arbitrary basis:

\begin{equation}
Q=\begin{pmatrix}
Q_{xx} & Q_{xy} & Q_{xz}\\
Q_{xy} & Q_{yy} & Q_{yz}\\
Q_{xz} & Q_{yz} & Q_{zz}
\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathrm{Tr}\,Q=0,
\end{equation}

However, when fitting the nuclear transition frequencies in this work, we consider the measurements with a single orientation of the magnetic field. Under these conditions, the measured NMR frequencies are not sufficient to describe the full quadrupole tensor. It is intuitively clear that the measured spectrum should be invariant under a rotation around the applied field direction $z$, and that only $4$ out of the $5$ parameters that describe a quadrupole tensor can be determined at best. This is mathematically evidenced when expressing the quadrupole tensor as a function of rank-2 spherical tensors $T^{(2)}_{m}$. These operators are related to the spin operator basis by

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
T^{(2)}_{0} &= 3 I_z^2 - I(I+1), \\
T^{(2)}_{\pm 1} &= \mp \left( I_z I_{\pm} + I_{\pm} I_z \right), \\
T^{(2)}_{\pm 2} &= I_{\pm}^2 .
\end{split}
\end{equation}

and by algebraic manipulation we can rewrite the quadrupolar interaction as

\begin{equation}
    \cH{Q} / \hbar = \mathbf{I} \cdot \bar{\bar{Q}} \cdot \mathbf{I} = \sum_{m = -2}^2 S_m T^{(2)}_m(\hat{\mathbf{I}})
    \label{eq:quadrupole_spherical}
\end{equation}

\noindent with

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
S_{0} &= Q_{zz}, \\
S_{\pm 1} &= Q_{zx} \pm i Q_{zy}, \\
S_{\pm 2} &= \tfrac{1}{2}(Q_{xx} - Q_{yy}) \pm i Q_{xy}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

One can show (Appendix \ref{app:rotations_quadrupole}) that the coefficients transform as 
\begin{equation}
S_m \;\mapsto\; e^{-im\zeta}\, S_m,
\end{equation}
under a physical rotation of angle $\zeta$ about the $z$-axis. Since the energy levels of the Hamiltonian are insensitive to these phase factors and depend only on invariants such as $S_0$, $|S_{\pm 1}|$, and $|S_{\pm 2}|$, the energy spectrum is invariant under such rotations. The matrix form of the quadrupole tensor in terms of these invariants is

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \bar{\bar{Q}}(\zeta) =&\begin{pmatrix}
-|S_2| \cos(2\zeta+2\Delta)-\frac{S_0}{2}&  |S_2|\sin(2\zeta+2\Delta) & |S_1|\cos(\zeta) \\ 
|S_2| \sin(2\zeta+2\Delta)  & |S_2|\cos(2\zeta+2\Delta)-\frac{S_0}{2} & |S_1|\sin(\zeta) \\
|S_1|\cos(\zeta) & |S_1|\sin(\zeta) & S_0\end{pmatrix} \\\\
\end{split}
\end{align}

where $\Delta$ is defined as

\begin{equation}
\Delta = \arctan\left(\frac{2Q_{xy}}{Q_{xx}-Q_{yy}}\right) -  \arctan\left(\frac{Q_{yz}}{Q_{xz}}\right)
\end{equation}

For single axis measurements, this leaves $S_0$, $|S_1|$, $|S_2|$, and $\Delta$ to be determined, whereas $\zeta$ can be fixed to an arbitrary value. This is a subtle point with an important consequence: any two tensors related by a rotation along the quantization axis produce identical NMR spectra for a fixed field orientation\sidenote{It is straightforward to see that the Zeeman term is also degenerate under rotations along the axis of $B_0$.}. Equivalently, no matter how precise the measurement of the nuclear transition frequencies, one can only reconstruct the quadrupole tensor up to a rotation about the field axis. Consequently, the only way to lift this degeneracy is by measuring the spectra at different orientations of the magnetic field\sidenote{At zero magnetic field, no orientation information can be extracted, only the magnitudes of the principal components of the quadrupole}.

Finally, we note that another degeneracy inherent to the one-axis nature of the measurement: $\Delta^{(\Downarrow)}$ is $\pi$/2 periodic. For the purposes of the fit, we fix that $0 <\Delta^{(\Downarrow)} < \pi/2$.

\subsubsection{Monte Carlo Markov Chains}

We now focus on fitting the parameters of the ground state Hamiltonian $\mathcal{H}^{(\Downarrow)}$ to the measured data, but the procedure is identical for $\mathcal{H}^{(\Uparrow)}$. We recall that 

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}^{(\Downarrow)}/\hbar = -\omega_S / 2 + \omega_I^{(\Downarrow)} I_z^{(\Downarrow)} + \mathbf{I^{(\Downarrow)}}\cdot \bar{\bar{Q}}^{(\Downarrow)}\cdot \mathbf{I^{(\Downarrow)}}.
\end{equation}

The parameters to fit are $\bar{\bar{Q}}^{(\Downarrow)}$ and $B^{(\Downarrow)}_0 = - \omega_I^{(\Downarrow)} / \gamma_\text{\Nb}$ , where $B^{(\Downarrow)}_0$ is the effective magnetic field experienced by the \Nb spin when the \Er is in the ground state, which combines the external field $B_0$ and the effective field generated by the \Er spin. When parameterizing the quadrupole in terms of the rank-2 spherical tensors, the total set of variables to fit is defined as $\mathcal{V^{(\Downarrow)}} \equiv\{B^{(\Downarrow)}_0, S^{(\Downarrow)}_0, |S^{(\Downarrow)}_1|, |S^{(\Downarrow)}_2|, \Delta^{(\Downarrow)}\}$. Following the discussion of the previous section, we fix $\zeta^{(\Downarrow)}=0$.

The ground state NMR transitions $\omega_{n,\ n+1}^{\text{fit}}(\mathcal{V^{(\Downarrow)}})$ are calculated by numerically diagonalizing $H^{(\Downarrow)}(\mathcal{V}^{(\Downarrow)})$ and calculating the energy differences between the relevant energy levels. We define the logarithmic-likelihood as

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}^{(\Downarrow)}(\mathcal{V}^{(\Downarrow)}) = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^9 \left(\frac{\omega^{(\Downarrow)}_{n, \ n+1} - \omega_{n, \ n+1}^{\text{fit}}(\mathcal{V^{(\Downarrow)}})}{\sigma^{(\Downarrow)}_{n, \ n+1}}\right)^2,
\end{equation}

where $\sigma^{(\Downarrow)}_n=1$~Hz is the measurement uncertainty. The Hamiltonian fit was performed via Monte-Carlo Markov-Chain simulation \sidecite{foreman-mackey_emcee_2013}. In brief, the algorithm initializes an arbitrary number of walkers, or particles, in the parameter space. After an evaluation of the $\mathcal{L}^{(\Downarrow)}$ function, the positions of the walkers are updated through the "stretch move" \sidecite{goodman_ensemble_2010} with the objective of maximizing the evaluation for the next iteration. We run the algorithm with 64 walkers and 60000 iterations, from which we obtain the following median values for the ground and excited state parameters

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    &B^{(\Downarrow)}_0 =  460.8320(4)\text{~mT} \; | \; S^{(\Downarrow)}_0/2\pi = -237.3609(6)\text{~kHz}\\  
    |S^{(\Downarrow)}_1|/2\pi& = 2.08(5) \text{~kHz} \; | \;  |S^{(\Downarrow)}_2|/2\pi = 149.456(6)\text{~kHz}\; | \; \Delta^{(\Downarrow)} = 0.2(1) \\ 
    \\
    &B^{(\Uparrow)}_0 = 447.994(6)\text{~mT} \; | \; S^{(\Uparrow)}_0/2\pi = -237.256(9)\text{~kHz} \\
    |S^{(\Uparrow)}_1|/2\pi& = 5.0(3)\text{~kHz} \; | \; |S^{(\Uparrow)}_2|/2\pi = 149.48(8)\text{~kHz}\; | \; \Delta^{(\Uparrow)} = 0.2(1)
\end{split}
\end{align}

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics{chapter5/figures/fit_residuals.pdf}
    \caption[Residuals for the $\mathcal{H}^{(\Downarrow)}$ and $\mathcal{H}^{(\Uparrow)}$ fit]{Residuals for the $\mathcal{H}^{(\Downarrow)}$ and $\mathcal{H}^{(\Uparrow)}$ fit, top and bottom respectively. Error bars correspond to the uncertainty of the measured values. Dashed line corresponds to zero deviation.}
    \labfig{fit_residuals}
\end{figure}

The residuals of the two fits are shown in \reffig{fit_residuals}. The results agree up to statistical uncertainty ($1$\,Hz for the $\Downarrow$ data, $20$\,Hz for the $\Uparrow$ data), and show no discernible trend. We calculate the reduced chi-squared for the ground and excited state and obtain $\chi^{2,(\Downarrow)}_\nu=0.75$ and $\chi^{2,(\Uparrow)}_\nu=1.14$, respectively. The proximity of these values to 1 indicates that the model correctly describes the data while avoiding over-fitting. We note that the uncertainty of $S_1^{(\Downarrow)}$ and $\Delta^{(\Downarrow)}$ is much larger than the other parameters. This is readily explained by the effect of these quantities on the energy levels of the Hamiltonian. On the one hand, $S_1$, $\Delta$ are closely related to the values of the quadrupole perpendicular to $B_0$, and are only observable as second and third order effects respectively. On the other hand, $B_0$, $S_0$ and $S_2$ are responsible for zero and first order effects and their estimation is more precise. Appendix \ref{app:mcmc_fit} dives into the resulting posterior distributions and explicit details of the fit procedure.

The fit yields the quadrupolar tensors $\bar{\bar{Q}}^{(\Downarrow)}$ and $\bar{\bar{Q}}^{(\Uparrow)}$, up to an arbitrary rotation around the $z^{(\Downarrow)}$ and $z^{(\Uparrow)}$ axes, respectively. Given this degeneracy and the fact that the quantization axis are not parallel in the two cases, component-wise comparison of the tensor is inappropriate. Therefore, we compare the principal values of the tensor obtained diagonalizing $\bar{\bar{Q}}^{(\Downarrow)}$ and $\bar{\bar{Q}}^{(\Uparrow)}$. This analysis reveals that the principal axis associated with the largest eigenvalue (denoted $X$) lies approximately within the crystal $(a,b)$-plane, up to an undetermined in-plane angle. The second-strongest principal axis (denoted as $Z$) is approximately parallel to the crystal $c$-axis and therefore to $B_0$.

    \labfig{q_histograms}
\end{figure}

The posterior distribution of the quadrupole principal values are shown in \reffig{q_histograms} for both \Er spin states. The main result is the existence of a statistically-significant ($3\sigma$) difference between the values of $Q_{Z}^{(\Uparrow)}$ and $Q_{Z}^{(\Downarrow)}$. Due to the larger uncertainty on $Q_{X}$ and $Q_{Y}$, this observation is not reproduced on $X$ or $Y$. Consequently, our results indicate the existence of a new term in the spin Hamiltonian Eq.~\ref{eq:hamiltonian_ninehalf_full}, which modifies the nuclear quadrupolar interaction depending on the electron spin state, or Spin Dependent Quadrupole (SDQ) interaction. 

\subsection{Fitting $\mathcal{H}$}

To further test our model, we take a step back in the approximations and simultaneously fit all frequencies. To account for the SDQ coupling, we introduce a new term in the system Hamiltonian,

%term that models the interaction measured in the previous section. We model this term in analogy to a standard quadrupole term, coupled to the state of the electron spin. However, given that our measurements are maximally sensitive along the $z$ axis we only consider first order effects,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:SDQ}
\mathcal{H}_\mathrm{sdq} = Q_\mathrm{sdq} \, S_z \tfrac{1}{2} \left(3 I_z^2 - I(I+1) \right).
\end{equation}

Where $Q_\mathrm{sdq}$ is the interaction coupling strength. Moreover, to account for the high spectral resolution of our data we include additional terms that we had so far neglected: the non-secular hyperfine, and the frequency shifts that arise from coupling to the resonator.

\subsubsection{Non-secular hyperfine}

Up to this point, we have treated the hyperfine interaction within the secular approximation, retaining only terms proportional to $S_z$. This approximation is well justified by the large mismatch between the electronic and nuclear Zeeman energies, which differ by approximately three orders of magnitude. However, given the smallness of the spin-dependent interaction, it becomes necessary to include non-secular hyperfine contributions.

Given that non-secular hyperfine terms will induce second- and third-order corrections to the energy spectrum and exhibit strong correlation with each other, we choose not to directly fit these terms. Instead, we calculate their values using magnetic dipole simulations (see \refsec{interaction_between_to_spins}). This approximation is justified by the robustness of the site assignment, where the discrepancy between the measured and calculated hyperfine is smaller than 10\%. 

Furthermore, we fix the perpendicular hyperfine term to the independently measured value reported in \refsec{solid_effect_ninehalf}, $A_\perp/2\pi = 46(9)$~kHz. As shown in \refsec{assignment}, this value quantitatively agrees with the dipole-dipole simulations. However, in the fit we will include the uncertainty of the measured parameter.

Consequently, the hyperfine tensor is given by 

\begin{equation}
\bar{\bar{A}}/2\pi=\begin{pmatrix}
-438, &  0,    &  -19.5\\
0     & -440.4 &       0\\
46(9) & 0      & A_\parallel/2\pi
\end{pmatrix} \text{~kHz}
\end{equation}

and the only free parameter of the hyperfine interaction is $A_\parallel$.

\subsubsection{Lamb shift}

First, we consider the Lamb shift of the EPR-allowed transitions, which renormalize the \Nb transitions in the excited state of the \Er spin. As discussed in \refsec{spin_coupled_to_resonator}, the Lamb shift of the excited state of a spin-1/2 coupled to a resonator in its (vacuum) ground state, with coupling strength $g_0$ and detuning $\Delta$ is given by

\begin{equation}
\Delta\omega = \Delta \frac{g_0^2}{\Delta^2 + \kappa^2/4}.
\end{equation}

In the case of the coupled \Nb--\Er system, the \Er excited state levels $\ket{\Uparrow,n}$ are shifted by $\Delta \omega_n = g_0^2 \frac{\Delta_n}{\Delta_n^2 + \kappa^2/4}$, where $\Delta_n = \omega_0 - \omega_n$ is the detuning of the EPR-allowed transition from the resonator frequency. The order of magnitude is $\Delta \omega / 2\pi \sim g_0^2 / (2\pi\kappa) \sim 100$~Hz and the dependence on $n$ causes shifts $\Delta_{n+1} - \Delta_n$ of the NMR transition frequencies $\omega_{n,n+1}^\Uparrow $, which therefore must be taken into account in our analysis. We achieve this by including the following term in the Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_L = \Delta \omega_n \ket{\Uparrow,n}\bra{\Uparrow,n}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Final Hamiltonian}

Combining all the terms, the full Hamiltonian of an \Er:\Ca spin coupled to a \Nb nuclear spin (and to its detection resonator) is

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
        \mathcal{H}/\hbar &= \omega_S\cdot S_z + B_0 \gamma_{\text{\Nb}} \cdot I_z +\mathbf{S} \cdot \bar{\bar{A}} \cdot \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{I} \cdot \bar{\bar{Q}} \cdot \mathbf{I} + \\ 
        &+ Q_\mathrm{sdq} \, S_z \tfrac{1}{2}  [3 I_z^2 - I(I+1) ]  + \Delta \omega_n \ket{\Uparrow,n}\bra{\Uparrow,n}
\end{split}
\label{eq:full_hamiltonian_all}
\end{align}

Note that this Hamiltonian is not invariant under rotation around the $z$ axis; therefore, the full quadrupolar tensor can now be obtained. Following the parameterization presented in \refsec{rot_invariance}, the set of eight variables to fit is defined as $\mathcal{V}\equiv\{\gamma_{\text{\Nb}}, A_\parallel, S_0,S_1,S_2,\Delta,\zeta,Q^{\text{(SDQ)}}_{zzz}\}$. Naturally, we define the total likelihood as

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{V}) = \mathcal{L}^{(\Downarrow)}(\mathcal{V}) + \mathcal{L}^{(\Uparrow)}(\mathcal{V}).
\end{equation}

The fit is performed using the MCMC algorithm described in the previous section. To account for the 9~kHz uncertainty in the measurement of $A_\perp$, we repeat the fit 400 times while sampling the value of $A_\perp$ from a Gaussian distribution with a standard deviation of 9~kHz. The median values for the parameters, obtained from the resulting ensemble of fits, are given by

\begin{align}
\label{seq:full_fit_results}
\begin{split}
    \gamma_{\text{\Nb}} =  10.6113(1) \text{~MHz/T} \; &| \; A_\parallel/2\pi =  133.751(5)\text{~kHz} \\ 
    S_0/2\pi= -237.314(3)\text{~kHz} \; &| \; S_1/2\pi= -3.44(1) \text{~kHz} \\
    S_2/2\pi = -149.457(5)\text{~kHz}\; &| \;  Q_\mathrm{sdq}/2\pi= 76(4) \text{~kHz}\\
    \Delta = 1.62(2)\; &| \;\zeta = 0.9(1), \\
\end{split}
\end{align}

\noindent with a reduced chi-squared of $\chi^2_\nu=1.2$ and residuals similar to those reported in \reffig{fit_residuals}. Compared to the estimate of the gyromagnetic tensor in \refsec{assignment}, this value is considerably closer to the literature. We assign the remaining difference to the pseudo-nuclear-Zeeman effect and/or the chemical shift.

From these values, we reconstruct the nuclear quadrupole tensor $\bar{\bar{Q}}$ and diagonalize it to obtain its principal axes $X$, $Y$, and $Z$. We find that the $Z$ axis is tilted by $0.06(3)^\circ$ with respect to the crystal $c$ axis, while the $X$ axis is rotated by $-10(6)^\circ$ relative to the crystal $a$ axis. The corresponding principal values are $Q_X/2\pi = 268.138(9)$~kHz, $Q_Y/2\pi = -30.799(7)$~kHz, and $Q_Z/2\pi = -237.338(3)$~kHz. These values correspond to a quadrupolar interaction strength of $C_q / 2\pi = 19.3059(7)\,\mathrm{MHz}$ and a biaxiality parameter $\eta = 0.77026(7)$.

\section{Origin of the SDQ interaction}

We identify two possible mechanisms that could account for the SDQ term: the pseudo-quadrupole coupling and a magnetically-induced electrostatic interaction. The origin and strength of these interactions for \Er:\Ca was discussed in \refsec{erbium_ions_in_ca}. In the following we numerically investigate the strength of these two effects.

%The first one is the magnetic coupling of the \Nb to higher-excited states of the \Er. Such coupling is well-known to cause nuclear frequency shifts that mimic a quadrupolar interaction, leading to a so-called pseudo-quadrupolar contribution of magnetic origin \sidecite{foley_secondorder_1947, abragam_electron_2012}. This pseudo-quadrupolar component might itself depend on the \Er spin state, as envisioned in \sidecite{ghatikar_zeemanfielddependent_1966}. 

%The second one is the electrostatic interaction between the \Er and the \Nb spins. Bloembergen predicted \sidecite{bloembergen_linear_1961} that paramagnetic centers located at sites without inversion symmetry carry an electric dipole whose magnitude is proportional to $B_0$ and whose orientation changes with the spin state. This leads to linear electric-field shifts of the EPR transition frequency that have been observed in numerous paramagnetic systems~\sidecite{ludwig_splitting_1961}, including in \Er:\Ca \sidecite{mims_electric_1964, mims_electric_1965}. 


\subsection{Pseudo-quadrupole interaction}

In this section we attempt to explain the observed SDQ effect through the action of higher energy Kramers doublets $Z_2, \dots,Z_8$ on the nuclear transition energies of the \Nb spin. As explained in \refsec{spin_hamiltonian_final}, the spin-1/2 model (Eq.~\ref{eq:spin_half_hamiltonian}) is a restriction of the more complete Hamiltonian (Eq.~\ref{eq:full_spin_hamiltonian}) to the lowest two \Er energy levels. In particular, the hyperfine Hamiltonian coupling the effective spin $S$ and the nuclear spin is a two-level restriction of the hyperfine Hamiltonian $\cH{hf, $J$}$ (Eq.\ref{eq:hf_J_hamiltonian}). The latter couples the nuclear spin not only to the $Z_1$ doublet, but also to the other higher-energy $Z_{2-8}$ states. These couplings renormalize the nuclear energy levels in a manner that may mimic an effective quadrupolar interaction. This pseudo-quadrupolar interaction is of purely magnetic origin, contrary to the real quadrupolar interaction~\sidecite{foley_secondorder_1947,abragam_electron_2012}, and can be of similar order of magnitude as the electric quadrupole when the nuclear spin is located on the nucleus of the paramagnetic ion~\sidecite{schweiger_principles_2001}. 

The effective Hamiltonian of the pseudo-quadrupolar interaction depends on the point-symmetry of the site where the ion is located. It has been predicted that this interaction can be described by a term of the form $S_z I_z^2$ for D$_{3\text{h}}$ symmetry \cite{ghatikar_zeemanfielddependent_1966}, which would imply that the pseudo-quadrupolar part may depend on the spin state, as observed in our experiment. However, no prediction was made for the $S_4$ symmetry. We therefore rely on numerical simulations to estimate the relevance of this effect.


To quantitatively characterize the effect of the pseudo-Quadrupole for this specific configuration, we proceed by performing the following numerical analysis. First, we generate a series of the NMR transitions for the \Nb -- \Er system by diagonalizing the $J=15/2$ Hamiltonian

\begin{align}
        \mathcal{H} =\ \mu_B \ g_J\mathbf{B_0}\cdot \mathbf{J} + \mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cf}} 
        + \omega_I I_z + \mathbf{I}\cdot \bar{\bar{Q}}\cdot \mathbf{I}  
    + \ \mathbf{J}\cdot \lambda \bar{\bar{A}}_J\cdot\mathbf{I} +\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{L}}.
\end{align}

As for the Hamiltonian fit in the previous section, the hyperfine coupling tensor $\bar{\bar{A}}_J$ is calculated from a dipole-dipole interaction between the \Er spin and the \Nb at the expected position. We use the $S_4$-symmetric crystal-field Hamiltonian $\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cf}} $ determined in~\cite{enrique_optical_1971}, whose validity is questionable here due to the $S_4$ symmetry breaking due to the \Nb presence. Given that the pseudo-quadrupole effect is a second order interaction of the hyperfine coupling, we use a dimensionless scaling parameter $\lambda$ for the hyperfine interaction strength. Otherwise, the Hamiltonian parameters are set to the measured values. 

The NMR transition frequencies computed in the lowest two \Er manifolds are then fitted with the effective spin-1/2 model, using the same procedure as the one used for the experimental data (described in the previous section), yielding the effective quadrupolar interaction in each \Er state. The difference between the principal values along the $c$ axis between the $\uparrow$ and $\downarrow$ values, $Q_\mathrm{sdq, pq}$, is shown in \reffig{pseudoquadrupole}a as a function of the scaling parameter $\lambda$.

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics{chapter5/figures/pseudoquadrupole.pdf}
    \caption[Pseudo-Quadrupole shift simulation]{Pseudo-Quadrupole shift simulation.
    \textit{(left)} Effective SDQ interaction from the pseudo-quadrupole, $Q_\mathrm{sdq, pq}$, is plotted as a function of the hyperfine scaling parameter $\lambda$. The vertical line marks $\lambda=0$. The simulation was performed for $\theta_S=-0.57^\circ$ and $\beta_0=-0.57^\circ $.
    \textit{(right)} $Q_\mathrm{sdq, pq}$ as a function of the in-plane angle $\theta_S$ with $\lambda=1$ and $\beta_0=-0.57^\circ$.}
    \labfig{pseudoquadrupole}
\end{figure}

We first note that the computed $Q_\mathrm{sdq, pq}$ is $0$ when $\lambda=0$, as expected. For non-zero values of $\lambda$, a non-zero spin-dependent pseudo-quadrupole is computed. Its value scales quadratically with the hyperfine scaling factor, as expected for an effect that can be described in second-order perturbation theory. The magnitude of the interaction is of similar order as the experimentally measured value, although the $\lambda=1$ value $Q_\mathrm{sdq, pq}/2\pi =-8$~Hz is a factor $10$ smaller than the measurement and has an opposite sign. We also compute $Q_\mathrm{sdq, pq}$ at $\lambda=1$ as a function of $\theta$ (see \reffig{pseudoquadrupole}b). In a window of $\pm5$ degrees, $Q_\mathrm{sdq, pq}$ remains consistently smaller than 10~Hz in absolute value.

We conclude that pseudo-quadrupole interactions arising from second-order paramagnetic coupling between the electron $J$-multiplet and the nuclear spin needs to be considered in a complete analysis. However, it has opposite sign and is one order of magnitude smaller than the measurements, implying that the effect is likely not the only one at play.

\subsection{Electrostatic coupling}

The second one is the electrostatic interaction between the \Er and the \Nb spins. Bloembergen predicted \sidecite{bloembergen_linear_1961} that paramagnetic centers located at sites without inversion symmetry carry an electric dipole whose magnitude is proportional to $B_0$ and whose orientation changes with the spin state. This leads to linear electric-field shifts of the EPR transition frequency that have been observed in numerous paramagnetic systems~\sidecite{ludwig_splitting_1961}, including in \Er:\Ca \sidecite{mims_electric_1964, mims_electric_1965}. This effect was discussed in \refsec{erbium_ions_in_ca}, but we briefly summarize the results here. 

Ions in non-centrosymmetric sites will experience admixing of wavefunctions with different parity due to the odd terms of the crystal field Hamiltonian \sidecite{kiel_theory_1966}. In turn, this results in a magnetoelectric effect which couples static electric and magnetic fields and can be effectively described as a magnetically induced electric dipole. As described in \refsec{erbium_ions_in_ca}, the Hamiltonian used to describe this effect is 

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \cH{m-e} / \hbar &= \sum_k-d_k E_k = -\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{E}, \\
    d_k &= - \sum_{i,j} T_{k, i, j} \langle \hat{S}_{i}\rangle B_j.
\end{split}
\label{eq:electric_dipole_ninehalf}
\end{align}

where $\mathbf{d_k}$ acts as an effective electric dipole which is proportional to the static magnetic field. The numerical values of the $T_{k,i,j}$ were determined by Mims~\sidecite{mims_electric_1965} and are provided in \refsec{erbium_ions_in_ca}. We note that, in the high-field regime, the spin orientation in the ground state and excited state are opposite and the associated electric dipole moments of each state also point in opposite directions. Consequently, the electric field and electric field gradient generated by the dipole will be different depending on the electron spin state. Bloembergen~\sidecite{bloembergen_linear_1961} also predicted that an externally applied electric field modifies linearly the quadrupolar interaction of a nuclear spin if the latter is located at a site lacking inversion symmetry \sidecite{armstrong_linear_1961}. In our system, both the \Er and the \Nb sites lack inversion symmetry, and we hypothesize that the electric field generated by the spin-dependent \Er dipole couples linearly to the \Nb quadrupole, leading to the SDQ effect. Although the electric quadrupole sensitivity of \Nb:\Ca is not known, values ranging between $0.1$ and $1$ Hz/(V/cm) were reported for a variety of nuclei \sidecite{macfarlane_optical_2014}.

It is relevant to note that for \Er:\Ca the dipole moment is zero when the magnetic field is parallel to the $c$-axis ($\theta_S = 0$, $\beta = 0$).. However, in the experiment, $\theta_S=-0.57^\circ$ and $\beta=-0.57^\circ$ so the electric dipole is non-zero. Additionally, the symmetry of the system is broken by the presence of the \Nb impurity as discussed in the previous section. This will likely result on a different sensitivity to electric fields, and we note that the values for this estimation only work as a reasonable order of magnitude calculation.

We estimate the strength of the dipole by evaluating Eq. \ref{eq:electric_dipole_ninehalf} and obtain $\mathbf{d} = \pm$(-0.26, 0.25, 0.02)~mD. The dipole is confined in the $(a,b)$-plane of the crystal and generates both an electric field and electric field gradient at the position of the \Nb, with values

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    &\mathbf{E} = (34,~-33.6,~7) \text{~V/cm}\\
    &\partial E_z / \partial z = V^{\text{(d)}}_{zz} = 0.02~\text{\textmu V/\r{A}}^2
\end{split}
\end{align}

The electric field gradient induces a spin-dependent quadrupole of $C_\mathrm{sdq, dipole} \approx 0.2$~mHz, far smaller than the observed interaction (76~Hz). The electric field at the Nb site is $\sim$48~V/cm, giving a differential field of $\sim$96~V/cm between the two spin states. If we assume a similar sensitivity for \Nb:\Ca as for the previously studied nuclear spins \sidecite{macfarlane_optical_2014} we obtain an interaction strength of $\sim$10 -- 100~Hz, consistent with the measured value. Our data therefore bring some evidence for a new type of coupling between a paramagnetic center and a nuclear spin, of electrostatic nature. 

\section{Octupole and hexadecapolar bounds}

Given the spectral resolution of the ground-state data, we can test the presence of higher-order terms in the nuclear spin Hamiltonian, beyond the magnetic dipolar and electric quadrupolar interactions. In our system, a magnetic octupole term in the \Nb Hamiltonian may arise due to the magnetic field gradients caused by the proximal \Er magnetic dipole, while a hexadecapolar term may be caused by the crystal field. We test the presence of an octupolar and hexadecapolar term by independently adding the following terms to $\mathcal{H^{(\Downarrow)}}$ and fitting the ground state data,

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \cH{octupole} &= \frac{C_3}{I(2I-1)(I-1)}\,I_z^3 , \\
    \cH{hexadecapole} &= \frac{C_4}{I(2I-1)(I-1)(2 I -3)}\,  I_z^4.
\end{split}
\end{align}

\reffig{octupole_hexadecapole} shows the posterior distribution of $C_3$ and $C_4$ for each of two fits. We find $C_3 = 8(14)$~Hz and $C_4 = 12(10)$~Hz, compatible with zero in both cases.

    \includegraphics{chapter5/figures/octupole_hexadecapole.pdf}
    \caption[Octupole and hexadecapole posterior distributions]{Posterior distributions for the octupole and hexadecapole coupling constant ($C_3$ and $C_4$ respectively) from the MCMC fits. The fit was performed by including the term $\cH{octupole}$ ($\cH{hexadecapole}$) to $\mathcal{H^{(\Downarrow)}}$ and following the procedure described in \refsec{final_hamiltonian_fit}.}
    \labfig{octupole_hexadecapole}
\end{figure}

The vanishingly low octupolar interaction $C_3$ is not surprising given the relatively large ($0.57$\,nm) distance between the \Er magnetic moment and the \Nb atom. Much larger octupolar interactions may be expected in spin systems combining a high-spin nucleus and a paramagnetic center with large orbital moment on the same atom, such as $^{167}\mathrm{Er}^{3+}$. The upper bound on the hexadecapolar interaction $C_4$ is one order of magnitude tighter than the most precise ensemble NMR measurement reported of $C_4=58\pm140$~Hz~\sidecite{liao_nuclear_1994}. Indeed, the spectral resolution of ensemble measurements is limited by the dipolar coupling between the constituent nuclear spins. Instead, we achieve one order of magnitude narrower linewidth by measuring an individual impurity in an otherwise nuclear-spin-silent crystal and by polarizing the surrounding heteronuclear spin environment to further reduce the linewidth. With longer coherence times, and applied to nuclei for which larger hexadecapolar interaction is predicted such as $^{175}\mathrm{Lu}$, $^{179}\mathrm{Hf}$, or $^{181}\mathrm{Ta}$, our method may provide a viable route towards hexadecapolar interaction measurements in the solid-state.

\section{Conclusion and outlook}

We now discuss the generality of our findings. The spin-dependent pseudo-quadrupole mechanism should be sizable in paramagnetic centers with low-lying excited states, such as metallic centers and particularly lanthanides. The direct electrical coupling mechanism requires paramagnetic centers with spin-orbit coupling and non-inversion symmetry, also found in metallic centers. Overall, measurable SDQ terms should therefore be found mainly in systems involving metallic paramagnetic centers (molecules or impurities in solids), but not in organic radicals. Larger SDQ values than the one reported here should be observable on the nuclear spin belonging to the metallic center itself, for instance in $^{167}\mathrm{Er}^{3+}$. 

The detection of single paramagnetic centers by microwave photon counting only requires sufficiently long spin-lattice relaxation times at 10~mK, and should therefore be applicable to a large variety of systems and samples~\sidecite{wang_singleelectron_2023}. Our demonstrated ability to characterize chemically and structurally an unknown nuclear-spin-carrying atomic impurity next to a paramagnetic center therefore opens interesting perspectives, such as the ability to characterize the constituent atoms of individual molecules for instance. The high spectral resolution reached in the \Nb levels spectroscopy should be achievable more generally in arbitrary nuclear spin systems, as long as they can be coupled to a paramagnetic center measurable by photon counting. This would be particularly relevant for quadrupolar nuclei in disordered systems (molecules in frozen solutions for instance), where high spectral resolution is difficult to achieve by conventional ensemble methods. Regarding the new spin-dependent-quadrupole interaction term evidenced here, future work could measure its magnitude in other coupled paramagnetic-nuclear spin systems, and possibly confirm its electrostatic origin. Finally, our results suggest that precision hyperfine spectroscopy by single-spin measurement using microwave photon counting bears potential to observe and study magnetic octupole and electric hexadecapolar interactions in the solid-state.
