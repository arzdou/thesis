\chapter{Introduction}


% Motivation

Nuclear magnetic resonance is among the most precise spectroscopic techniques available for probing matter at the atomic scale. By exploiting the magnetic response of nuclear spins, it reveals detailed information about the local structure and interactions of physical systems~\sidecite{abragam_principles_1986, slichter_principles_1996}. It has a wide range of applications, both in the academic and the industrial spheres, ranging from medical imaging~\sidecite{lauterbur_image_1973}, to structural biology~\sidecite{wuthrich_protein_1989}, pharmaceutical quality control~\sidecite{holzgrabe_quantitative_2010} and materials science~\sidecite{haworth_solidstate_2022}.

In a fundamental level, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy resonantly isolates the signal from the magnetic and angular momentum of nuclear spins out of the total magnetic susceptibility of a sample, which enables the measurement of relatively weak signals. However, one of the main limitations of NMR spectroscopy is the need of nuclear spin ensembles, as the signal of a single nuclear spin is not strong enough to detect. When dealing with ensembles in the solid-state, two effects will distort and broaden the resonance signal. Magnetic dipole interaction between the nuclei shifts the individual resonance frequency and cause dephasing. In addition, differences in the local environment of each nuclear spin will create a static shift of the resonance frequency. These two effects broaden the resonance linewidth and reduce the precision of the frequency measurement. Magnetic dipole interactions can be averaged away through a physical rotation of the sample, a technique known as magic angle spinning \sidecite{andrew_nuclear_1958, lowe_free_1959}. On the contrary, static broadening is often unavoidable in ensemble measurements and limits resolution. In addition, static shifts become larger for high-nuclear spins ($I>1/2$) with quadrupolar interaction, as it is proportional to electric field gradients, and therefore to local strain conditions. Therefore, the small shifts produced by higher terms such as the octupole and hexadecapole have not been observed in the solid-state despite active effort~\sidecite{liao_nuclear_1994}.

The ultimate limit for nuclear magnetic resonance is at the single spin level, where the precision of the signal is only limited by the electromagnetic noise in the system. Nuclear spins are highly isolated owing to their low gyromagnetic ratio and their response to external noise is weak, resulting in long coherence times. Consequently, numerous architectures have been put forth for using nuclear spins for quantum computing \sidecite{awschalom_quantum_2018,zwanenburg_silicon_2013}, raising interest towards the detection and control of single nuclear spins.

So far, the most successful route to single nuclear-spin detection is to couple the nucleus to a nearby electron spin. The idea of detecting nuclear spins coupled to electrons is not new, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) \sidecite{schweiger_principles_2001} is the sister technique to NMR, where the target of study are electron spins instead. The presence of nuclear spins can be detected in EPR by a modulation of the Hahn echo decay due to the multilevel nature of the hyperfine structure~\sidecite{rowan_electron_1965}. Direct and indirect driving of the nuclear spins modifies the EPR spectra and is used to infer the hyperfine parameters of a system~\sidecite{mims_pulsed_1965, davies_new_1974, schosseler_pulsed_1994}. While providing lower resolution, these techniques allow the measurement of electron-nuclear interactions which holds valuable structural information. However, the same limitations of inhomogeneous and dipolar broadening apply, and magic angle spinning is not a common technique due to the technical complexity~\sidecite{nir-arad_highfield_2024}. In addition, dipolar interactions with the nuclear spin bath will induce an extra source of decoherence which can be mitigated through the use of dynamical nuclear polarization (DNP)~\sidecite{overhauser_polarization_1953}.

Individual detection of electron spins has been demonstrated by leveraging state-dependent optical fluorescence~\sidecite{jelezko_observation_2004} or spin-to-charge conversion mechanisms~\sidecite{morello_singleshot_2010} which are present in a select number of systems. High-fidelity electron spin readout has enabled the detection of single nuclear spins such as $^{13}\mathrm C$~\sidecite{neumann_singleshot_2010}, $^{31}\mathrm P$\sidecite{pla_highfidelity_2013} and $^{123}\mathrm {Sb}$~\sidecite{asaad_coherent_2020} among others. These techniques are system specific and are not easily generalizable to a broader class of samples and a more general single spin detection technique is missing.

Our group has been focused on the development of a new and more general technique to detect individual electron spins in the solid state. This approach is based on the detection of the microwave spontaneous emission of the spin, and has recently achieved enough sensitivity for single spin detection~\sidecite{wang_singleelectron_2023}. This experiment leverages the large sensitivity of the single microwave photon detector (SMPD), a new type of detector which transposes optical detection of individual photons to the microwave regime. The detector is based on a transmon qubit that captures incoming microwave photons in a narrow bandwidth and can be readout with high fidelity~\sidecite{lescanne_irreversible_2020}. The SMPD used during for the experiments in this work has a sensitivity of $3\times 10^{-23}$~W/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ at $7.7$~GHz, making it the most sensitive detector at this frequency band. This demonstration does not leverage any system specific mechanism and relies only on the detection of the fluorescence of the spin transition. Therefore, this technique can be applied, in principle, to a large range of electron and substrate systems.

The goal of this manuscript is to \textbf{demonstrate how single microwave photon counting can be used to perform detection of individual nuclear spins. I then proceed demonstrate EDNMR spectroscopy, dynamical nuclear polarization and coherent control. Using only microwave pulses I build single- and two-qubit gates to demonstrate the operation of a two qubit register with seconds long coherence time. Finally, I perform precision NMR spectroscopy and detect a new type of interaction between electron and nuclear spins and set a tighter bound on the hexadecapolar interaction in the solid-state.}


The first part of this thesis aims to provide the necessary background to understand the experiments. We cover the physics of lanthanides and how an erbium ion can be treated as an effective electron with spin-1/2 when it enters in substitution in \Ca. In addition, we also discuss the energy level structure of quadrupolar nuclear spins and the hyperfine interaction between the two spin species. We then present the basic concepts of Cavity-QED and how coupling a high-quality resonator enhances the spontaneous relaxation rate via the Purcell effect \cite{purcell_resonance_1946}. Also, we introduce the unique type of detector used in this work: the Single-Microwave-Photon-Detector (SMPD)  Finally, we present two techniques to control the state of the nuclear spin which using only microwave pulses: sideband and stimulated Raman driving.

In the second part of the manuscript, we leverage the single-electron spin sensitivity and the long coherence of \Er spins to detect proximal nuclear spins. The nuclear bath in \Ca is dominated by \W nuclear spins with $\sim14$\% abundance. Consequently, each \Er spin displays a different environment: some will have no neighbouring \W and other might have several. In Chapter \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w} we probe the nuclear spin environment through high-resolution EPR spectroscopy at a fixed magnetic field where a single \Er is resonant with the cavity. We measure the number of fluorescent counts after a microwave pulse as a function of pulse frequency and time (see \reffig{ch1}b). For some \Er spins, the frequency displays telegraphic behaviour which we associate with changes in the state of a neighbouring \W nuclear spin. As shown in see \reffig{ch1}a the hyperfine coupling splits the electron resonance frequency depending on the state of the \W nuclear spin.

By leveraging the spectral separation of the electron spin transition we define a single-shot quantum-non-demolition readout of the nuclear spin state. The redout protocol applies two interleaved microwave pulses at each of the EPR-allowed \red{introduce before} transitions and collects the fluorescence counts after each pulse. The histogram of the count difference shows two symmetric and well resolved peaks (see \reffig{ch1}c) which enables single-shot readout of the nuclear spin state. 

The zero- and double-quantum transitions (represented in \reffig{ch1}a with dashed arrows) are weakly allowed due to the hyperfine interaction and simultaneously change the state of the electron and nuclear spin. In ensemble EPR, this weak coupling is exploited in electron-electron double resonance (ELDOR) detected NMR (EDNMR) measurements to probe the hyperfine spectra~\sidecite{schosseler_pulsed_1994}. Here we demonstrate a single spin EDNMR technique by directly driving the ZQ/DQ transitions. We use the resulting spectra (shown in \reffig{ch1}d) to identify the coupled nuclear spin as a \W.

Finally, we use solid-effect dynamical nuclear polarization (DNP)~\sidecite{abragama.proctow._nouvelle_1958} to define a preparation protocol for the nuclear spins. As shown in  \reffig{ch1}e, repeatedly pulsing the ZQ (DQ) transition allows one to polarize the nuclear spin to $\ket{\downarrow}$ ($\ket{\uparrow}$).


%This demonstration relies on the hyperfine coupling between the spins, which splits the electron spin transitions depending on the state of the nuclear spin. Thanks to the low decoherence rate of the \Er spins, enabled by the low concentration of magnetic spins in the crystal, we are able to resolve the spin-dependent microwave fluorescence, and we define a single-shot quantum-non-demolition readout protocol on the nuclear spin state. Using a single spin version of Electron-Electron Double Resonance (ELDOR) detected NMR (EDNMR) we identify the chemical nature and the relative position of the \W spins with respect to the \Er spin. Finally, we demonstrate solid-effect Dynamical Nuclear Polarization (DNP) of the individual nuclear spins~\sidecite{travesedo_allmicrowave_2025}.

\section{A register of two nuclear spin qubits}

Nuclear spins in the solid state are highly coherent quantum systems and different architectures for quantum computing using electron and nuclear spins in the solid state have been proposed \cite{dutt_quantum_2007b,awschalom_quantum_2018,zwanenburg_silicon_2013}. The ability to detect and coherently control the state of nuclear spin with high fidelity quantum register has been demonstrated in a series of systems \sidecite{bradley_tenqubit_2019,pla_highfidelity_2013, song_entanglement_2026}. Although some electrical control schemes have been demonstrated for high-spin nuclei~\sidecite{asaad_coherent_2020}, the implementation of gates is typically based on dynamical decoupling sequences, which are not suited in our specific system. In the third part of the thesis we propose and implement single and two-qubit gates on a two-\W quantum register by  stimulated Raman driving.

The principle of stimulated Raman driving is shown in \reffig{ch2}a. Two detuned microwave pulses drive a second order process capable of transferring population between the nuclear spin levels. The transfer does not significantly populate the electron spin excited levels which preserves the coherence properties of the nuclear spins. This is demonstrated in the NMR spectroscopy of \reffig{ch2}b, which plots the nuclear spin $\downarrow$ population as a function of the pulse frequency. The linewidth of the transition is smaller than 1~kHz, much smaller than the electron decoherence rate. We directly measure the coherence time of the nuclear spin through a Ramsey sequence and find $T_2^*=0.8$~s, as shown in \reffig{ch2}c. This long coherence time is readily explained by the low magnetic density of the material, which mostly only hosts other \W nuclear spins which posses a low gyromagnetic factor.

In Chapter \ref{ch:individual_nuclear_spins_quantum_information} we focus on a system where an \Er spin is coupled to two neighboring \W nuclear spins, named Qb1 and Qb2. By exploiting the AC-Zeeman shifts induced by detuned Raman pulses, we develop a protocol for implementing a two-qubit gate. The Raman detuning $\Delta$ determines the magnitude of the AC-Zeeman shifts and gives rise to two distinct operating regimes. \reffig{ch2}d shows the two: on the left an unconditional regime, where the transition frequency of Qb2 is independent of the state of Qb1, and on the right a conditional regime, where this frequency depends on the state of Qb1.

Entangled spin pairs in the solid state have been shown to mitigate correlated sources of noise~\cite{bartling_entanglement_2022}. We demonstrate this effect with \W nuclear spins by creating $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}+\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\right)$ Bell state (see tomography in see \reffig{ch2}e) and measuring the coherence time. We observe an increase of a factor two with respect to the single qubit coherence, which can be attributed to the magnetic noise mitigation of the decoherence-free-subspace.

Entanglement provides a powerful means to suppress correlated noise in solid-state spin systems~\cite{bartling_entanglement_2022}. Using \W nuclear spins, we prepare the Bell state $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}+\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\right)$, verified by state tomography (Fig.~\reffig{ch2}e), and measure its coherence time. We find a twofold increase relative to the single-qubit coherence time, indicating effective magnetic noise suppression within a decoherence-free subspace.


\section{Precision spectroscopy on a 9/2 nuclear spin}

In the fourth part of this work, we study an initially unknown nuclear spin species. Using the methods developed in the previous chapters, we are able to chemically and structurally identify it as a \Nb ion. We leverage the long coherence of the nuclear spin to measure its hyperfine spectrum with hertz resolution. By fitting the spectrum to a Hamiltonian model we identify a new type of interaction between electron and nuclear spins. Finally, thanks to the precision of our data, we are able to set an order of magnitude tighter bound on the hexadecapolar coupling of \Nb nuclear spins with respect to previous state-of-the-art measurements \sidecite{liao_nuclear_1994}.
