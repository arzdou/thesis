% Motivation v2

Magnetic resonance is a spectroscopic technique that uses the coupling between spins and electromagnetic radiation to probe microscopic properties of matter. The field is broadly divided into Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR)~\sidecite{abragam_principles_1986} and Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR)~\sidecite{slichter_principles_1996} depending on the type of spin being studied, either unpaired electron spins or nuclear spins respectively. Owing to the ubiquity of spins in matter, magnetic resonance techniques have a wide range of applications ranging from medical imaging~\sidecite{lauterbur_image_1973} to materials science~\sidecite{haworth_solidstate_2022}, biology~\sidecite{wuthrich_protein_1989} and pharmaceutical quality control~\sidecite{holzgrabe_quantitative_2010}.

An important limitation of conventional magnetic resonance techniques is their low sensitivity. Because the interaction between spins and electromagnetic fields is weak, large ensembles of spins are required to generate a detectable signal. Consequently, most measurements yield ensemble-averaged quantities, while microscopic and local variations are difficult to access. 

By using quantum limited amplifiers and superconducting resonators, the sensitivity has been pushed to the extreme, sufficient to detect ten electron spins~\sidecite{ranjan_electron_2020}. Single-spin detection has been achieved in a small number of solid state systems, such as NV centers in diamond~\sidecite{jelezko_single_2002} and donor impurities in silicon~\sidecite{morello_singleshot_2010}.  This has been possible thanks to system-specific mechanisms, including spin-selective optical transitions and spin-to-charge conversion. The results in these systems demonstrate how individual electron and nuclear spins in the solid state can be used for quantum computing~\sidecite{bradley_tenqubit_2019,edlbauer_11qubit_2025} and precision spectroscopy~\sidecite{abobeih_atomicscale_2019,breitweiser_quadrupolar_2024}.

Recently, our group has demonstrated how individual paramagnetic impurities in a dielectric can be detected via microwave fluorescence of their electron spin transition at 10~mK~\sidecite{wang_singleelectron_2023}. This method is, in principle, applicable to a large number of paramagnetic species and substrates, as it only requires to enhance the spontaneous emmision rate of the electron spin beyond the non-radiative relaxation rate at cryogenic temperatures. \textbf{This thesis reports how microwave photon counting can be used to detect and coherently control individual nuclear spins coupled to a single electron spin.} Furthermore, we demonstrate precision spectroscopy of the NMR transitions and how nuclear spins can find applications in quantum computing. 

The thesis is divided into three parts. The first part of the manuscript describes the physical system and the experimental setup. We introduce the spin Hamiltonian for \Er electron spins in \Ca, the nuclear spins present in the crystal and their interaction. We describe how coupling the \Er spin to a resonator enhances the spontaneous relaxation rate through the Purcell effect and the detection device: the single microwave photon detector. In addition, we describe the microwave techniques used to control the nuclear spin state. The second part of this work focuses on the detection and control of \W nuclear spins. We measure quantum jumps of the nuclear spin state and demonstrate hyperfine spectroscopy and deterministic preparation using only microwave pulses. In addition, we use microwave stimulated Raman driving to demonstrate single- and two-qubit gates in a two-qubit register of \W nuclear spins. We also measure coherence times on the order of one second, which are among the longest measured in the solid state. In the third part of the thesis we address an \Nb impurity with nuclear spin-9/2. We generalize the techniques presented above and perform precision spectroscopy of the nuclear spin transitions.

Conventional pulsed EPR spectrometers measure the transverse magnetization of electron spin ensembles, which cooperatively radiates a coherent field detected by a proximal antenna. Efforts to increase the sensitivity of EPR spectroscopy for the detection of small samples have led to the design of detection resonators with small mode volume, which is conveniently achieved using planar metallic resonators. Normal-metal electrodes lead to quality factors in the 10--100 regime~\sidecite{narkowicz_scaling_2008,dayan_advanced_2018}, while higher quality factors and smaller mode volumes can be reached using superconducting resonators \sidecite{wallace_microstrip_1991}. Our group has pushed these efforts to the extreme limit by cooling down the sample to millikelvin temperatures and using microwave superconducting amplifiers that reach the quantum limit of sensitivity \sidecite{bienfait_reaching_2016}, achieving sensitivity sufficient to detect 10 spins in 1 second of integration time \sidecite{ranjan_electron_2020}. Ultimately, the remaining noise is entirely due to vacuum fluctuations of the microwave field. While the latter can be slightly reduced using microwave squeezed states \sidecite{bienfait_magnetic_2017}, this was not sufficient to reach single-spin sensitivity.

A new detection method for EPR spectroscopy that overcomes these limitations was recently demonstrated in our group, shown in \reffig{principle}a. The method is based on detecting the microwave fluorescence of the electron spins when they relax to their ground state, similar to photon-counting techniques in the optical regime~\sidecite{albertinale_detecting_2021}. To measure the fluorescence we use a Single Microwave Photon Detector (SMPD), a superconducting device that maps an incoming photon to the state of a transmon qubit~\sidecite{lescanne_irreversible_2020}. By detecting the photon energy instead of its quadrature, the technique is insensitive to vacuum fluctuations. However, detecting electron spins through their microwave fluorescence has to overcome a major obstacle: the spontaneous emission rate of a spin in free space is vanishingly low ($10^{-12}~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$), and in the solid state, non-radiative decay via phonon interaction is the dominant mechanism.

By inductively coupling the electron spins to a low-mode-volume, high-quality resonator, the radiative relaxation rate can be enhanced through the Purcell effect~\sidecite{purcell_spontaneous_1946}. Superconducting resonators have demonstrated Purcell factors on the order of $10^{14}$, reaching radiative relaxation rates on the order of $10^{3}~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. This extreme enhancement is possible thanks to the superconducting nature of the devices, which greatly suppresses internal losses, and by focusing the magnetic field into a small region through inductive lumped elements such as nanowires~\sidecite{wang_singleelectron_2023}.

In addition, two problems can be addressed when operating at cryogenic temperatures. First, microwave blackbody radiation is exponentially suppressed, which results in a reduced number of thermal photons at the SMPD input, thereby enhancing the detection SNR. Second, the phonon bath is frozen and the non-radiative relaxation rate is reduced. Under these conditions, it was possible to demonstrate single-spin detection via microwave photon counting~\sidecite{wang_addressing_2023, balembois_magnetic_2023, pallegoix_high_2025}

The measurement scheme is depicted in \reffig{principle}a. An electron spin is coupled to a resonant cavity (both in red). The cavity is coupled to two ports, input and output. A microwave excitation (red Gaussian) brings the electron spin to the excited state. The spin rapidly relaxes through the Purcell effect and emits an incoherent photon into the output port, which is routed toward the SMPD, where it is detected. The electron spin is also coupled to the nuclear spins in the environment (in orange) via hyperfine interaction. Therefore, the state of the nuclear spin influences the electron spin frequency, and resolving these shifts would permit single nuclear spin detection.

Electron spins in solids have different Larmor frequencies due to variations in their electrostatic or magnetic local environment. As a result, the spectral width of the spin ensemble is much larger than the width of its individual spin components (see \reffig{principle}b). This so-called inhomogeneous broadening poses limitations to the spectral resolution in EPR spectroscopy, and is one of the motivations for single-spin EPR. In the experiments reported in this thesis, inhomogeneous broadening helps us to address individual spins which are found at the edges of the inhomogeneous distribution, as shown in \reffig{principle}b.

To perform our experiments we use the spin degree of freedom of an \Er ion in \Ca. The first unit cell of the crystal is shown in \reffig{crystal_structure_ch1} with the \Er ion in the center. These ions are paramagnetic impurities in the crystal lattice with an unpaired electron spin. They can be effectively treated as an electron spin $S=1/2$ with frequency $\omega_S$ with an anisotropic gyromagnetic factor. The host material is chosen due to the low nuclear magnetic moment density, which enables long coherence times for the electron spin~\sidecite{ledantec_twentythreemillisecond_2021}. A single-spin-resolved rotation pattern is shown in \reffig{principle}c. The figure shows the measured number of counts $\langle C \rangle$ after microwave excitation as a function of the magnetic field amplitude and angle. An increase in $\langle C \rangle$ indicates the presence of one or more \Er spins. Each parabola corresponds to a single \Er spin with a different gyromagnetic factor and can be followed independently as a function of $\theta$. In this manuscript we investigate the nuclear spin environment of these spins and probe their interactions with neighboring nuclear spins.

%Energy level structures of electron spins in the solid state are rarely as simple as a free electron in free space. In particular paramagnetic impurities in dielectrics tend to have atom-like energy structures, as the electron is generally bound to the impurity by its electrostatic potential. As such, certain electron spin-like systems possess an optical transition whose frequency depends on the spin-state. Consequently, monitoring the spin-dependent fluorescence allows for discrimination of the electron spin. The most representative example of these systems are Nitrogen-Vacancy (NV) centers in diamond, where a nitrogen impurity and carbon vacancy combine and create a defect state within the band-gap of diamond. In its negatively charged state, a spin $S=1$ is bound to the impurity. The spin-dependent optical transitions of the electron spin can be resolved due to the long coherence time of the spin and single-shot readout of the electron spin state was demonstrated in 2004 by Fiodor Jelezko~\sidecite{jelezko_observation_2004}. Using the NV center as a local antenna, proximal $^{13}\mathrm C$ nuclear spins can be detected through their hyperfine interaction~\sidecite{neumann_singleshot_2010}.

The current through a SET is non-zero only for specific values of its electrochemical potential and the tunneling of a single electron spin into the device is enough to switch the current. In the second part of the manuscript, we report the main experimental results obtained in this thesis. In Chapter \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w}, we demonstrate methods to read out, measure the spectrum of, and polarize nuclear spins in close proximity to the electron spins. We leverage the single-electron spin sensitivity and the long coherence of \Er spins to detect proximal nuclear spins. The nuclear bath in \Ca is dominated by \W nuclear spins with $\sim14$\% abundance. Consequently, each \Er spin might have a different environment: some will have no neighboring\sidenote{We use neighboring to refer to lattice sites in the first unit cell of the crystal, centered around the impurity} \W spins, while others might have several. As shown in \reffig{ch1}a, when an \Er spin is coupled to a \W nuclear spin, the hyperfine coupling splits the electron spin transition frequencies depending on the state of the \W nuclear spin, $\omega_S^\uparrow$ and $\omega_S^\downarrow$.

In Chapter \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w} we probe the nuclear spin environment through high-resolution EPR spectroscopy at a fixed magnetic field where a single \Er is resonant with the cavity. We measure the number of counts after a microwave pulse as a function of pulse frequency and time (see \reffig{ch1}b). For some \Er spins, the frequency displays telegraphic behavior, which we associate with quantum jumps in the state of a neighboring \W nuclear spin in real time.

By leveraging the spectral separation of the electron spin transition we obtain single-shot quantum-non-demolition readout of the nuclear spin state. The redout protocol applies two interleaved microwave pulses at each of the EPR-allowed transitions and collects the fluorescence counts after each pulse. The histogram of the count difference shows two symmetric and well resolved peaks (see \reffig{ch1}c) which enables single-shot readout of the nuclear spin state. 

The zero- and double-quantum transitions (represented in \reffig{ch1}a with dashed arrows) are weakly allowed due to the anisotropic term in the hyperfine interaction and simultaneously change the state of the electron and nuclear spin. In ensemble EPR, these weakly-allowed transitions are exploited electron-electron double resonance (ELDOR) detected NMR (EDNMR) measurements to probe the hyperfine spectra~\sidecite{schosseler_pulsed_1994}. Here we demonstrate single spin EDNMR by driving the ZQ/DQ transitions on a single \Er spin. We use the resulting spectra (shown in \reffig{ch1}d) to identify the coupled nuclear spin as a \W.

As shown in  \reffig{ch1}e, repeatedly pulsing the ZQ (DQ) transition allows one to polarize the nuclear spin to $\ket{\downarrow}$ ($\ket{\uparrow}$).

A key requirement for single-spin spectroscopy and quantum computing is deterministic preparation of individual nuclear spins in an arbitrary state. We achieve deterministic single-spin polarization to $\ket{\downarrow}$ ($\ket{\uparrow}$) by exciting the ZQ (DQ) transitions and waiting for the electron spin to relax. After several iterations, the nuclear spin should always end up in the desired state, as evidenced by the level diagram in \reffig{ch1}a. As shown in \reffig{ch1}e, after the application of a few ZQ $\pi$-pulses resonant with the ZQ transition, we observe that the \W nuclear spin is deterministically prepared in $\ket{\downarrow}$. This polarization scheme is the single-spin version of a method invented by Abragam and Proctor for ensembles of spins and called solid-effect dynamical nuclear polarization (DNP)~\sidecite{abragama.proctow._nouvelle_1958}.

\section{A register of two nuclear spin qubits}



In Chapter \ref{ch:individual_nuclear_spins_quantum_information}, we harness the readout and polarization methods developed in Chapter \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w} to demonstrate the operation of a two-nuclear-spin-qubit register. Nuclear spins in magnetically silent crystals have demonstrated coherence times ranging from seconds to hours~\sidecite{saeedi_roomtemperature_2013, muhonen_storing_2014, zhong_optically_2015} and, as such, are interesting qubit candidates. Different architectures for quantum computing using electron and nuclear spins in the solid state have been proposed~\cite{dutt_quantum_2007b,awschalom_quantum_2018,zwanenburg_silicon_2013}. The ability to detect and coherently control the state of nuclear spins with high fidelity in a quantum register has been demonstrated in a series of systems~\sidecite{pla_highfidelity_2013, song_entanglement_2026}. Nuclear spin driving is usually done by application of resonant radiofrequency (rf) pulses~\sidecite{vandersar_decoherenceprotected_2012, bradley_tenqubit_2019}. However, the \Er spins in our setup are addressed by a microwave resonator, capacitively coupled to the microwave lines, and therefore all rf pulses are filtered.

Due to these limitations we demonstrate in this thesis a novel method to drive the nuclear spins with only microwaves. The principle of this technique is shown in \reffig{ch2}a. Two detuned microwave pulses with amplitudes $\Omega_\text{A}$ and $\Omega_\text{B}$ are applied to the system. The pulses are detuned from each other by $\delta$, and pulse A is detuned from the EPR-allowed transition when the nuclear spin is in $\ket{\downarrow}$ by $\Delta$. As the pulses are detuned from the EPR transition, no significant population is driven to the electron spin excited state. However, a second-order process can drive the nuclear spin state if $\delta$ is resonant with the nuclear spin frequency $\sim\omega_I$. Since the pulses do not significantly populate the electron spin excited levels, the process preserves the coherence properties of the nuclear spins. This is demonstrated in the NMR spectroscopy of \reffig{ch2}b, which plots the nuclear spin $\downarrow$ population as a function of the pulse frequency. The linewidth of the transition ($\sim200~\mathrm{Hz}$) is much smaller than the electron linewidth, as expected for a Raman transition. We measure the coherence time of the nuclear spin through a Ramsey sequence and find $T_2^*=0.5$--$1$~s, as shown in \reffig{ch2}c. This result confirms that \Ca is a good host for nuclear spin qubits, as already shown for electron spins~\sidecite{ledantec_twentythreemillisecond_2021}.

In Chapter \ref{ch:individual_nuclear_spins_quantum_information} we focus on a system where an \Er spin is coupled to two neighboring \W nuclear spins, named Qb1 and Qb2. By exploiting the AC-Zeeman shifts induced by detuned Raman pulses, we develop a protocol for implementing a two-qubit gate. The Raman detuning $\Delta$ determines the magnitude of the AC-Zeeman shifts and gives rise to two distinct operating regimes. \reffig{ch2}d shows the two: on the left an unconditional regime, where the transition frequency of Qb2 is independent of the state of Qb1, and on the right a conditional regime, where this frequency depends on the state of Qb1.

Magnetic noise that is correlated between the two spins can be suppressed by preparing the system in specific singlet or triplet states with zero-average magnetic moment~\cite{bartling_entanglement_2022, bradley_tenqubit_2019}. Using \W nuclear spins, we prepare the Bell state $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}+\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\right)$, verified by state tomography (Fig.~\reffig{ch2}e), and measure its coherence time. We find a twofold increase relative to the single-qubit coherence time, indicating effective magnetic-noise suppression within a decoherence-free subspace.

\begin{figure*}[t!]

Another application of the methods developed in Chapter \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w} is to characterize an unknown atom near an electron spin. This is the topic of Chapter \ref{ch:nb_nuclear_spin}, where we fully characterize an unknown nuclear spin-9/2 impurity near an \Er spin. We moreover perform precision hyperfine spectroscopy of this \Nb atom and find previously unknown terms in the spin Hamiltonian.

\reffig{ch3}a represents the twenty energy levels of the \Nb--\Er coupled system as well as all EPR transitions, with frequencies $\omega_n$, and NMR-allowed transitions, split into two manifolds depending on the state $m_S$ of the \Er spin and with frequencies $\omega_{n,n+1}^{(m_S)}$. We initially characterize the system through EPR spectroscopy by measuring similar time traces as in Chapter \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w}. \reffig{ch3}b shows the ten different EPR-allowed transitions of the electron spin equally spaced from each other, characteristic of an electron spin coupled to a nuclear spin $I=9/2$.

We use the methods developed in the previous chapters (single-shot readout, solid-effect DNP, and stimulated Raman driving) to measure the NMR spectrum of the \Nb spin. By comparing the Larmor frequency of the impurity with the gyromagnetic ratios of nuclear spins, we identify it as a \Nb spin. We leverage the long coherence of the nuclear spin to measure its hyperfine spectrum with hertz resolution, shown in \reffig{ch3}c as a function of the transition index. The large splitting of the levels indicates that the quadrupolar interaction strength of the nuclear spin is of similar magnitude to the Zeeman interaction strength.

We fit the NMR spectrum to a Hamiltonian model in both \Er manifolds with hertz accuracy, as demonstrated by the residuals of the fit (shown in \reffig{ch3}c). We use these results to reconstruct the \Nb quadrupole and compare it with DFT simulations. Both the quadrupole reconstruction and the DFT result are shown in \reffig{ch3_extra}. We find quantitative agreement between the two, both in magnitude and angular orientation, indicating correct site assignment.

In addition, we observe that the quadrupole interaction strength is different between the two manifolds, as shown in \reffig{ch3}d through the statistically significant difference between the posterior distributions for the quadrupolar interaction strength between the two fits. We assign this difference to a new type of interaction between electron and nuclear spins. We theorize that the origin of the interaction lies on the coupling between the magnetically-induced electric dipole of the \Er spin and the nuclear quadrupole interaction~\sidecite{bloembergen_linear_1961,mims_electric_1964}.

Finally, we are able to set bounds on the hexadecapolar interaction that are an order of magnitude tighter than previous state-of-the-art measurements~\sidecite{liao_nuclear_1994}, as shown in \reffig{ch3}e.
