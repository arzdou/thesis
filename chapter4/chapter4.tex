\chapter{Individual nuclear spins in the solid state for quantum information}
\label{ch:individual_nuclear_spins_quantum_information}

\begin{quote}
\itshape
In this chapter we focus on a single spin system, formed by two \W nuclear spins coupled to a single \Er impurity. Using the techniques demonstrated in the previous chapter of single-shot nuclear readout and DNP, we expand on the control of the spins by driving stimulated Raman transitions, which can be used to define single- and two-qubit gates. Using these techniques, we further show that certain Bell states exist in a so-called decoherence free subspace and are inherently resilient to magnetic field noise, extending by a factor two the coherence of the two-qubit protected state with respect to the individual qubits.
\end{quote}

%Nuclear spins in the solid-state are attractive candidates for quantum computing, offering long coherence times~\cite{steger_quantum_2012,pla_highfidelity_2013a, zhong_optically_2015, maurer_roomtemperature_2012a} and high density. However, the typically weak coupling of nuclear spins to their environment that enables such long coherence times makes their control and readout challenging. Single-shot QND readout and control of the electron spin of rare earth ion defects in the solid state via their optical transitions using nanophotonic cavities has been demonstrated\cite{raha_optical_2020a, kindem_control_2020a}. Furthermore, a frequency-erasing photon detection scheme was recently used to herald remote entanglement between up to three such ions\cite{ruskuc_scalable_2024}. However, nuclear spin qubits are usually addressed through their hyperfine coupling to an electron spin ancilla~\cite{pla_coherent_2014a, madzik_precision_2022, abobeih_onesecond_2018a, waldherr_quantum_2014}, which then needs to be detected. Electrical detection of electron spin donors in silicon enabled the demonstration of long-lived single-$^{31}$P nuclear spin qubits in isotopically-enriched silicon~\cite{muhonen_storing_2014a}, the operation of a two-$^{31}$P quantum gate~\cite{madzik_precision_2022}, and the control~\cite{fernandezdefuentes_navigating_2024} and preparation of a $^{123}$Sb $7/2$-nuclear spin in a Schr\"odinger-cat state~\cite{yu_creation_2024}. Electrical detection was used also for TbPc$_2$ molecular quantum dots, enabling the manipulation and readout of a spin-3/2 $^{159}$Tb nuclear spin~\cite{thiele_electrically_2014a} and the implementation of Grover's search algorithm on a single $^{159}$Tb nucleus~\cite{godfrin_operating_2017}. Optical detection of individual NV center electron spins in diamond enabled the operation of a register of up to ten $^{13}$C nuclear spin qubits~\cite{bradley_tenqubit_2019}, the fault-tolerant operation of a logical qubit utilizing five $^{13}$C nuclear-spin qubits~\cite{abobeih_faulttolerant_2022b}, and the demonstration of high-fidelity single- and two-qubit gates with a $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ nuclear spin~\cite{bartling_universal_2024}. Individual nuclear spin control has also been demonstrated using other color centres in diamond~\cite{beukers_control_2025, maity_mechanical_2022, nguyen_quantum_2019}, rare earth ions~\cite{uysal_coherent_2023a} and SiC~\cite{babin_fabrication_2022}.

%In this chapter, we present an alternative hybrid architecture to control and read out individual nuclear spin qubits via an electron spin defect ancilla by magnetically coupling this ancilla to a high-Q superconducting resonator. Requiring relatively straightforward fabrication techniques, this approach permits a single resonator to control numerous coherent spin defects. The techniques used are also readily applicable to a wide range of paramagnetic defects with nearby nuclear spins. By detecting spin fluorescence~\cite{albertinale_detecting_2021} via a superconducting resonator using a superconducting circuit-based Single Microwave Photon Detector (SMPD)~\cite{balembois_cyclically_2024,lescanne_irreversible_2020}, detection of individual electron spins has recently been demonstrated \cite{wang_singleelectron_2023}. Leveraging this technique, we use single Er$^{3+}$ spin impurities in a CaWO$_4$ crystal to control and read-out neighboring spin-1/2 $^{183}$W nuclei via the hyperfine interaction. This method permits single-shot Quantum Non-Demolition (QND) readout of individual $^{183}$W nuclear spin qubits~\cite{travesedo_allmicrowave_2025}. Individual all-microwave qubit control on a timescale of milliseconds is achieved by driving a stimulated Raman transition. Two-qubit gates are achieved by taking advantage of induced AC-Zeeman shifts on the energy level structure of the electron and nuclear spin system. We use this to construct a NOT gate and generate Bell states of the two nuclear spin qubits. We further show that certain Bell states exist in a so-called decoherence free subspace and are inherently resilient to magnetic field noise, extending $T_2^*$ from $0.8(2)~$s and $1.2(3)~$s for the individual qubits to $1.7(2)~$s for the two-qubit protected state.

The position of the \W spins was estimated in the previous chapter by matching the measured hyperfine coupling between the electron and the nuclear spins to dipole-dipole simulations, and a schematic view of the system is shown in \reffig{crystal_2_W}. 

The spin Hamiltonian of this system is

\begin{equation}
\hat{H} = \omega_S \hat{S}_{z} + \sum_{i\in\{1,2\}} \omega_{I,i} \hat{I}_{z,1} + A_\parallel^{(i)} \hat{S}_z \hat{I}_{z,i} + A_\perp^{(i)} \hat{S}_z \hat{I}_{x,i},
\end{equation}

\section{Readout and preparation}

We extend the readout and preparation protocols from the previous chapter to the two nuclear spin Er$_4$ system. Preparation into arbitrary nuclear spin states is done through a combination of sideband pumping (see \refsec{dnp_half}) and single-qubit gates (see next section). Sideband pumping is achieved by driving the electron-nuclear transitions (represented as gray arrows in \reffig{8_level_system}) with a $\tau_{\mathrm{prep}}=3.5$~ms length square pulse followed by $\tau_\text{w}$ = 2~ms of wait time, allowing the electron spin to decay. This sequence is then repeated $N_\text{prep}=50$ times to maximize the preparation efficiency. To prepare $\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}$ we drive the high frequency sidebands. To cover both Qb1 and Qb2 sidebands, the frequency of the pulse is chirped 50 kHz. To prepare other states, we apply the appropriate single-qubit gates after initializing in $\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}$.

    \includegraphics{chapter4/figures/spectra_electron.pdf}

Each trace was obtained by preparing each nuclear spin state deterministically and measuring the ensemble averaged counts $\langle C\rangle$ number of counts as a function of the frequency of the excitation pulse of $80$~\textmu s of duration. The data was taken with an integration time of $\tau_\text{int}=1$~ms}
    \labfig{spectra_electron}

Readout exploits the spectral separation of the electron allowed transitions $\omega_{\uparrow\uparrow}$, $\omega_{\uparrow\downarrow}$, $\omega_{\downarrow\uparrow}$ and $\omega_{\downarrow\downarrow}$ (blue, orange, green and red in \reffig{8_level_system}). The linewidth of the resonator $\kappa \approx 700$~kHz and the bandwidth of the SMPD $\sim200$~kHz are sufficient to span the four possible frequencies and the measured spin coherence $T_2^*=170$~\textmu s, allows us to frequency-resolve them. \reffig{spectra_electron} shows four photon-counting spectra obtained by high-resolution spectroscopy with an $80$~\textmu s electron excitation pulse. Each trace was acquired after deterministically preparing the nuclear spins in one of the four possible nuclear spin configurations.  We see that the peak photon count occurs at a different frequency for each of the four nuclear spin states, and that the peaks are all clearly distinguishable, allowing for high fidelity readout of the nuclear spin state.

Each point corresponds to the value of $\Delta_x$ and $\Delta_y$ measured after preparing in a specific state.     \labfig{kmeans}

To measure the state of the nuclear spin we use the photon counting statistics obtained after sending a $\pi$-pulse at each of the four resonant frequencies. We define $C_{\uparrow \uparrow}$, $C_{\downarrow \uparrow}$, $C_{\uparrow \downarrow}$ and $C_{\downarrow \downarrow}$ as the accumulated number of counts after applying a $\pi$-pulse with frequency matching each of the electron spin transitions respectively. We then introduce 

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
   \Delta_x &= (C_{\uparrow \uparrow} + C_{\uparrow \downarrow}) - (C_{\downarrow \uparrow} + C_{\downarrow \downarrow}), \\
   \Delta_y &= (C_{\uparrow \uparrow} + C_{\downarrow \uparrow}) - (C_{\uparrow \downarrow} + C_{\downarrow \downarrow}).
\end{split} 
\end{equation}

$\Delta_x$ ($\Delta_y$) effectively "traces out" the state of Qb2 (Qb1) as well as cancelling the contribution of the dark counts to the photon counting statistics. \reffig{kmeans} plots the measured $\Delta_y$ versus $\Delta_x$ for each possible state of the nuclear spin manifold with $N_\text{RO}=200$ readout pulses. The color of the points represents the state in which the system was prepared before the measurement took place. There are four well resolved distributions, each corresponding to a different prepared state of the two-nuclear-spin system. We use a $k$-means algorithm to separate the space corresponding to each distribution, displayed in the plot with the corresponding background color. The boundaries approximately correspond to the $x=0$ and $y=0$ curves as expected. The skewness in the boundaries can be attributed to inaccurate calibration of the driving frequencies $\omega_{\uparrow\uparrow}$, $\omega_{\downarrow\uparrow}$, $\omega_{\uparrow\downarrow}$ and $\omega_{\downarrow\downarrow}$, which would result in different counting statistics for each state. The measurement probability after preparation is of 0.91, 0.93, 0.92 and 0.95 to be in the desired state $\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}$, $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$, $\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$, respectively. As discussed in the previous chapter, the main limiting factor of this scheme is the non-zero probability for the electron spin to relax through a nuclear spin flipping transition, which is proportional to $\left(A_\perp / 2\omega_I \right)^2$ (see \red{section ref}). These cross-relaxation events limit the number of readout pulses that can be applied before the readout becomes non-QND.

\section{Stimulated Raman driving.}

A straightforward method to implement single-qubit gates on the qubits would be to directly drive the nuclear transitions in the radiofrequency regime. However, we are constrained by the experimental setup, since the electron spins are addressed via a resonator, filtering any excitation outside its bandwidth. Therefore, we require a different method to control the nuclear spins, which can be implemented with microwave pulses. As discussed in \refch{nuclea_spin_control}, the method of choice used for coherent control of the \W nuclear spins is stimulated Raman driving.

In \refsec{raman_driving} we introduce the principle of stimulated Raman driving, derived the analytical Rabi frequency and discussed the concept of the \textit{differential AC-Zeeman shift}. We now proceed to briefly remind the reader the concept of stimulated Raman driving in the case of a single qubit and introduce the subtleties that arise when a second \W qubit is strongly coupled to the \Er spin, from which we naturally implement two-qubit gates.

Let us first consider a single \W qubit coupled to the \Er spin. To control the state of the nuclear spin through stimulated Raman driving, we simultaneously apply two detuned microwave pulses with amplitudes $\Omega_\text{A}$ and $\Omega_\text{B}$. The frequency detuning between the two pulses is defined as $\delta$ and the frequency detuning between the drive A and the $\ket{\Downarrow \uparrow} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Uparrow \uparrow}$ is defined as $\Delta$, as depicted in \reffig{raman_scheme_v2}. A coherent transfer of population between the two lower nuclear spin levels is activated when $\delta = \omega_I^{(\Downarrow)} + \delta_\text{AC}$, where $\omega_I^{(\Downarrow)}$ is the bare NMR transition frequency and $\delta_\text{AC}$ corresponds to the differential AC-Zeeman shift due to the off-resonant driving of the microwave pulses.

    \includegraphics{chapter4/figures/raman_scheme.pdf}

We highlight the nuclear spin transition in the ground state of the \Er ground state with frequency $\omega_I^{(\Downarrow)}$. \textit{(right)} Two microwave pulses with amplitude $\Omega_\text{A, B}$ are applied on a four-level system. The detuning between drive A and the $\ket{\Downarrow \uparrow} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Uparrow \uparrow}$ is defined as $\Delta$. The frequency difference between the two pulses is defined as $\delta$. The bare energy levels (gray dashed lines) have been AC-Zeeman shifted by the strong off-resonance drives. The combined AC-Zeeman shift of the system results in an effective shift of the nuclear spin transition by $\delta_\text{AC}$}
    \labfig{raman_scheme_v2}
\end{figure}

We now return to the system with two \W nuclear spins. In the following, we concentrate on the control of Qb1, as the same methods apply to Qb2. We differentiate two cases, depending on the state of Qb2, highlighted in different colors in \reffig{2q_raman_scheme}a. The key point in the diagram comes from the different Raman shifts $\Delta^{\uparrow, \downarrow}$ depending on the state of Qb2. The origin of this difference lies on the split of the electron allowed frequencies and is evident that for the same Raman drive, $\Delta^{\uparrow} \approx \Delta^{\downarrow} + A_{\parallel}^{(2)}$.

Using Eq. \ref{eq:ac_zeeman} we can calculate the expected differential AC-Zeeman shift on the Qb1 transition for both possible states of Qb2 and equal driving amplitudes $\Omega_\text{A} = \Omega_\text{B}$ (see \reffig{2q_raman_scheme}b). Stimulated Raman driving is a second order process that requires $\Delta \gg \Omega_\text{Ram}$ and the figure only plots $\delta^{\downarrow, \uparrow}_\text{AC}$ where this condition is met. As expected, the two quantities are similar but not equal, and we naturally introduce the following quantities:

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \delta_{2qb} &\equiv \delta^\uparrow_\text{AC} - \delta^\downarrow_\text{AC}, \\
    \bar{\delta}_\text{AC} &\equiv (\delta^\uparrow_\text{AC} + \delta^\downarrow_\text{AC})/2,
\end{split}
\end{align}

\reffig{2q_raman_scheme}c shows $\delta_{2qb}$ as a function of $\Delta^\uparrow$. Based on the value of $\delta_{2qb}$ we can distinguish two regimes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unconditional driving.} In this regime the Qb1 transition frequency is effectively independent of Qb2. It is obtained for $\Delta^{\uparrow} \approx \Delta_u \equiv -\omega_{I,1}/2 - A_\parallel^{(2)}/2$, where the driving speed is also close to its maximum. Experimentally, this is realized when $|\delta_{2qb}| \ll \Omega_\text{Ram}$. For $\Omega_\text{Ram}\approx0.1\,$kHz we observe tolerance for $\Delta^{\uparrow}$ variations of order $\sim 10\,$kHz while remaining unconditional. \red{appendix with derivation?}
    \item \textbf{Conditional driving.} For all other values of $\Delta^{\uparrow}$, the transition frequency for Qb1 is conditioned on the state of Qb2.
\end{itemize}

These two regimes respectively define the single- and two-qubit gates. To implement a single-qubit gate, the frequency of the nuclear spin transition should not depend on the state any other qubit of the register, which is preserved in the unconditional regime. On the contrary, the conditional driving creates a native CNOT gate, as we discuss in the following sections.

\subsection{Single-qubit gates}

We now perform spectroscopy and characterization of the two \W nuclear spins via stimulated Raman driving in the unconditional regime. We first perform Raman spectroscopy using the unconditional driving condition $\Delta/2\pi = -408.8$~kHz~$\approx \Delta_u/2\pi$, and $\Omega_A = \Omega_B$. The nuclear spin register is prepared in the $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ state, after which a Raman pulse is applied whose detuning $\delta$ is swept, followed by nuclear spin readout. When the resonance condition is met, we observe a large change in the nuclear spin population to either the $\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}$ or the $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$ state (see \reffig{spectra_and_rabi}a). The resonance linewidth is of order $100$~Hz or less, two orders of magnitude narrower than the electron spin linewidth, which confirms that the Raman driving occurs through virtual transitions of the electron excited states. The $\sim 2$~kHz frequency separation between the two ground-state nuclear spin transitions is due to the different values of $A_\parallel,A_\perp$ and $\omega_I$ for each \W nuclear spin, due to their different position relative to the \Er ion. Knowing the required drive frequencies, we then prepare the $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ state and sweep the drive duration, giving rise to coherent Rabi oscillations of each qubit, as shown in \reffig{spectra_and_rabi}b. This allows us to measure the Rabi Raman rate $\Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}}^{(1)} / 2\pi = 121(1)$~Hz and $\Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}}^{(2)} / 2\pi = 87(1)$~Hz. for a specific microwave amplitude and calibrate the $\pi$-pulses for Qb1 and Qb2, respectively.

As detailed in section \refsec{raman_driving}, the frequency of the Rabi oscillations of a nuclear spin under a Raman drive is proportional to the hyperfine coupling $A_\perp$. Consequently, we can use the measured values of $\Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}}^{(1,2)}$ to estimate $A_\perp$. In the unconditional regime with both drives having the same microwave amplitude, this relation is readily expressed as 

\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}} \approx \frac{\Omega_A\Omega_B A_\perp}{\omega_I^2}
\end{equation}

From the data presented in \reffig{spectra_and_rabi}b we obtain $A_\perp^{(1)} / 2\pi = 71(3)$~kHz and $A_\perp^{(2)} / 2\pi = 12.8(6)$~kHz. We note that there is a difference between the measured value of $A_\perp^{(1)}$ compared to the measurement using time trace statistics presented in the previous chapter (where we obtain $\sim$40~kHz). This difference is readily explained by the nature of the time trace measurement, for which rapid changes in the nuclear spin state will be hidden by averaging artifacts. Therefore, the measurement of large values of $A_\perp$ will be biased towards a lower estimation if the SNR limit of the measurement is reached. We take the value measured by the Raman fit to be more precise, although limited by the precision in microwave amplitude.
