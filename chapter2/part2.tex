\section{Quantum circuits}
\labsec{quantum_circuits}

We consider a lumped element resonator \red{ref}, where the capacitance $C$ and the inductance $L$ can be considered as separate elements (see \reffig{resonator}). Classically, the energy of the system is given by the voltage $V$ that accumulates on the capacitor and the current $I$ that goes through the inductor 

\begin{equation}
    E = \frac{1}{2}LI^2 + \frac{1}{2}CV^2
\end{equation}

This results in a resonant frequency $\omega_r=1/\sqrt{LC}$ with an impedance of $Z_r = \sqrt(L/C)$, and the Hamiltonian of the classical system is 

\begin{equation}
    H_r =\frac{Q^2}{2C} + \frac{\phi^2}{2L}, 
\end{equation}

where $Q$ is the charge on the capacitor and $\phi$ is is the flux of the inductor\sidenote{The charge and flux are related to the voltage and current as 
\begin{align*}
    &I = -\frac{dQ}{dt} = -C\frac{dV}{dt}, \\
    &V = \frac{d\phi}{dt} = L\frac{dI}{dt}
\end{align*}
} the quantization of this Hamiltonian is performed by replacing with the charge and phase operators ($\hat{Q}$ and $\hat{\phi}$) which follow the following commutation relation: $[\hat{Q}, \hat{\phi}]=i\hbar$. By introducing the creation and anihilation operators ($\hat{a}$ and $\hat{a}^\dagger$) we can write the Hamiltonian of the resonator in second quantization


\begin{marginfigure}
    \includegraphics{chapter2/figures/resonator.pdf}
    \caption[Lumped element resonator]{Sketch of a lumped element resonator. The capacitor is defined by the capacitance to ground $C$ and the charge accumulated $Q$ while the inductor by its inductance to ground $L$ and the flux threading the loops $\phi$.}
    \labfig{resonator}
\end{marginfigure}



\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \cH{r} / \hbar = \omega_r(\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a} + 1/2), \\
    \hat{\phi} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar Z_r}{2}} (\hat{a}^\dagger + \hat{a}) = \delta \phi (\hat{a}^\dagger + \hat{a}), \\
    \hat{Q} = i \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2 Z_r}} (\hat{a}^\dagger - \hat{a}) = i \delta Q (\hat{a}^\dagger - \hat{a}),
\end{split}
\end{align}

\noindent and the eigenstates of the system are the Fock states $\ket{n}$ with associated energy $\cH{r}\ket{n} = \hbar \omega_r (n + 1/2)$. The potential as a function of the flux and the energy diagram are represented in \reffig{resonator_energy}. The Zero Point Fluctuations (ZPF) of the phase and the charge inside of the resonator ($\delta \hat{\phi}$ and $\delta \hat{Q}$) correspond the root mean square vacuum fluctuations of these quantities. Similarly, the electric and magnetic field generated by the resonator will be given by

\begin{marginfigure}
    \includegraphics{chapter2/figures/resonator_energy.pdf}
    \caption[Energy of a harmonic oscillator]{Energy diagram (black) and potential (green) of a harmonic oscillator with \red{$\phi_0=  h/2e$}. The levels are all separated by $\hbar\omega_r$}
    \labfig{resonator_energy}
\end{marginfigure}

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \hat{\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{r}) =   \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{B}_1(\mathbf{r}) \, (\hat{a}^\dagger + \hat{a}), \\
    \hat{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r}) = i \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) \, (\hat{a}^\dagger + \hat{a}). 
\end{split}
\end{align}

The electric and magnetic field have their associated ZPF ($\boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})$ and $\boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{B}_1(\mathbf{r})$). These quantities have a spatial distribution: the magnetic field ZPF will be located close to the inductor and the electric field ZPF will be stronger between the capacitor plates.


Throughout this work, the thermal energy of the system $k_B T$ is constantly lower than the energy of the resonator $\hbar \omega_r$ which ensures that the oscillator is at its vacuum state at thermal equilibrium. 

\section{Spin-resonator coupling}
\labsec{spin_coupled_to_resonator}

We now consider an individual \Er spin in the $S=1/2$ approximation, as described in \refsec{erbium_ions_in_ca}, coupled to a resonator. The Hamiltonian modeling the interaction between the two can be written as \sidecite{haroche_exploring_2006}:

%Since the \Er spins are intrinsic magnetic dipoles, they couple inductively to the resonator. Without loss of generality, 

\begin{align}
    \cH{r-s} / \hbar = \omega_r(\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a} + 1/2) +  \omega_S \hat{S}_z + \cH{int},
\end{align}

\noindent where $\omega_S = | \mathbf{B_0} \cdot \bar{\bar{\gamma}}_{\text{\Er}} | $ is the Larmor frequency of the spin, and we have arbitrarily chosen the $z$-axis as the orientation of $\mathbf{B}_0$. The spin couples inductively to the magnetic field generated by the resonator $\mathbf{B}_1$, and the interaction Hamiltonian $\cH{int}$ is given by

\begin{equation}
    \cH{int} / \hbar = \hat{\mathbf{B}}_1 \cdot \bar{\bar{\gamma}}_{\text{\Er}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{S}} = (\hat{a}^\dagger + \hat{a}) \, \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{B}_1 \cdot \bar{\bar{\gamma}}_{\text{\Er}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{S}},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{B}_1$ are the zero-point fluctuations of the magnetic field at the \Er spin's position. The Hamiltonian can be re-formulated in terms of the raising and lowering operators \sidenote{Which are defined as
\begin{align*}
    \hat{S}_+ = \hbar \ket{\Uparrow}\bra{\Downarrow}, \\ 
    \hat{S}_- = \hbar \ket{\Downarrow}\bra{\Uparrow}.
\end{align*}
},

\begin{equation}
    \labeq{res_spin_inter}
    \cH{int} / \hbar = (\hat{a}^\dagger + \hat{a}) \, (g_0 \hat{S}_+ + g_0 \hat{S}_- + \alpha_\Downarrow \ket{\Downarrow}\bra{\Downarrow} + \alpha_\Uparrow \ket{\Uparrow}\bra{\Uparrow}), 
\end{equation}

\noindent where $g_0$ is the coupling strength between the resonator and the spin and $\alpha_\Uparrow$ and $\alpha_\Uparrow$ are re-normalization of the energy levels of the electron spin states, given by

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    g_0 =&\, \bra{\Uparrow} \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{B}_1 \cdot \bar{\bar{\gamma}}_{\text{\Er}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{S}} \ket{\Downarrow}, \\ 
    \alpha_\Uparrow =&\, \bra{\Uparrow} \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{B}_1 \cdot \bar{\bar{\gamma}}_{\text{\Er}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{S}} \ket{\Uparrow}, \\ 
    \alpha_\Downarrow =&\, \bra{\Downarrow} \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{B}_1 \cdot \bar{\bar{\gamma}}_{\text{\Er}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{S}} \ket{\Downarrow}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:coupling_strength_og}
\end{align}

Therefore, the $g_0$ is depends on the orientation of $\mathbf{B_1}$, and is maximal when perpendicular to $\mathbf{B_0}$. The Hamiltonian takes a more intuitive form in the rotating frame of the resonator and the spin~\sidecite{haroche_exploring_2006}:

%\end{align}

%\noindent and the Hamiltonian will be transformed as

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
%    \cH{r-s}' / \hbar &= \hat{U}(t) \cH{r-s}/ \hbar \hat{U}^\dagger(t) + \dot{\hat{U}}(t)^\dagger  \hat{U}^\dagger(t) = \hat{U}(t) \cH{int}/ \hbar \hat{U}^\dagger(t) = \\
%    &= g_{0} \Big( \hat{a}\hat{S}_{+} e^{i(\omega_{s}-\omega_{r})t} + \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{-} e^{-i(\omega_{s}-\omega_r)t} \Big) \\
%    &\quad + g_{0} \Big(\hat{a}\hat{S}_{-} e^{-i(\omega_{s}+\omega_{r})t} + \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{+} e^{i(\omega_{s}+\omega_r)t} \Big) \\
%    &\quad + \hbar \big( \hat{a} e^{-i\omega_{r}t} + \hat{a}^{\dagger} e^{i\omega_rt} \big) 
%    \big( \alpha_{g}\ket{\Uparrow} \ket{\Downarrow} + \alpha_{e} \ket{\Downarrow} \bra{\Uparrow} \big) \\
    \cH{int}/\hbar \approx g_{0} \Big( \hat{a}\hat{S}_{+} e^{i(\omega_{s}-\omega_r)t} 
    + \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{-} e^{-i(\omega_{s}-\omega_r)t} \Big).
\end{split}
\end{align}

Where we have considered that the spin and the resonator are close to resonance $(\omega_{s}-\omega_{r}) \ll \omega_{s}, \omega_r$, and we have taken the rotating-wave approximation. \sidenote{For the rotating-wave approximation to be valid, the interaction strength must also be small compared to the energies of the resonator and the spin \[g_0 \ll (\omega_{s}+\omega_{r})\]} In the laboratory frame, the resulting Hamiltonian is given by

\begin{equation}
    \cH{JC} / \hbar = \hbar\omega_r(\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a} + 1/2) +  \omega_S \hat{S}_z + g_{0} \Big( \hat{a}\hat{S}_{+} + \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{-} \Big), \\
\end{equation}

\noindent also known as the Jaynes-Cummings Hamiltonian \sidecite{jaynes_comparison_1963}, which can be used to accurately describe the interaction of a two-level system (be it an atom, a transmon or an electron spin) with a single electromagnetic mode of light. The effect of the interaction term is the coherent exchange of photons between the spin and the resonator.

\subsection*{Coupling strength}

As discussed in \refsec{sample_details}, the resonator takes the form of two superconducting electrodes shunted by a nanowire. As shown in Eq. \ref{eq:coupling_strength_og} $g_0$ is proportional to the gyromagnetic factor of the \Er along the direction of $B_1$. Given that the gyromagnetic factor in the $(a, b)$-plane is $\sim$7 times larger compared to the $c$-axis\sidenote{See Eq. \ref{eq:gyromagnetic_tensor}}, the inductive element of the resonator is designed as a nanowire parallel to the $c$-axis. For this geometry, the magnetic field of the nanowire is confined in the $(a, b)$-plane. 

The ZPF of the magnetic field generated by the nanowire can be estimated using the Biot-Savart law,

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{B}_1(r) &= \frac{\mu_0}{2 \pi r} \delta I \mathbf{u}_\theta, \\
    \delta I = \frac{\delta \phi}{L} &= \omega_r \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2Z_r}}.
\end{split}
\end{align}

\noindent where $\delta I$ are the ZPF of the current and $\mathbf{u}_\theta$ is a unit vector confined to the $(a, b)$-plane. In the experiments, the $\mathbf{B}_0$ is applied approximately parallel to the $c$-axis. From Eq. \ref{eq:coupling_strength_og} we have that the coupling strength is then given by

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    g_0 = \frac{\gamma_\perp \mu_0}{2 \pi r} \delta I \bra{\Uparrow}  \mathbf{u}_\theta \cdot \hat{\mathbf{S}} \ket{\Downarrow} = \frac{\gamma_\perp \mu_0}{4 \pi r} \omega_r \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2Z_r}}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:coupling_strength_nanowire}
\end{align}

From this equation we conclude the following. First, the closer the spin is to the resonator, the stronger it will be coupled. Second, given that the coupling is inversely proportional to the impedance of the resonator, the design should maximize the capacitance and minimize the inductance, while maintaining the frequency constant. These considerations are addressed in \refch{sample_and_setup} where we described the resonator design and characterization.

\subsection{Driving the \Er spin}
\labsec{rabi_oscillations_theory}

In this section, we derive how a coherent drive on the resonator induces Rabi oscillations of the spin which naturally reveals the role of the cavity as a Lorentzian filter. We begin by considering the Jaynes-Cummings Hamiltonian, where the resonator is under the effect of a drive with complex amplitude $\epsilon_d$ and frequency $\omega_d$

\begin{equation}
    \cH{JC} / \hbar = \omega_r\,\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a} +  \frac{\omega_S}{2} \hat{S}_z + g_{0} ( \hat{a}\hat{S}_{+} + \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{-} ) + (\epsilon_d e^{-i\omega_d t} \hat{a}^\dagger + \epsilon_d^* e^{i\omega_d t} \hat{a}),
\end{equation}

where we have dropped the constant $1/2$ factor from the resonator energy. In the rotating frame of the drive\sidenote{The unitary of the transformation is given by \[\hat{U}(t) = \exp{i\omega_d t \left(\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a} +  \frac{1}{2} \hat{\sigma}_z \right)} \]} and applying the rotating wave approximation, the Hamiltonian becomes time independent and can be written as

\begin{equation}
    \cH{JC} / \hbar = \Delta_r\,\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a} +  \frac{\Delta_S}{2} \hat{S}_z + g_{0} ( \hat{a}\hat{S}_{+} + \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{-} ) + (\epsilon_d \hat{a}^\dagger + \epsilon_d^* \hat{a}),
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\Delta_r = \omega_r - \omega_d$ and $\Delta_S = \omega_S - \omega_d$. The driving term in the Hamiltonian will induce the cavity to develop a large coherent field $\alpha$ which translates as a non-zero photon occupation. From input-output theory \sidecite{blais_circuit_2020}, the equation of motion for the coherent state of the resonator under drive is given by  

\begin{equation}
    \dot{\alpha} = - \left( i\Delta_r + \frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\alpha - i\epsilon_d.
\end{equation}

For the steady state $\dot{\alpha}=0$, 

\begin{equation}
    \alpha = -\frac{\epsilon_d}{\Delta_r- i\kappa/2}.
\end{equation}

From this equation, we can see that the resonator beheaves as a Lorentzian filter: the coherent amplitude of the resonator is damped at large detunnings and broadened by $\kappa$. Given the non-zero cavity field, it is convinient to displace the Hamiltonian to be centered around $\alpha$ by introducing the displacement operator 

\begin{equation}
D(\alpha) = \exp\!\left(\alpha \hat{a}^\dagger - \alpha^* \hat{a}\right),
\end{equation}

which redefines the annihilation operator so that $\hat{a}$ describes only quantum fluctuations around the coherent amplitude. After the transformation the Hamiltonian becomes


\begin{equation}
    \cH{JC} / \hbar = \Delta_r\,\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a} +  \frac{\Delta_S}{2} \hat{S}_z + g_{0} \left( (\hat{a} + \alpha)\hat{S}_{x} + (\hat{a}^{\dagger} + \alpha^\dagger)\hat{S}_{y} \right),
\end{equation}

By reorganizing terms we can distinguish three contributions:

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \cH{JC} / \hbar = \underbrace{\Delta_r\,\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}}_\text{resonator} + \underbrace{\frac{\Delta_S}{2} \hat{S}_z + g_{0} \left( \alpha\hat{S}_{+} + \alpha^\dagger\hat{S}_{-} \right)}_\text{spin} + \underbrace{g_{0} \left( \hat{a}\hat{S}_{+} + \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{S}_{-} \right)}_\text{coupling}.
\end{split}
\end{align}

If the amplitude of the coherent state is sufficiently large ($|\alpha|\gg1$) the coupling interaction between the resonator and the spin can be treated as a perturbation and the dynamics of the spin and the resonator can be disentangled. The resulting Hamiltonian for the spin is

\begin{equation}
    \cH{d} / \hbar = \frac{\Delta_S}{2} \hat{S}_z + \frac{\Omega}{2} \left( \cos{\phi}\hat{S}_{x} + \sin{\phi}\hat{S}_{y} \right),
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\alpha = |\alpha| e^{i\phi}$ and $\Omega$ is the Rabi frequency of the drive, given by

\begin{equation}
    \Omega = 2g_0|\alpha| = \frac{2g_0\epsilon_d}{\sqrt{\Delta_r^2 + \kappa^2/4}} \propto \frac{\bra{\Uparrow} S_x \ket{\Downarrow}}{\sqrt{\Delta_r^2 + \kappa^2/4}}.
\end{equation}

This corresponds to a driven two-level system Hamiltonian. We note that $\Omega$ scales proportionally to the $S_x$ matrix element between the two relevant states and is filtered by  a Lorentzian function centered around the cavity frequency. These considerations will be relevant in the following section, when treating weakly allowed transitions with small oscillator strength.

If the spin is initially in the ground state, the excited-state population at time $t$ is

\begin{marginfigure}
    \includegraphics{chapter2/figures/qubit_dynamics.pdf}
    \caption[Spin dynamics]{Quantum trajectory of the spin in the Bloch sphere under a coherent drive. The detuning $\Delta_S$ and the drive $\Omega$ are represented as green and red arrows respectively and the resulting drive vector is plot in black. The trajectory is shown as a black curve.}
    \labfig{spin_trajectory}
\end{marginfigure}

\begin{equation}
    P_e(t) 
    = \frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega^2 + \Delta_S^2}\,
      \sin^2\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Omega^2 + \Delta_S^2}\;t\right).
\end{equation}

At resonance ($\Delta_S=0$), this reduces to simple Rabi oscillations

\begin{equation}
    P_e(t) = \sin^2\!\left(\tfrac{\Omega}{2}\,t\right),
\end{equation}

with resonant Rabi frequency given by  $\Omega = 2g_0|\alpha| = 4g_0 \epsilon_d / \kappa $

\reffig{spin_trajectory} shows the quantum trajectory of the spin in the Bloch sphere under the influence of a detuned drive with phase $\phi=0$. The state evolves from the ground state $\ket{\Downarrow}$ and is coherently excited. Full population transfer to the excited state is only possible when $\Delta_S=0$.


\section{The effect of the environment}
\labsec{effect_environment}

So far, we have considered a resonator coupled to a spin isolated from the environment. In reality, both the spin and the resonator interact with their environment, which can be modeled with an open-system description. These external degrees of freedom effectively induce energy losses on both the resonator and the spin, as shown in \reffig{resonator_spin_lines}. 

\subsection{Energy losses}

The resonator is fabricated on the surface of a dielectric and the electromagnetic mode will be partially contained inside the material. Therefore, the energy of the resonator is subject to dielectric losses and will be lost at a rate $\kappa_i$. In addition, the resonator is coupled to microwave transmission lines, with which it exchanges energy at a rate $\kappa_c$. Therefore, the total loss rate of the resonator is $\kappa = \kappa_i + \kappa_c$. \sidenote{We say the resonator is overcoupled when the total loss is dominated by the capacitive coupling ($\kappa_c>\kappa_i$), undercoupled when the internal damping dominates ($\kappa_c<\kappa_i$), and critically coupled when they are balanced ($\kappa_c=\kappa_i$).}

In general, the electron spin relaxation rate in the solid-state $\Gamma_1$ is dominated by a non-radiative decay where spins exchange energy with the crystal phonons. The strength of this interaction depends on the temperature of the crystal and the frequency of the spins. The non-radiative decay of \Er at $8$~GHz and $10$~mK with $B_0$ along the c axis has been measured to be $\Gamma_\text{NR} = 5$~s$^{-1}$ \sidecite{ledantec_electron_2022}. 

\subsection{Spin decoherence}

The electron spin is magnetically coupled to the surrounding fluctuating magnetic moments of other paramagnetic species and nuclear spins of the host matrix. The fluctuations arise from the flip-flop interactions between the spins which modify the effective magnetic field at the electron position. These interactions result in a loss of phase coherence and create spectral diffusion with a characteristic rate $\Gamma_\phi$.

Spectral diffusion can be suppressed by reducing the density of paramagnetic impurities and nuclear spins. In this context, \Er:\Ca has demonstrated extraordinary large ensemble coherence times of 23~ms~\sidecite{ledantec_twentythreemillisecond_2021}. This achievement was possible due to two factors. First, low concentration of erbium and other paramagnetic impurities, in the order of parts per billion~(ppb). In this regime, the average distance between impurities is much larger than the magnetic dipole interaction range and their contribution to spectral discussion can be neglected. Second, \Ca has one of the lowest nuclear magnetic moment densities among natural abundance crystals. Only the \W isotope has a non-negligible abundance ($14.4$\%), with nuclear spin $I=1/2$ and a relatively weak gyromagnetic ratio.

In addition, nuclear polarization can be used to suppress spectral diffusion when the nuclear spin flip-flop rate is smaller than the measurement rate. In \refsec{dnp_half}, we explore dynamical nuclear polarization (DNP) using a single spin.

Finally, an additional source of spectral diffusion are fluctuations of the static magnetic field, which arise from the intrinsic noise of the current source. We mitigate this by using superconducting coils that are set in persistent-current mode, where the current source is effectively disconnected from coil. This results in a substantial increase of field stability (see \refsec{measurement_setup}).

Given that the coherence time of the spin cannot exceed its lifetime, the total decoherence rate of the spin is given by

\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_2 = \frac{\Gamma_1}{2} + \Gamma_\phi
\end{equation}

In practice, this corresponds to the linewidth of the EPR-allowed transition of a single spin. When $\Gamma_\phi \ll \Gamma_1$ the spin is said to be lifetime-limited, as it does not visibly suffer any decoherence before energy relaxation.

\subsection{Purcell effect}

We now consider how coupling the spin to the resonator can result in a modification of its lifetime though the Purcell effect. Depending on the relative magnitude of the coupling $g_0$ and the relevant loss rates, two regimes can be distinguished.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Strong coupling regime}: $g_0 \gg \kappa, \Gamma_2$, where the system coherently exchanges excitations between the spin and the resonator multiple times before dissipating.
    \item \textit{Weak coupling regime}: $g_0 \ll \kappa, \Gamma_2$, where energy is damped before any coherent oscillations can occur.
\end{itemize}

The as measured in \refch{sample_and_setup}, the total losses of the resonator used in this work are $\kappa/2\pi \approx 700$~kHz. By comparing the losses to the expected coupling to the spins $g_0/2\pi \approx 10$~kHz $\ll \kappa$ we conclude we are deep in the weak-coupling regime. Nevertheless, the resonator enhances the density of states at the resonant frequency therefore enhancing the radiative decay rate of any resonant \Er spin via the Purcell effect \sidecite{purcell_resonance_1946}. The Purcell radiative relaxation rate\sidenote{An in-depth derivation of the Purcell effect can be found in \cite{haroche_exploring_2006}.} is given by

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Purcell_formula}
    \Gamma_\text{R}(\omega_S) = \frac{g_0^2\kappa}{(\omega_r-\omega_S)^2 + \kappa^2/4}.
\end{equation}

Moreover, the spin it undergoes a frequency shift (the Lamb shift)

\begin{equation}
\Delta\omega(\omega_S) = (\omega_r-\omega_S)\, \frac{g_0^2}{(\omega_r-\omega_S)^2 + \kappa^2/4}.
\end{equation}

At exact resonance ($\omega_r=\omega_S$) the shift vanishes and the Purcell rate is given by $\Gamma_\text{R}=4g_0^2/\kappa$. Using the parameter estimates above gives $\Gamma_\text{R}\sim10^3\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$, which is several orders of magnitude larger than $\Gamma_\text{NR}$. Consequently, the main relaxation mechanisms for resonant \Er spins becomes radiative.

The resulting photons can either be dissipated due to material losses or sent to the coupled microwave lines, depending on the ratio between the $\kappa_i$ and $\kappa_c$.


\section[Microwave photon counting]{Microwave photon counting}
\labsec{Detecting_individual_Er}

Single-electron spin sensitivity has been recently demonstrated in our group through the use of a unique device known as the Single Microwave Photon Counter (SMPD) \sidecite{wang_addressing_2023}. The device is based on a superconducting circuit, designed to detect individual microwave photons with high efficiency in a narrow frequency bandwidth \sidecite{lescanne_irreversible_2020}. The device consists of a transmon qubit coupled to two coplanar waveguide resonators, called the \textit{buffer} and the \textit{waste} resonators. The incoming photon impinges the buffer resonator and is converted to an excitation in the qubit and the waste through a parametric process controlled by a pump tone. The waste is strongly coupled to the microwave lines and the excitation on this resonator is rapidly dissipated, rendering the process irreversible. Finally, the state of the qubit is measured via dispersive readout through the waste resonator. The qubit is conditionally reset with a $pi$-pulse and subsequently readout until it's measured in its ground states. This process is repeated cyclically.

The detector used in this work was designed, fabricated and characterized by Louis Pallegoix and an in depth study can be found in \sidecite{pallegoix_high_2025, pallegoix_enhancing_2025}. The main improvement of this design with respect to the previous generation is the tunable input bandwidth between $\sim$100~kHz and $\sim$1~MHz. For our purposes, we only discuss the relevant performance metrics: the dark count rate and the quantum efficiency. Appendix \ref{app:SMPD} gives a summary explanation of the operation and characteristics of the device. 

The quantum efficiency of the detector is the ratio between the number of counts and the number of incoming photons. The dark count rate is the count rate when the device is operated without any input signal. Its origin can be traced back to two main effects, spurious qubit excitations and thermal photons on the microwave lines. 
%By reducing the input bandwidth of the detector, we can minimize the effect of the microwave lines radiation. 
\red{fig} shows a series of traces as a function of calibrated input power: at zero power, the detection events correspond to the dark count rate of the detector, as the power increases the number of counts also does so, until the detector saturates. We measure the efficiency of the detector by fitting the number of detection events as a function of the calibrated input power with a linear curve (see \red{fig}). In this work we operate the SMPD at $300$~kHz where the quantum efficiency is approximately 0.8 and the dark count rate fluctuates between 50 and 100~counts/s.

\section{Single-spin detection by microwave photon counting}
\labsec{single_spin_detection_microwave_photon_counting}

Most of the measurements shown in this manuscript are performed by monitoring the Purcell enhanced microwave fluorescence of the \Er spin after a resonant excitation using the SMPD. Unlike traditional single-spin readout methods that rely on spin-dependent optical fluorescence \sidecite{wrachtrup_optical_1993} or on transport-based spin-to-charge conversion (STM \sidecite{elzerman_singleshot_2004} or quantum-dot readout \sidecite{rugar_single_2004}), microwave photon counting operates entirely in the microwave regime and does not require system specific mechanisms like optical transitions or spin-to-charge conversion. 

The measurement scheme is summarized by the diagram presented in \reffig{resonator_spin_SMPD}. The crystalline \Ca sample, where the \Er spins are hosted, takes the form of a rectangular monocrystalline slab. On the surface of the crystal, a superconducting Nb thin film resonator is fabricated. The \Er spins are inductively coupled to the resonator through the magnetic field generated by the nanowire inductive element, thereby enhancing their radiative relaxation rate via the Purcell effect. A microwave pulse is generated at room temperature with a frequency that is resonant with the cavity. If an \Er spin is resonant with the cavity, it will be excited. The spin will subsequently spontaneously relax into a microwave photon, routed with a microwave circulator to the SMPD, where it is detected. The \textit{spin-to-click} efficiency $\eta_\text{spin}$ depends on the SMPD efficiency, the ratio between the coupling and total loss of the resonator and the attenuation of the microwave line connecting the two.
