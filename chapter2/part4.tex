The experiments presented in this thesis were carried out on a single monocrystalline \Ca sample on which a superconducting thin-film resonator was fabricated. The fabrication followed the procedures developed by previous group members \sidecite{ledantec_electron_2022a, wang_addressing_2023}. The key modification implemented in this work was the reduction of the nanowire thickness from 500~nm to 300~nm, resulting in an increased magnetic coupling strength to the \Er ions.

The \Ca sampled used for this work was grown using high-purity, natural-abundance materials and no paramagnetic impurities were added during crystal growth\sidenote{Specific details on the growth of a similar sample can be found in \cite{erb_growth_2013}.}. Nevertheless, the sample contains a residual concentration of \Er impurities of 3.1~ppb, measured using electron-spin resonance (ESR) techniques \red{appendix}. Such a low concentration of paramagnetic ions is crucial to achieve large spin coherence times for \Er, as demonstrated by earlier work of the group where ensemble spin coherence times as long as 23~ms have been demonstrated for \Er:\Ca \sidecite{ledantec_twentythreemillisecond_2021}.

The \Ca sample used in this work was grown from high-purity, natural-abundance materials and no paramagnetic impurities were added during crystal growth\sidenote{Specific details on the growth of a similar sample can be found in \cite{erb_growth_2013}.}. Standard ESR measurements yield a residual \Er concentration of 3.1~ppb (see \red{appendix}), a level low enough to strongly suppress dipolar interactions between impurities. This extremely low doping is crucial for achieving long spin coherence, as demonstrated by previous work of the group, where ensemble coherence times up to 23~ms were measured in \Er:\Ca \sidecite{ledantec_twentythreemillisecond_2021}.

A key property of \Ca that enables long spin coherence is its low magnetic density. The nuclear-spin environment of \Ca is predominantly composed of \W nuclear-spins, which have a natural abundance of 14.4\% and remarkably low gyromagnetic ratio $\gamma_{\text{\W}}/2\pi = 1.77394$~MHz/T \sidecite{knight_solidstate_1986}. The combination of a low density of nuclear-spins and a weak gyromagnetic ratio results in an environment with a reduced magnetic moment density. As such, the magnetic noise perceived by any paramagnetic impurity hosted in the crystal is significantly reduced compared to other hosts used in quantum applications, such as yttrium orthosilicate (Y$_2$SiO$_5$, YSO) and yttrium oxide (Y$_2$O$_3$). This results in slow spectral diffusion and increased coherence times for any paramagnetic impurity hosted in the crystal.

\subsubsection{Sample assembly}

To deliver microwave signals to the spin resonator, the sample was mounted inside a 3D copper cavity and coupled to the microwave lines via a pin antenna \sidenote{In transmission mode the resonator is coupled to two antennas connected to separate outputs.}. The antenna is soldered to an SMA connector and passes through a small pinhole in the cavity wall; the coupling is tuned by adjusting the antenna insertion depth. The sample is affixed to a silicon bridge with vacuum grease, and the bridge is held in place by compression between the cavity halves. See \red{reffig}. The 3D cavity is then mounted on the fridge with a three-axis magnet surrounding the assembly. 

\section{Measurement setup}

In order for the SMPD to properly operate and for the spin population to be polarized in the ground state, the experiments need to be performed at cryogenic temperature. In order to achieve this requirement, both the sample and the SMPD are mounted at the bottom stage of a dilution refrigerator, where a temperature of 10~mK can be achieved. We use microwave coaxial cable and low temperature components to deliver the microwave signals and interface the devices. The wiring diagram of the fridge is presented in \reffig{wiring_diagram}.  During the wiring of the cryostat, special care was taken to properly attenuate the lines that are directly connected to the SMPD. Lack of proper attenuation leads to increased thermal dark counts, degrading the performance of the detector.  \red{do room temperature diagram}

We note that during the course of this thesis two measurement configurations were employed: a reflection configuration and a transmission configuration. These correspond to the two experimental runs that comprise the bulk of the results. The first run, performed in reflection mode, comprises the work presented in chapters \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w} and \red{ch}. The results of the second run were obtained in transmission mode and are presented in \red{refsec}. The motivation for switching to the transmission configuration was the expectation that placing the microwave cavity between the room-temperature environment and the SMPD input would reduce the flux of thermal photons reaching the detector and therefore lower the dark-count rate. In practice this we did not measure any significant effect. In addition, when switching to the transmission configuration, a second TWPA was added at the output of the resonator with the intention of performing a specific experiment, which has not taken place yet.

The magnet is a 1T/1T/1T superconducting vector magnet, which can produce magnetic field along the three orthogonal directions $X$, $Y$ and $Z$. The coils are independently driven by three current sources which can be individually shunted on demand which allows the magnets to go into \textit{persistent mode} which locks the current going through the superconducting magnet. Consequently, when performing any single spin measurements the magnet is always set into persistent mode as the stability of the field is greatly increased. Given that the resonator is a superconducting film, the magnetic field needs to be applied on the plane of the resonator, as any small out-of-plane component will destory the superconducting its properties.


\section{Superconducting resonator characterization}

The initial characterization of the resonator is performed at zero magnetic field using a Vector Network Analyzer. By fitting the complex reflection parameter $S_{11}$ we obtain the internal and coupling losses, $\kappa_i/2\pi=319.23$~kHz and $\kappa_c/2\pi = 421.96$~kHz, respectively (see \reffig{resonator_fit}).

    \caption[Zero-field resonator fit]{Reflection signal of the resonator at zero magnetic field. The data is fit to a standard reflection model (black lines) which yields $\omega_r/2\pi=7.74845$~GHz and a quality factor of $Q\approx10\,000$.}
    \labfig{resonator_fit}

During the mounting process, the resonator assembly is fixed such that the $Y$ and $Z$ axes of the magnet are close to the plane of the resonator. Due to the limited precision of manual alignment, we begin the experimental run by measuring the relative alignment between these coordinate frames. We follow the procedure described in \sidecite{dantec_electron_2022}, which we briefly summarize below.

The working principle of the alignment procedure is the strong dependence of the resonator frequency on any out-of-plane component of the magnetic field \sidenote{A magnetic field increases the penetration depth, which increases the kinetic inductance and reduces the resonator frequency. This effect is also present for in-plane fields but is drastically stronger for out-of-plane components.}. By measuring the dependence of the frequency on the components of the field we can map a rotation from the magnetic-field axes $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ to the resonator-plane axes $x$, $y$, and $z$. The steps of the procedure, shown in a diagram in \reffig{field_alignment}, are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Apply a 50~mT field along the $Y$ axis.
    \item Sweep the amplitude of a small magnetic field along the $X$ axis and measure the frequency of the resonator. The combined field makes an angle $\varphi$ with respect to the $(Y, Z)$-plane. We measure the angle for which the resonator frequency is maximized $\varphi_0$ which defines the intermediate coordinate frame $X'$, $Y'$, and $Z$ (see \reffig{field_alignment}.a). 
    \item Ramp down the magnetic field along $Y$ to 0~mT.
    \item Ramp up the magnetic field along $Z$ to 50~mT.
    \item Repeat the procedure described in step 2. The angle $\psi_0$ in the ($Y'$, $Z$)-plane defines the aligned coordinate system $x$, $y$, and $z$ (see \reffig{field_alignment}.b). 
    \item Define $\theta$ as the angle in the resonator plane with respect to the $z$ axis (see \reffig{field_alignment}.c).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[b]
    \includegraphics{chapter2/figures/b_field_alignment_scheme.pdf}
    \caption[Magnetic field alignment]{Magnetic field alignment scheme and data. Figures a, b and c show the subsequent rotations performed to arrive to the resonator reference frame. Figures d and e plot the frequency of the resonator as a function of the out of plane angle during step 2 and 5 of the aligning procedure (see text). The data were fit to a parabola and the alignment angles $\varphi_0$ and $\psi_0$ correspond to the top of the parabolic fit.}
    \labfig{field_alignment}
\end{figure}

The resonator frequency as a function of $\varphi$ and $\psi$ is plot in \reffig{field_alignment}.d and \reffig{field_alignment}.e, respectively. The data were fit to a parabola and the top gives the values of $\varphi_0$ and $\psi_0$. The applied magnetic field in the resonator plane can be then expressed in the laboratory frame as

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{B}_{0} = B_{0}
    \begin{pmatrix}
    \sin \theta \, \sin \phi_0 - \cos \theta \, \sin \psi_0 \, \cos \phi_0 \\
    \sin \theta \, \cos \phi_0 + \cos \theta \, \sin \psi_0 \, \sin \phi_0 \\
    \cos \theta \, \cos \psi_0
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Spin ensemble}

Before performing single spin manipulation we begin by characterizing the \Er ensemble. We ramp the magnetic field along the $z$-axis (which is close to the $c$-axis) to approximately 440~mT and proceed with the following measurement. We apply a strong microwave pulse with a duration of 100~\textmu s \red{check} at the frequency of the resonator $\omega_r$. After the pulse, we collect the number of counts impinging on the SMPD during an integration window of $\tau_\text{int}=200$~ms. We average the total number of counts during the integration time over a number of iterations to obtain the ensemble average counts $\langle C \rangle$. We repeat this measurement while sweeping the magnetic field. Due to the dependence of the resonator frequency with the applied magnetic field, we re-tune the SMPD center frequency for every value of $B_0$. \reffig{high_power_spectroscopy} plots $\langle C \rangle$ as a function of $B_0$, where the increase in counts corresponds to the EPR signal of an ensemble of \Er ions. 


\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics{chapter2/figures/high_power_spectroscopy.pdf}
    \caption[High power spectroscopy]{High power spectroscopy. Ensemble average of counts after microwave excitation $\langle C \rangle$ as a function of the magnetic field along the $z$-axis. The data points are fit to a Lorentzian curve (black line) centered at $B_0^\mathrm{max}$. \textit{Top right inset.} Pulse sequence. \textit{Top left inset} Microwave count rate as a function of time for the green and red data points. The integral of the curve, which corresponds to $\langle C \rangle$, is highlighted with the respective colors. The dark count rate of the SMPD is plot as a dashed horizontal line.}
    \labfig{high_power_spectroscopy}
\end{figure}


The inset plot shows the count rate $\langle \dot C \rangle$ after the excitation pulse at $t=0$ for two different magnetic fields. The integral of the curves yields $\langle C \rangle$ of the red and green points in the main plot. When the resonator is not resonant with the ensemble (red curve), the count rate is flat and equal to the dark count rate of the SMPD, shown as a black dashed line. The first point of the curve escapes that trend, and we attribute this increase in counts to heating of the microwave lines and attenuators due to the pulse. In contrast, when the ensemble is resonant the count rate displays a non-exponential decay over a period of time longer than 200~ms, consistent with the excitation of an ensemble of \Er spins with different coupling strength.

The data is fit to a lorentzian which yields the center field of the main line $B_0^\mathrm{max}$. We repeat this measurement as a function of $\theta$, the angle between $B_0$ and the $z$-axis. \reffig{sample_field_alignment}.a shows the Lorentzian centers as a function of the angle and fit to a parabola. We note that the maximum frequency for the \Er main line is \textit{not} found at the expected field\sidenote{
    At $B_0\approx440$~mT, the resonator's frequency is $\omega_r \approx 7.7492$~GHz, so the expected field is \[\omega_r / \gamma_\parallel \approx 444\text{~mT}.\]
}, which indicates that the crystalline and the resonator axis are not parallel to each other. Indeed, the $c$-axis of the crystal is not necessarily contained within the resonator plane: the sample is cut from a larger \textit{boule} and, due to fabrication constraints, the alignment precision is approximately one degree. To measure the misalignment, we apply at $\theta=0$ an out of plane component along the $x$-axis and measure the center frequency of the resonance as a function of the out-of-plane angle $\beta$. \reffig{sample_field_alignment}.b plots the gyromagnetic factor $\gamma$ associated to the measured resonance\sidenote{
    As discussed above, a magnetic field will modify the resonator frequency. This effect can be ignored for small changes of in-plane field, but \textit{not} for out-of-plane components. We take into consideration this effect by measuring $\gamma \equiv \omega_r(B_0) / B_0$ instead.
} as function of $\beta$, and the data is fit to a parabola which yields $\beta_0=-0.57$º.

\subsection{Individual \Er spins}
\labsec{individual_er_ion_characterization}

We now proceed to describe the procedure to detect and characterize single \Er spins. Due to spectral crowding in ensemble resonance, it is not possible to detect spins that are spectrally close to the main line. We leverage the large inhomogeneous broadening of REI in crystals by measuring 4 or 5~mT above the main \Er ensemble line. With this separation, the reduced spectral density allows for a clear identification of single emitters. At this field, we set the $Z$-axis of the magnet in persistent mode to increase the stability of the field. The detection scheme for single spins follows the same principle as the spectroscopy measurement described in the previous section. However, the amplitude of the pulse is reduced by a factor \red{check} and its duration to 10~\textmu s and the integration time is set to $\tau_\text{int} = 10$~ms. \reffig{electron_characterization}.a plots $\langle C \rangle$ as a function of the magnetic field in the $y$-axis. The data shows a constant background with a distribution of narrow and reproducible peaks which we attribute to the fluorescence of individual \Er ions. 

To individually address one spin, we ramp $B_y$ to the resonant value and set the remaining two axes of the magnetic field in persistent mode. We systematically characterize each spin with the following procedure:

\begin{figure*}
    \includegraphics{chapter2/figures/electron_characterization.pdf}
    \caption[Low power spectroscopy and electron spin characterization]{Low power spectroscopy and electron spin characterization. Pulse sequences are shown as an inset in all subplots.
    \textbf{a.} Single spin spectroscopy. Ensemble average counts $\langle C \rangle$ as a function of $B_y$ measured with an integration time $\tau_\text{int}=10$~ms. The measurement data have been connected with a line for visual clarity. 
    \textbf{b.} Rabi sequence. $\langle C \rangle$ as a function of the duration of the driving pulse. The measurement was done with an integration time of 1~ms. The data is fit to a cosine oscillation with a linearly increasing background
    \textbf{c.} Single spin fluorescence. Count rate $\langle \dot C \rangle$ as a function of the time $t$ after a $\pi$ pulse. The data is fit to an exponential decay with a constant background. The integral of the data minus the background (gray area and dashed line respectively) gives a \textit{spin-to-click} efficiency $\eta_\text{spin}=0.39$. The fit yields $T_1=1.24$~ms from which we calculate the coupling $g_0/2\pi=4.5$~kHz.
    \textbf{d. \& e} Ramsey and Hahn echo sequence. $\langle C \rangle$ as a function of the interpulse delay $\tau$ between the two $\pi/2$-pulses. The exponentially decaying cosine fits yield $T_2^*=81$~\textmu s and $T_2=1.3$~ms respectively.
    }
    \labfig{electron_characterization}
\end{figure*}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rabi oscillations}. We measure $\langle C\rangle$ as a function of the duration of the driving microwave pulse (see \reffig{electron_characterization}.b). On top of the Rabi oscillations, we note a linearly increasing background that we attribute to the heating of the microwave lines. The data is fit to a cosine oscillation with a linearly increasing background which yields the Rabi frequency $\Omega_S$ for this specific microwave amplitude.
    \item \textbf{Fluorescence}. We measure the count rate $\langle \dot C\rangle$ as a function of the time $t$ after the microwave pulse (see \reffig{electron_characterization}.b). To avoid any signal coming from the heating of the microwave lines, we wait a time $\tau_\text{w}=120$~\textmu s after the pulse before measuring. The data is fit to a single exponential decay with a constant background. Given that the signal comes from a single emitter, the integral of the signal minus the background rate gives the \textit{spin-to-click} efficiency $\eta_\text{spin}$. During the experiments presented in this thesis, $\eta_\text{spin}\approx0.3--0.4$, a considerable improvement from previous demonstrations \sidecite{wang_singleelectron_2023}, thanks to the increased sensitivity of the SMPD used in this work \sidecite{pallegoix_enhancing_2025}.
    In addition, the fit yields the relaxation time of the spin $T_1$. Since the relaxation is completely dominated by the Purcell rate, we calculate the coupling to the resonator as $g_0 = \tfrac12 \sqrt{\kappa / T_1}$.
    \item \textbf{Ramsey and Hahn echo coherence}. We measure $\langle C\rangle$ after a Ramsey and a Hahn echo sequence as a function of the interpulse delay between the two $\pi/2$ pulses. The data are fit to an exponentially decaying cosine which yield $T_2^*$ and $T_2$ respectively.
\end{itemize}

In addition, previous work of the group has demonstrated that each peak corresponds to a single emiter through instensity-instensity correlation analysis \sidecite{wang_singleelectron_2023}. 

Finally, we can perform a 2D spectroscopy by simultaneously sweeping the field amplitude and the in-plane angle $\theta$. \reffig{2d_spectroscopy} shows a large map created by stitching several of such measurements. The data shows many individual ions, each with a different gyromagnetic tensor, as well as a small section of the main line. This can be explained by the individual strain and charge compensation conditions of each spin which induce a local electric field at the ion's position. As discussed in \refsec{induced_dipole}, \Er in \Ca has a magnetically-induced electric dipole, and variations on local electric conditions will yield different shifts of the spin transition.
