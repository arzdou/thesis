\begin{quote}

In this chapter, we approach our system from a quantum-computing perspective.  We describe how to build a two-qubit register using two \W nuclear spins coupled to a single \Er impurity. Using the single-shot readout and DNP techniques demonstrated in the previous chapter, we expand on the control of the spins by driving stimulated Raman transitions, which enable the implementation of single- and two-qubit gates.

\section{Introduction}

Nuclear spins in the solid-state are attractive candidates for quantum computing, offering long coherence times~\cite{steger_quantum_2012,pla_highfidelity_2013a, zhong_optically_2015, maurer_roomtemperature_2012a} and high density. However, the same weak coupling to the environment that enables long coherence also makes nuclear spins difficult to control and read out with high fidelity. As a result, most nuclear-spin-based quantum architectures rely on coupling to an ancillary electron spin that provides both control and a readout channel. 

Two classes of systems can be distinguished based on the measurement technique. On the one hand, optically detected systems infer the state of the electron spin through the spin-dependent fluorescence of optical transitions, either by identifying "dark-states" where the fluorescence is suppressed or by resolving spin-dependent frequency shifts in the optical transition~\cite{neumann_singleshot_2010,taminiau_detection_2012,bourassa_entanglement_2020,uysal_coherent_2023}. On the other hand electrically detected electron spins rely on charge conversion mechanisms to measure the state of the electron through contact electrodes or an STM tip~\cite{vincent_electronic_2012,pla_highfidelity_2013,pla_coherent_2014,willke_hyperfine_2018,asaad_coherent_2020}.

Among these platforms, optically detected Nitrogen-Vacancy (NV) centers in diamond represent a particularly mature benchmark for nuclear-spin quantum computing. Strong hyperfine interactions between the NV electron and its neighboring nuclear spins enable single-shot readout of the nuclear spin state by resolving hyperfine shifts on the electron spin transitions~\cite{neumann_singleshot_2010}. Single- and two-qubit gates exceeding $99.9\%$ fidelity are enabled by dynamical decoupling (DD) techniques on the electron spin~\cite{bartling_universal_2024}. DD sequences are crucial to achieve high-fidelity, as they allow to selectively enhance the coupling to a single nuclear spin, while decoupling the rest of the register, effectively "turning-off" the hyperfine interaction. \red{fig} shows an example of such a sequence, where at specific interpulse delays the loss of electron spin coherence indicates entanglement with a single $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ nucleus. The ability to frequency resolve each nuclear spin comes from the $S=1$ nature of the electron spin, as DD only enhances the hyperfine splitting at first order when the average magnetic moment between the two electronic levels is non-zero. This is achieved restricting the manifold of the NV electron spin to $m_S = \{-1, 0\}$. This type of high-precission control has enabled the realization of multi-qubit nuclear registers, including a ten-qubit register~\cite{bradley_tenqubit_2019} and fault-tolerant logical qubits~\cite{abobeih_faulttolerant_2022b}. Despite these achievements, scaling nuclear-spin registers while maintaining long coherence and optical accessibility remains challenging due to cross-talk and uncontrolled interactions with surrounding spins.

In parallel, electrical detection of electron spin donors in silicon enabled the demonstration of long-lived single-$^{31}$P nuclear spin qubits in isotopically-enriched silicon~\cite{muhonen_storing_2014a}, the operation of a two-$^{31}$P quantum gate~\cite{madzik_precision_2022}, and the control~\cite{fernandezdefuentes_navigating_2024} and preparation of a $^{123}$Sb $7/2$-nuclear spin in a Schr\"odinger-cat state~\cite{yu_creation_2024}. 

%Electrical detection was used also for TbPc$_2$ molecular quantum dots, enabling the manipulation and readout of a spin-3/2 $^{159}$Tb nuclear spin~\cite{thiele_electrically_2014a} and the implementation of Grover's search algorithm on a single $^{159}$Tb nucleus~\cite{godfrin_operating_2017}. 

%We particularly highlight the achievements of optically detected individual Nitrogen-Vacancy (NV) centers in diamond for quantum computing. This color center is formed by a carbon vacancy and a nitrogen impurity in proximity to each other, in its negatively charged state an extra electron is located at the vacancy, forming a triplet spin state $S=1$. Additionally, nitrogen's most abundant isotope ($^{14}\mathrm{N}$) possesses a nuclear spin $I=1$. 

%The proximity between the two spins yields a strong contact interaction that gives rise to a sizeable hyperfine coupling, enabling coherent and fast conditional control between the electron and nuclear spins. Dynamical Decoupling (DD) techniques have been used to demonstrate high fidelity ($>99.9\%$) single- and two-qubit gates between the two spins~\cite{bartling_universal_2024}. These impressive benchmarks arise from the noise-decoupling properties of the gates, which refocus environmental noise while selectively exploiting the hyperfine interaction, which is ``always-on''. The nuclear spin is driven through a combination of direct RF pulses and DD sequences on the electron spin that effectively generate tunable, conditional nuclear-spin rotations mediated by the hyperfine interaction.

%In addition, diamond naturally hosts $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ nuclear spins, with a natural abundance of approximately $1\%$. NV centers can be used to directly read out and control the state of neighboring $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ nuclear spins through their hyperfine interaction. However, realizing large quantum registers is challenging owing to intrinsic decoherence channels arising from cross-talk and interactions with nuclear spins outside the register. As in the case of $^{14}\mathrm{N}$, the combination of DD gates and direct RF control can be used to generate high-fidelity operations while canceling decoherence channels and suppressing cross-talk. This has enabled the operation of a register of up to ten $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ nuclear spin qubits~\cite{bradley_tenqubit_2019} and the fault-tolerant operation of a logical qubit utilizing five $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ nuclear-spin qubits~\cite{abobeih_faulttolerant_2022b}.

Rare-earth ions (REIs) doped in crystals provide a complementary route towards scalable quantum information processing in the solid state by providing a natural spin--photon interface through optical transitions with long-lived spin degrees of freedom. An early proposal by Roos and M{\o}lmer~\cite{roos_quantum_2004} highlights the use of inhomogeneous broadening as a resource, enabling the selective optical addressing of sub-ensembles of ions within a single crystal via frequency-selective excitation. This concept laid the foundation for later rare-earth quantum computing architectures, in which spectrally resolved ions form small, addressable clusters with controllable interactions mediated by optical excitation \cite{wesenberg_scalable_2007}. Individually detected REIs is an emerging platform where single nuclear spin detection \cite{ruskuc_nuclear_2022b, kornher_sensing_2020} and coherent control \cite{uysal_coherent_2023} has been recently demonstrated through the use of DD sequences. In this context, this chapter explores the use of individual \W nuclear spins coupled to a single \Er impurity as a minimal multi-qubit quantum register. In particular, we demonstrate how to implement single-qubit gates using situated Raman pulses to drive transitions between nuclear spin levels. Compared to DD pulse sequences, this scheme is advantageous in cases where a pair of states with non-zero average magnetic moment is not readily available, as is the case for $S=1/2$ systems. In addition, we demonstrate how energy shifts induced by the microwave drives in this scheme can be used to implement two-qubit gates between the \W nuclear spin qubits.

Requiring relatively straightforward fabrication techniques, this approach permits a single resonator to control numerous coherent spin defects. The techniques used are also readily applicable to a wide range of paramagnetic defects with nearby nuclear spins. Individual all-microwave qubit control on a timescale of milliseconds is achieved by driving a stimulated Raman transition. Two-qubit gates are achieved by taking advantage of induced AC-Zeeman shifts on the energy level structure of the electron and nuclear spin system. We use this to construct a CNOT gate and generate Bell states of the two nuclear spin qubits. We further show that certain Bell states exist in a so-called decoherence free subspace and are inherently resilient to magnetic field noise, extending $T_2^*$ from $0.8(2)~$s and $1.2(3)~$s for the individual qubits to $1.7(2)~$s for the two-qubit protected state.

\section{Readout and preparation}

We extend the readout and preparation protocols from the previous chapter to the two nuclear spin Er$_4$ system. Preparation into arbitrary nuclear spin states is done through a combination of solid-effect DNP (see \refsec{dnp_half}) and single-qubit gates (see next section). Solid-effect DNP is achieved by driving the electron-nuclear transitions (represented as gray arrows in \reffig{8_level_system}) with a $\tau_{\mathrm{prep}}=3.5$~ms length square pulse followed by $\tau_\text{w}$ = 2~ms of wait time, allowing the electron spin to decay. This sequence is then repeated $N_\text{prep}=50$ times to maximize the preparation efficiency. To prepare $\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}$ we drive the zero-quantum transitions. To cover both Qb1 and Qb2 sidebands, the frequency of the pulse is chirped by 50 kHz around the expected sideband transition frequency (see Chapter \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w}). To prepare other states, we apply the appropriate single-qubit gates after initializing in $\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}$.

Qubit eadout leverages the spectral separation of the electron allowed transitions $\omega_{\uparrow\uparrow}$, $\omega_{\uparrow\downarrow}$, $\omega_{\downarrow\uparrow}$ and $\omega_{\downarrow\downarrow}$ (blue, orange, green and red in \reffig{8_level_system}). The linewidth of the resonator $\kappa / 2\pi \approx 700$~kHz and the bandwidth of the SMPD $\sim200$~kHz are sufficient to span the four possible frequencies and the measured spin coherence $T_2^*=170$~\textmu s, allows one to frequency-resolve them. \reffig{spectra_electron} shows four photon-counting spectra obtained by high-resolution spectroscopy with an $80$~\textmu s electron excitation pulse. The peak photon count occurs at a different frequency for each of the four nuclear spin states, and are all clearly distinguishable, allowing for readout of the nuclear spin state.

    \labfig{kmeans}

The readout protocol is shown in \reffig{kmeans}a. Four $\pi$-pulses are consecutively applied, each of the pulses is resonant with one of the electron spin transitions. After each pulse, we monitor the number of counts during an integration time $\tau_\mathrm{int}=1$~ms and after $N_\text{RO}$ iterations the total number of counts after each pulse is given by $C_{\uparrow \uparrow}$, $C_{\downarrow \uparrow}$, $C_{\uparrow \downarrow}$ and $C_{\downarrow \downarrow}$. We define 

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
   \Delta_x &= (C_{\uparrow \uparrow} + C_{\uparrow \downarrow}) - (C_{\downarrow \uparrow} + C_{\downarrow \downarrow}), \\
   \Delta_y &= (C_{\uparrow \uparrow} + C_{\downarrow \uparrow}) - (C_{\uparrow \downarrow} + C_{\downarrow \downarrow}).
\end{split} 
\end{equation}

$\Delta_x$ ($\Delta_y$) effectively "traces out" the state of Qb2 (Qb1) as well as cancelling the contribution of the dark counts to the photon counting statistics. \reffig{kmeans}b plots the measured $\Delta_y$ versus $\Delta_x$ for each possible state of the nuclear spin manifold with $N_\text{RO}=200$ readout pulses. The color of the points represents the state in which the system was prepared before the measurement took place. There are four well resolved distributions, each corresponding to a different prepared state of the two-nuclear-spin system. We use a $k$-means algorithm to separate the space corresponding to each distribution, displayed in the plot with the corresponding background color. The boundaries approximately correspond to the $x=0$ and $y=0$ curves as expected. The skewness in the boundaries can be attributed to inaccurate calibration of the driving frequencies $\omega_{\uparrow\uparrow}$, $\omega_{\downarrow\uparrow}$, $\omega_{\uparrow\downarrow}$ and $\omega_{\downarrow\downarrow}$, which would result in different counting statistics for each state. 

The fact that the assignment of the prepared state is not perfect in \reffig{kmeans}b is indicative of non-negligible State Preparation And Measurement (SPAM) errors. These errors arise from non-idealities in the preparation and measurement procedures and ultimately limit the performance of any quantum computing platform. SPAM errors can be characterized and subsequently mitigated in complex measurements, such as state tomography. One such approach is the $T$-matrix SPAM correction technique \sidecite{lidar_quantum_2013}. The $T$ matrix has dimension $2^n \times 2^n$, where $n$ is the size of the quantum register; in our case, $n = 2$. Each column of the $T$ matrix corresponds to the measured probability distribution obtained after preparing a specific basis state. To correct for SPAM errors, the inverse of the $T$ matrix is applied to the results of any measurement. From the data in \reffig{kmeans}b, we extract the following $T$ matrix:

\begin{equation}
    T = \begin{pmatrix}
0.91 & 0.02 & 0.05 & 0.01 \\
0.01 & 0.92 & 0 & 0.06 \\
0.05 & 0 & 0.91 & 0.03 \\
0 & 0.05 & 0.01 & 0.94 
\end{pmatrix} 
\end{equation}

As discussed in \refsec{single_shot_ro_singlespin}, the main limiting factor of this scheme is the non-zero probability for the electron spin to relax through a nuclear spin flipping transition, which is proportional to $\left(A_\perp / 2\omega_I \right)^2$. These cross-relaxation events limit the number of readout pulses that can be applied before the readout becomes non-QND.

\section{Stimulated Raman driving.}

A straightforward method to implement single-qubit gates would be to directly drive the nuclear transitions at approximately 800~kHz through RF pulses. However, we are constrained by the experimental setup, since the electron spins are addressed via a resonator, filtering any excitation outside its bandwidth. Therefore, we require a different method to control the nuclear spins, which can be implemented with microwave pulses. As discussed in \refch{nuclea_spin_control}, the method of choice used for coherent control of the \W nuclear spins is stimulated Raman driving.

In \refsec{raman_driving} we introduced microwave stimulated Raman driving, derived the analytical Rabi frequency and discussed the origin of the \textit{differential AC-Zeeman shift}. We now proceed to briefly remind the reader these concepts and introduce the subtleties that arise when a second \W qubit is strongly coupled to the \Er spin, from which we naturally implement two-qubit gates.

Let us first consider a single \W qubit coupled to the \Er spin. To control the state of the nuclear spin through stimulated Raman driving, we simultaneously apply two detuned microwave pulses with amplitudes $\Omega_\text{A}$ and $\Omega_\text{B}$. The frequency detuning between the two pulses is defined as $\delta$ and the frequency detuning between drive A and the $\ket{\Downarrow \uparrow} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Uparrow \uparrow}$ transition is defined as $\Delta$, as depicted in \reffig{raman_scheme_v2}. The population between the two lower nuclear spin levels is coherently driven when $\delta = \omega_I^{(\Downarrow)} + \delta_\text{AC}$, where $\omega_I^{(\Downarrow)}$ is the bare NMR transition frequency and $\delta_\text{AC}$ corresponds to the differential AC-Zeeman shift due to the off-resonant driving of the microwave pulses.

    \includegraphics{chapter4/figures/raman_scheme.pdf}

We highlight the nuclear spin transition in the ground state of the \Er ground state with frequency $\omega_I^{(\Downarrow)}$. \textit{(right)} Two microwave pulses with amplitude $\Omega_\text{A, B}$ are applied on a four-level system. The detuning between drive A and the $\ket{\Downarrow \uparrow} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Uparrow \uparrow}$ is defined as $\Delta$. The frequency difference between the two pulses is defined as $\delta$. The bare energy levels (gray dashed lines) have been AC-Zeeman shifted by the strong off-resonance drives. The combined AC-Zeeman shift of the system results in an effective shift of the nuclear spin transition by $\delta_\text{AC}$}
    \labfig{raman_scheme_v2}

We now return to the system with two \W nuclear spins. In the following, we concentrate on the control of Qb1, as the same methods apply to Qb2. We differentiate two cases, depending on the state of Qb2, highlighted in different colors in \reffig{2q_raman_scheme}a. The key difference between the two cases arises from the different Raman shifts $\Delta^{\uparrow, \downarrow}$ depending on the state of Qb2. The origin of this lies on the split of the electron allowed frequencies and for the same Raman drive, $\Delta^{\uparrow} \approx \Delta^{\downarrow} + A_{\parallel}^{(2)}$.

Using Eq. \ref{eq:ac_zeeman} we can calculate the expected differential AC-Zeeman shift on the Qb1 transition for both possible states of Qb2 and equal driving amplitudes $\Omega_\text{A} = \Omega_\text{B}$ (see \reffig{2q_raman_scheme}b). Stimulated Raman driving is a second order process that requires $\Delta \gg \Omega_\text{Ram}$ and the figure only plots $\delta^{\downarrow, \uparrow}_\text{AC}$ where this condition is met. As expected, the two quantities are similar but not equal, and we naturally introduce:

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \delta_{2qb} &\equiv \delta^\uparrow_\text{AC} - \delta^\downarrow_\text{AC}
\end{split}
\end{align}

\reffig{2q_raman_scheme}c shows $\delta_{2qb}$ as a function of $\Delta^\uparrow$. Based on the value of $\delta_{2qb}$ we can distinguish two regimes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unconditional driving.} In this regime $\delta_{2qb}\approx0$ and the Qb1 transition frequency is effectively independent of Qb2. It is obtained for $\Delta^{\uparrow} \approx \Delta_u \equiv -\omega_{I,1}/2 - A_\parallel^{(2)}/2$, where the driving speed is also close to its maximum\sidenote{Experimentally, the unconditional regime applies when $|\delta_{2qb}| \ll \Omega_\text{Ram}$. For $\Omega_\text{Ram}\approx0.1\,$kHz we observe tolerance for $\Delta^{\uparrow}$ variations of order $\sim 10\,$kHz while remaining unconditional.}.
    \item \textbf{Conditional driving.} For all other values of $\Delta^{\uparrow}$, the transition frequency for Qb1 is conditioned on the state of Qb2.
\end{itemize}

These two regimes respectively define the single- and two-qubit gates. In the unconditional regime, the frequency of the nuclear spin transitions do not depend on the state any other qubit of the register, a crucial property for implementing single-qubit gates. On the contrary, conditional driving creates a native CNOT gate, which will be explored in detail in \refsec{cnot_gate}.

\subsection{Single-qubit gates}

We now perform spectroscopy and characterization of the two \W nuclear spins via stimulated Raman driving in the unconditional regime. We first perform Raman spectroscopy using the unconditional driving condition $\Delta/2\pi = -408.8$~kHz~$\approx \Delta_u/2\pi$, and $\Omega_A = \Omega_B$. The nuclear spin register is prepared in the $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ state, after which a Raman pulse is applied whose detuning $\delta$ is swept, followed by nuclear spin readout. When the resonance condition is met, we observe a tranfer of the nuclear spin population to either the $\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}$ or the $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$ state (see \reffig{spectra_and_rabi}a). The resonance linewidth is of order $100$~Hz or less, two orders of magnitude narrower than the electron spin linewidth, which confirms that the Raman driving occurs through virtual transitions of the electron excited states. The $\sim 2$~kHz splitting between the two nuclear spin transitions reflects different values of $A_\parallel,A_\perp$ and $\omega_I$ for the two \W nuclear spin, due to their distinct positions relative to the \Er ion.

We now confirm the unconditional nature of the drive by studying the spectrum of Qb2 after preparation of Qb1 in either the $\ket{\downarrow}$ or the $\ket{\uparrow}$ state \reffig{uncond_spectra}. At $\Delta \sim \Delta_u$, the two resonances occur at the same frequency, confirming the unconditional driving condition. Additionally, both curves are quantitatively reproduced by simulations based on the spin Hamiltonian performed by Z. Huang (see \refsec{simulations}), using parameters determined by spectroscopic measurements.

Knowing the required drive frequencies, we then prepare the $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ state and sweep the drive duration, giving rise to coherent Rabi oscillations of each qubit, as shown in \reffig{spectra_and_rabi}b. This allows us to measure the Rabi Raman rate $\Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}}^{(1)} / 2\pi = 121(1)$~Hz and $\Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}}^{(2)} / 2\pi = 87(1)$~Hz. for a specific microwave amplitude and calibrate the $\pi$-pulses for Qb1 and Qb2, respectively. 

As detailed in section \refsec{raman_driving}, the frequency of the Rabi oscillations of a nuclear spin under a Raman drive is proportional to the hyperfine coupling $A_\perp$. Consequently, we can use the measured values of $\Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}}^{(1,2)}$ to estimate $A_\perp$. In the unconditional regime with both drives having the same microwave amplitude, this relation is readily expressed as 

\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}} \approx \frac{\Omega_A\Omega_B A_\perp}{\omega_I^2}
\end{equation}

From the data presented in \reffig{spectra_and_rabi}b we obtain $A_\perp^{(1)} / 2\pi = 71(3)$~kHz and $A_\perp^{(2)} / 2\pi = 12.8(6)$~kHz. We note that there is a difference between the measured value of $A_\perp^{(1)}$ compared to the measurement using time trace statistics presented in the previous chapter (where we obtain $\sim$40~kHz). This difference is readily explained by the nature of the time trace measurement, for which rapid changes in the nuclear spin state will be hidden by averaging artifacts. Therefore, the measurement of large values of $A_\perp$ will be biased towards a lower estimation if the SNR limit of the measurement is reached. We consider the value measured by the Raman fit to be more precise, although limited by the precision in microwave amplitude.

One important limitation of this scheme is the increase in incoherent dephasing induced by the microwave pulses, which raise the excited-state population of the \Er spin. Although the microwave drives are detuned from the electron spin transitions, off-resonant excitation is not negligible because of the large drive amplitudes required to compensate for the small cross-transition matrix elements. By extending the Rabi pulse duration sweep up to 40~ms, as shown in \reffig{long_rabi}, we observe a decay of the oscillations with a characteristic time  $T_{2, \mathrm{drive}} = 150(35)$ ms, much faster than $T_2^*$ for this qubit (see next section). We attribute this to an increase in the thermal population of the electron spin $\bar{n}_{\text{th}}$ during driving which results in excess dephasing. The measurement data in \reffig{long_rabi} is fit to a simulation (see \refsec{simulations}) which yields a thermal population $\bar{n}_{\text{th}} = 9.54\times10^{-3}$, several orders of magnitude larger compared to the expected \Er excited state population at 10--20~mK. Due to time constraints, measurement of $T_{2, \mathrm{drive}}$ under different pulse amplitudes was not performed, which would be required to experimentally confirm the origin of this noise source.

\section{Nuclear spin coherence time}

We then proceed to measure the coherence properties of the nuclear spin qubits. We perform a Ramsey experiment on each qubit as shown in  \reffig{ramsey_2qb} and extract $T_2^*$ times of 0.8(2)~s and 1.2(3)~s for Qb1 and Qb2, respectively. To our knowledge, these are the longest single-nuclear-spin Free-Induction-Decay times measured in a natural-abundance material by more than an order of magnitude. These times are on par with those measured in isotopically-enriched silicon~\sidecite{muhonen_storing_2014} and diamond~\sidecite{bartling_universal_2024}, which further confirms the interest of \Ca for spin-based quantum computing~\sidecite{ledantec_twentythreemillisecond_2021}. The residual decay is likely caused by a combination of $B_0$ drift and spectral diffusion due to nuclear spin bath reconfiguration. Coupled Cluster Expansion calculations, performed by Sen Lin and Ren-bao Liu, predict a decay time of $0.2$~s, shorter than the measurements. We interpret the longer experimental $T_2^*$ as being due to partial polarization of the $^{183}$W nuclear-spin bath by the pulses used to prepare the $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ state, an effect which has been observed in similar systems~\sidecite{taminiau_universal_2014, madzik_controllable_2020}. Additionally, the Larmor frequency of \W neighboring the \Er impurity experience a paramagnetic shift due to the hyperfine coupling, which reduces the flip-flop interaction rate with the rest of the \W bath, also known as the frozen-core effect \red{ref}.

We performed Hahn echo measurements yielding $T_2$ times of 3.4(4)~s and 4.4(6)~s for qubits 1 and 2, respectively, as shown in \reffig{echo_2qb}. Coupled Cluster Expansion calculations predict a Hahn echo decay time of $10$~s due to nuclear spin bath dynamics. We believe the extra decoherence is due to residual excitations of the \Er ion, which occur at a rate $\bar{n} \Gamma_R$, $\bar{n} \approx 10^{-4}$ being the mean thermal occupation of the resonator mode, a value roughly consistent with the measured SMPD dark count rate. Energy relaxation of the two nuclear spin qubits likely occurs at a much lower rate, as indicated by ensemble measurements, which yield an upper bound of $\sim 10^{-6}~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ for the relaxation rate of $^{183}\mathrm{W}$ proximal to \Er:\Ca~\sidecite{wang_monthlonglifetime_2024}, and was not directly measured. Dynamically detuning the resonator from the \Er spin during pulse sequences while employing dynamical decoupling may offer a path to further extend these coherence times.


\section{Entangling the nuclear spins}
\labsec{cnot_gate}

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics{chapter4/figures/sedor.pdf}
    \caption[SEDOR measurement]{Spin-echo double resonance (SEDOR) measurement (dots) performed on Qb1, where sequence 1 and sequence 2 (as shown above) yielded the red and green curves respectively. The fits (solid lines) yield a nuclear-nuclear coupling rate $C_{zz}/2\pi = 0.8$~Hz.}
    \labfig{sedor}
\end{figure}

We now proceed to implement two-qubit gates in our two-nuclear spin register. First we investigate the direct interaction between Qb1 and Qb2, which we find too small to perform entangling gates. We then proceed to demonstrate how to implement an CNOT by driving stimulated Raman transitions in the unconditional regime.

Being in close-proximity, the two nuclear spins are coupled by the dipolar interaction $C_{zz} I_{z1} I_{z2}$. If strong enough, this interaction could be used to create a native two-qubit entangling gate. Consequently, we proceed by measuring $C_{zz}$ by performing a Spin Echo DOuble Resonance (SEDOR) experiment~\cite{abobeih_atomicscale_2019}, as shown in \reffig{sedor}. This consists of a Hahn echo experiment performed on one nuclear spin qubit, the probe qubit, where either no pulse (red curve) or an additional $\pi$ pulse (green curve) is applied to the other qubit at the middle of echo sequence. This yields a shift in frequency of the probe qubit equal to $C_{zz}/2$. We measure $C_{zz} / 2\pi = 0.8$~Hz, on the same order of magnitude as the qubit dephasing rate. Therefore, any gate implemented through direct nuclear-nuclear coupling will have a duration in the order of seconds, and it is desirable to find an alternative mechanism.

We now focus instead on the conditional AC-Zeeman shifts induced by the stimulated Raman drives. For this purpose, we try to maximize the value of $\delta_{2qb}$. We set $\Delta/2\pi=110$~kHz and $\Omega_B = 5\Omega_A$. \section{Bell state tomography}
\labsec{bell_states}

We use this CNOT gate to generate the Bell states $\ket{\Phi^+} = \left(\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}+\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}\right)/\sqrt{2}$ and $\ket{\Psi^+} = \left(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}+\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\right)/\sqrt{2}$. We perform state tomography and extract the reconstructed density matrices of the two Bell states, shown in \reffig{tomography_2qb}. For the purpose of characterization, we define the fidelity of a measured state $\rho$ with respect to a target state $\rho_t$ as \sidecite{nielsen_quantum_2010}

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F} = \left(\text{Tr}\left\{\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho_t} \cdot \rho \cdot \sqrt{\rho_t}}\right\}\right)^2
\end{equation}

SPAM error correction was applied during the reconstruction of the state tomography shown in \reffig{tomography_2qb}. After SPAM correction, we measure fidelities of 0.68 and 0.79 for the $\ket{\Phi^+}$ and $\ket{\Psi^+}$ states, respectively. We now proceed to detail the contribution of the different error sources through the use of numerical simulations.

\begin{figure*}
    \includegraphics{chapter4/figures/tomography_2qb.pdf}
    \caption[Bell state tomography]{Bell state tomography. Real (left) and imaginary (right) components of tomographically reconstructed density matrices of and Bell states $\ket{\Phi^+}$ and $\ket{\Psi^+}$. Extracted fidelities after SPAM error correction are 0.68 and 0.79, respectively.}
    \labfig{tomography_2qb}
\end{figure*}

Numerical simulations were used to investigate the origin of coherent and incoherent errors during Bell state preparation. We simulate the preparation sequence for $\ket{\Psi^+}$ including the required pulses for state tomography to account for the full experimental process. We then perform maximum likelihood estimation on the resulting set of measurement results to reconstruct the density matrix and extract the fidelity to the target state. 

When including only coherent errors (i.e. removing all decay and dephasing effects) the simulation yields a fidelity of 0.978; when including incoherent errors in addition we find an expected fidelity of 0.976. The long coherence times of the two qubits ($\sim$ s) in comparison to the state preparation time ($\sim$ ms) explains the relatively small reduction in fidelity due to incoherent errors. However, coherent errors are insufficient to account for the experimentally measured fidelity of 0.79. We attribute the remaining error to the finite thermal population of the \Er electron spin under a driving field causing a reduced dephasing time as discussed previously and illustrated in \reffig{long_rabi}. During tomography, the average pulse sequence was on the order of $30$~ms. Based on this and $T_{2, \mathrm{drive}}$ as measured in \reffig{long_rabi}, we expect approximately 15\% additional infidelity due to dephasing, which is roughly sufficient to account for the remaining error. Due to time constraints, measurement of $T_{2, \mathrm{drive}}$ under the exact conditions of the Bell state preparation sequence was not performed and further investigation would be required to verify the origin of the reduced fidelity. 

\section{Decoherence free subspace}

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics{chapter4/figures/dfs.pdf}
    \caption[Decoherence free subspace]{Decoherence free subspace. Ramsey coherence measurement of the $\ket{\Phi^+}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}+\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}\right)$ (coral) state and the $\ket{\Psi^+}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}+\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\right)$ (purple) state, yielding $T_2^*$ values of $0.3(1)$ and $1.7(2)~$s respectively. The final two $\pi/2$-pulses carry a phase $\phi$ as an artificial detunning which gives rise to the oscillations. The data (dots) are fit to an exponentially decaying cosine (solid line).}
    \labfig{dfs}
\end{figure}

In systems where magnetic fluctuations are the dominant source of decoherence, entangled spin pairs can exhibit a substantial enhancement of coherence time compared to individual qubits~\sidecite{bartling_entanglement_2022}. This improvement can be traced to the magnetic properties of these specific states; for example, the Bell state $\ket{\Psi^+}$ corresponds to a triplet state with a total spin of 1, and a magnetic projection of $m_S=0$. Compared to bare nuclear spin states the energy difference  between the $\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$ states is approximately 400 times smaller, which suppresses the effect of spatially correlated sources of magnetic field noise that affect both spins equally. As a result, $\ket{\Psi^+}$ forms an effective decoherence-free subspace, where quantum information can be preserved for a longer period of time.

We proceed to study this effect by measuring the coherence of these multi-qubit states. We begin by preparing the system in $\ket{\Psi^+}$, followed by an evolution time $\tau$. Then, we project the state back to the single qubit basis with a $\pi/2$-pulse in each qubit, followed by readout of the populations. We define

\begin{equation}
    S = \left(P_{\downarrow \uparrow} + P_{\uparrow \downarrow}\right) - \left(P_{\downarrow \downarrow} + P_{\uparrow \uparrow}\right),
\end{equation}

which measures the coherence of the Bell state\sidenote{This quantity measures how much of the population remains in the $\{\ket{\downarrow \uparrow}, \ket{\uparrow \downarrow }\}$ manifold compared to the $\{\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}, \ket{\uparrow \uparrow}\}$. As the system decays into a mixed state $S\rightarrow0$.}. \reffig{dfs} plots $S$ as a function of $\tau$. We measure a coherence time for $\ket{\Psi^+}$ of $1.7(2)~$s, which approximately doubles the individual qubit coherence time. In addition, we also measure the coherence of $\ket{\Phi^+}$, also a triplet state but with magnetic projection $m_S=1$. As expected, the measured coherence time for this state is $0.3(1)$, as correlated noise sources are boosted due to the effective increase in gyromagnetic factor.

If we assume additive contributions of independent dephasing rates we can consider that the coherence time for the two Bell states is given by

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \frac{1}{T_{2,\ket{\Psi^+}}^*} &= \sqrt{2}\pi\Gamma_\text{u} + \frac{1}{T_2}, \\
    \frac{1}{T_{2, \ket{\Phi^+}}^*} &= \sqrt{2}\pi\Gamma_\text{u} + 2\pi\Gamma_\text{c} + \frac{1}{T_2}.
\end{split}
\end{align} 

where $\Gamma_\text{u}$ and $\Gamma_\text{c}$ correspond to the dephasing rate due to spatially uncorrelated and correlated noise. In the case of $\ket{\Psi^+}$, correlated noise sources are suppressed to first order as they couple identically to both spins. \red{maybe justify more} From the measured coherence times, we extract the contributions of spatially correlated and uncorrelated noise, $\Gamma_\text{u} \approx 0.1 $ Hz and $\Gamma_\text{c} \approx 0.4 $ Hz. The amplitude of the correlated noise is in agreement with previous observations of magnetic field noise~\sidecite{wang_monthlonglifetime_2024}, pointing to this mechanism as the dominant term in correlated noise.

\section{Time-dependent Hamiltonian simulations}
\labsec{simulations}

We use numerical simulations developed by Zhiyuan Huang using the time-dependent Hamiltonian simulation suite \texttt{QuTip} \sidecite{johansson_qutip_2012} and \texttt{Dynamiqs} \sidecite{guilmin_dynamiqs_2025} to characterize the coherent and incoherent error sources in our system. The simulations consider an electron spin coupled to two nuclear spins and does not include a cavity or background spin bath, described by $\cH{}$ in Eq. \ref{eq:spin_hamiltonian_c4}. An additional driving term with frequency $\omega$ is modeled by the Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
    \cH{d}= \frac{\hbar\Omega_d}{2}(\hat{S}_{-}e^{i\omega t} + \hat{S}_{+}e^{-i\omega t}),
\end{equation}

where $\Omega_d$ is the pulse amplitude, $\hat{S}_{+}$ and $\hat{S}_{+}$ are the lowering and raising operators for the \Er spin respectively. The pulse amplitude used in the simulation is calibrated using the measured electron spin Rabi frequency and pulse durations are matched to those used in the experiment.

The simulation results follow from solving the master equation with the total time-dependent Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{dt} = \frac{1}{i \hbar} \left[ \cH{tot}, \hat{\rho} \right] + \sum_k \left( \hat{L}_k \hat{\rho} \hat{L}_k^\dagger - \frac{1}{2} \left\{ \hat{L}_k^\dagger \hat{L}_k, \hat{\rho} \right\} \right),
\end{equation}

where $\cH{tot} = \cH{} + \cH{d}$. The electron energy relaxation process is modeled by the Lindblad operators $\hat{L}_{s_-} = \sqrt{\Gamma_{1} (\bar{n}_{\text{th}} + 1)} \, \hat{S}_{-}$ and $\hat{L}_{s_+} = \sqrt{\Gamma_{1} (\bar{n}_{\text{th}})} \, \hat{S}_{+}$, where $\Gamma_{1} = \frac{1}{T_1}$ is the electron relaxation rate. Dephasing of the nuclear spins is modeled by $\hat{L}_{n_\varphi,i} = \sqrt{\Gamma_{\varphi}} \, \hat{I}_{z,i}$ for $i=1,2$, where $\Gamma_{\varphi} = \frac{1}{T_2^*}$ is the nuclear dephasing rate. We also note that this analysis only considers Markovian effects, but a more realistic description should also include non-Markovian terms, such as spin-bath dynamics, could be an extra source of decoherence. 

\section{Conclusion and outlook}

In conclusion, we have presented the building blocks for a quantum information processing platform utilizing nuclear spins in the solid state. Primarily, we have demonstrated how to implement single- and two-qubit gates through stimulated Raman driving. This technique leverages the hyperfine induced mixing of the electron and nuclear spin energy levels to drive coherent Raman transitions between the nuclear spin levels. Compared to DD pulse sequences, this scheme can be readily implemented for systems with electronic spin with $S=1/2$, which suffer from a zero-average magnetic moment, which result in longer DD sequences. 

The fidelity of the stimulated Raman gates demonstrated here remains lower than the state of the art achieved in other color-center platforms, such as NV centers. A key limitation of the present scheme is that the application of the Raman drives substantially increases the qubit decoherence rate.  We attribute this effect to an increased thermal occupation of the excited \Er state induced by the applied drives.  To properly address this problem, an in-depth study would need to be performed, tracking the source of the problem, with the goal of developing strategies to suppress or mitigate their impact.

Readout fidelity is intrinsically limited by cross-relaxation events of the nuclear spin during the readout protocol. Increasing the quality factor of the superconducting resonator is a straightforward path to improve this figure. In addition, achieving stronger Purcell rate would help address the long readout times of the experiment: readout of the joint 2-qubit state requires $\sim 1$~s per shot, in comparison to a previous demonstration of $\sim20$~ms for optical QND readout of an \Er spin \sidecite{raha_optical_2020}. It should be possible to reduce the measurement time by orders of magnitude by increasing the Purcell-enhanced spin decay rate using a resonator with a parallel-plate capacitor design to enhance resonator-spin coupling~\sidecite{bienfait_reaching_2016,haikka_proposal_2017}. 

However, the specific parameter range for which this driving scheme is achievable is reduced: effective driving of the nuclear spin through a stimulated Raman process requires a low-quality resonator to avoid microwave filtering. Indeed, the Larmor frequency of the target nuclear spin must be on the same order of magnitude as the resonator's bandwidth $\kappa$. \W nuclear spins possess a low gyromagnetic moment which yields a Larmor frequency of $\sim 800$~kHz at the operating magnetic field. Tuning the electron spin frequency with the resonator results in the two stimulated Raman drives being applied at $\omega_r \pm \omega_I / 2$. If the linewidth of the resonator was an order of magnitude lower, something that would be desirable as previously discussed, the necessary microwave amplitude of each drive to achieve the same Rabi rate would be $\sim 7$ larger as the resonator would filter most of it. This could be addressed by adapting the resonator design to boast a tunable quality factor, as demonstrated in ref.~\sidecite{wen_addressing_2025}. One could envision scheme where $\kappa$ is dynamically tuned to be wider during the pulse scheme and narrower during the readout sequence. 

If one were able to solve the aforementioned problems, the question of scaling the nuclear spin register becomes relevant. Additional nuclear spin qubits could be interfaced using dynamical decoupling control techniques~\sidecite{bradley_tenqubit_2019, beukers_control_2025}. However, this would not be ideal as \Er has an electron spin $S=1/2$, hyperfine shifts in DD sequences would only be second order. In addition, a key necessity of the readout scheme is a rapidly relaxing electron spin, in contradiction with the long-lived spin that DD need to be able to resolve individual nuclear spins. This approach would be limited to around 10 qubits due to the weak dipolar coupling of \W spins to the \Er which decreases rapidly with distance. Distant \Er spins could be deterministically entangled using virtual photon exchange via the superconducting resonator~\sidecite{majer_coupling_2007, landig_virtualphotonmediated_2019}. Readout could be frequency multiplexed using multiple SMPDs, allowing multiple clusters of W spins coupled to \Er ancilla to be controlled via a single resonator.
