\begin{quote}

In this chapter, we approach our system from a quantum-computing perspective. We describe how to build a two-qubit register using two \W nuclear spins coupled to a single \Er impurity. \section{State of the art}
\labsec{sota_ch4}

We start the chapter with a brief review of quantum computing platforms using individual nuclear spins, interfaced and read out via an electron spin ancilla, highlighting topics that are relevant for putting our results in perspective. In these systems, the electron spin acts as a central node, coupling simultaneously to all nuclear spin qubits in the register. Because direct nuclear--nuclear coupling is generally too weak to support fast two-qubit gates, these gates are instead mediated by the electron spin. We highlight two experiments that are representative of the state of the art in two-qubit gate implementations: one using NV centers in diamond and another using phosphorus donors in silicon.

\subsection*{$^{13}\mathrm{C}$ register operated by an NV center in diamond}

Natural-abundance diamond hosts carbon isotopes with non-zero nuclear spin. In particular, $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ has an abundance of $1.07$\% and a spin-1/2. Therefore, the NV center is surrounded by a bath of nuclear spins coupled through the hyperfine interaction. For the purposes of quantum computing, the nuclear spins are both a resource, as they are long-lived qubits, and sources of noise: very weakly coupled nuclear spins are not addressable but nevertheless induce decoherence of the electron spin. Two-qubit gate implementations in electron--nuclear systems leverage the splitting of the ESR-allowed spectrum due to the hyperfine interaction. Consequently, an important challenge for implementing two-qubit gates mediated by the NV electron spin is decoherence of the electron spin. This is addressed by implementing dynamical decoupling sequences on the electron spin, which extend the coherence time and therefore the spectral resolution~\sidecite{vandersar_decoherenceprotected_2012,taminiau_detection_2012}.

We highlight a particular implementation of this scheme by Bradley \textit{et al.}, which was used to demonstrate single- and two-qubit gates in a nine nuclear spin register~\sidecite{bradley_tenqubit_2019}. The sequence is shown in \reffig{stateoftheart_ch4}a. Electron $\pi$-pulses are applied, separated by time intervals of duration $2\tau$. During these intervals, they apply narrowband radiofrequency (rf) pulses. The rf pulses are resonant with the NMR-allowed transition of the nuclear spin, in this case $^{13}\mathrm{C}$, when the electron spin is in state $m_S=-1$. Consequently, the initial state of the electron spin determines which rf pulses are resonant. For initial electron state $m_S=-1$, the odd (red) rf pulses are resonant. For $m_S=0$, the even (blue) rf pulses are resonant.

To ensure that the rf pulses build up constructively, their phases need to be updated to account for the periods of nuclear spin precession between them. \reffig{stateoftheart_ch4}b shows the evolution of the nuclear spin from the initial $\ket{\uparrow}$ state (in yellow). The red (blue) path shows the nuclear spin evolution for the case where the electron starts in the state $m_S=-1$ ($m_S=0$). After eight DD pulses, the nuclear-spin states are antiparallel, which effectively constitutes a CNOT gate.

If the electron spin is prepared in a superposition, and a nuclear spin is resonant during a DDrf sequence, the entangling interaction results in a loss of coherence of the electron spin. Therefore, by sweeping the frequency of the rf pulse, they perform spectroscopy. \reffig{stateoftheart_ch4}c shows the NMR spectra of eight individual $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ measured with a DDrf sequence. Each of them has a unique frequency, mainly due to the hyperfine coupling with the electron spin. They measure the coherence time of $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ nuclear spins and obtain $T_2^*\sim10$~ms and $T_2\sim1$~s. By adding extra dynamical decoupling pulses, they are able to achieve coherence times of tens of seconds with 256 pulses. This highlights the interest of nuclear spins for quantum computing, as their coherence times are remarkably long even in natural-abundance crystals.

%For, the electron spin coupling to the $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ register is relatively weak, mostly dominated by long-distance magnetic dipole interaction. These uncontrolled couplings to the solid-state environment unavoidably induce a strong decoherence rate on the electron spin, preventing high-fidelity gate performance, given that qubit gates are slow when compared to the dephasing rate of the electron spin. To address this issue, nuclear spin gates can be implemented through a combination of direct rf driving on the nuclear spin and dynamical decoupling (DD) sequences applied to the electron spin~\sidecite{vandersar_decoherenceprotected_2012}. 

%DD sequences suppress the coupling of the electron spin to dominant sources of noise through the repeated application of $\pi$ pulses, separated by free-evolution intervals of duration $2\tau$. Throughout this sequence, the effective Larmor frequency of the nuclear spin changes with the electron spin state due to the hyperfine interaction. Therefore, a high-selectivity rf-pulse will only drive the NMR-allowed transitions alternatively between $\pi$ pulses. When the decoupling frequency $1/\tau$ matches the hyperfine interaction strength, the nuclear spin performs $2\pi$ rotation in the Bloch sphere during the free-evolution time when the rf-pulse is not resonant. When the subsequent electron $\pi$-pulse brings the nuclear spin into resonance with the rf-pulse, nuclear spin driving is resumed. This effectively implements a two-qubit gate while simultaneously decoupling the electron spin from the rest of the bath, suppressing the electron decoherence rate. DD sequences without rf driving can also be used to drive the nuclear spin by leveraging the perpendicular hyperfine coupling~\sidecite{taminiau_detection_2012}, but require that the average magnetic moment of the electron between the superposed states of the electron is different from zero~\sidenote{This is readily achieved in NV centers if one restricts the electron spin to the $m_S = \{0, -1\}$ manifold}.

Moreover, they demonstrate that correlated sources of noise, mainly arising from the nuclear spin bath, can be suppressed by preparing a spatially close nuclear spin pair in a decoherence-free subspace (DFS)~\sidecite{lidar_decoherencefree_1998}. In particular, the Bell states $\ket{\Phi^+} = \left(\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}+\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}\right)/\sqrt{2}$ and $\ket{\Psi^-} = \left(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}-\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\right)/\sqrt{2}$ are triplet and singlet states of a nuclear spin pair system. The magnetic projection of the states is $m_{\Phi^+}=1$ and $m_{\Psi^+} = 0$. Therefore, $\ket{\Phi^+}$ is, in principle, twice as sensitive to correlated noise compared to single qubits while $\ket{\Psi^-}$ is only sensitive to uncorrelated noise. 

They measure the coherence time of each state, which shows statistically significant differences, as shown in \reffig{stateoftheart_nv1}. The magnitude of the effect is correlated with the distance between the spins in the pairs, consistent with the idea that spatially close nuclear spins experience similar bath dynamics.

The electronic wavefunction of a phosphorus donor in silicon extends over $\sim2$~nm. Therefore, when two neutral donors are separated by approximately 10~nm, their electronic wavefunctions will overlap, leading to exchange interaction between the electron spins (as shown in \reffig{stateoftheart2_ch4}a). This interaction splits the EPR spectrum of one electron spin depending on the state of the other. In addition, the spectrum is further split by the contact interaction with the $^{31}\mathrm{P}$ nuclear spin, leading to six EPR-allowed transitions, shown in \reffig{stateoftheart2_ch4}b. Each of the transitions corresponds to a different state of the hybrid qubit register. Note that, although the electron spin coherence time is approximately $T_2^*\approx 20$~\textmu s, the strong exchange and contact interactions allow them to resolve all the different transitions. 

A microwave $2\pi$-pulse on a specific transition will induce a $\pi$ phase shift on the corresponding state. Given that this operation is conditional on the state of the other electron and the two nuclear spins, this corresponds to a multi-qubit gate. The corresponding gate after a resonant $2\pi$-pulse for each transition is shown as an inset in \reffig{stateoftheart2_ch4}b. They measure the coherence time of the neutral donors and obtain $T_2^* =0.6$~ms. They are able to extend it to $T_2^* = 30$~ms by ionizing the donor, thereby removing the electron spin noise.

%Therefore, several phosphorus donors can trap a single electron that couples to their nuclear spin via contact interaction. This coupling breaks the degeneracy of the EPR-allowed transitions and a spin-selective $2\pi$-pulse imparts a conditional phase on the nuclear spin state~\sidecite{filidou_ultrafast_2012}. These systems have demonstrated high fidelity gates ($>99\%$) and long nuclear coherence times ($T_2^*\sim1$~ms and $T^\text{CPMG}_2\sim30$~s by ionizing the defect)~\sidecite{muhonen_storing_2014} but have been limited to small clusters of $^{31}P$ nuclear spins due to spectral crowding~\sidecite{madzik_precision_2022, thorvaldson_grovers_2024}. 

%Recently, coupling between two, physically separated nuclear spins was realized. In the work of Stemp \textit{et al.}, each nucleus was bound to a different electron, and the two electrons are coupled by exchange interaction~\sidecite{stemp_scalable_2025}. The gate protocol follows the same physical principle as the nuclear two-qubit gate presented above; the exchange interaction splits the ESR spectrum depending on the state of the electron spin and a spin-selective pulse can impart a conditional phase. Importantly, the ESR-spectrum is further split by the contact interaction with the nuclear spins, so that nuclear-nuclear entangling gates can be performed between the distant qubits (see \reffig{stateoftheart2_ch4}). The relatively low Bell state fidelities were rapidly improved by precise calibration and control of the electronic noise of the system, demonstrating an 11-qubit processor with high-fidelity gates ($>99\%$ two-qubit gate fidelity)~\sidecite{edlbauer_11qubit_2025}.


\subsection*{Discussion}

Both approaches to two-qubit gates leverage the hyperfine coupling to deterministically entangle the electron and nuclear spins. In both cases, a crucial requirement is electron spin coherence. The $T_2^*$ in both systems is relatively low, in the order of $1$~\textmu s for NVs and $20$~\textmu s for neutral $^{31}\mathrm{P}$ donors. However, the approaches to solve this problem in each system are different.

In the case of NV centers, they rely on minute-long electron-spin lifetimes at cryogenic temperatures. This enables them to perform long dynamical decoupling sequences, therefore enhancing the coherence time of the electron spin. For donors in silicon, the electron spins are coupled by a strong contact interaction $\sim10$~MHz, which allows them to spectrally resolve all EPR-allowed transitions, even with a wide electron linewidth of $\sim1$~MHz.

When performing detection by microwave fluorescence, DD sequences are not well suited for extending the coherence of the electron spin, as it is bounded by the Purcell-enhanced radiative rate\sidenote{Also, rf pulses are incompatible with the current setup. This could be overcome with a new resonator design.}. In addition, the electronic wavefunction of the unpaired electron in rare-earth ions is well confined by the more external $5s$ and $5p$ shells. Consequently, the interaction with distant nuclear spins is primarily mediated by magnetic dipole interaction, which is orders of magnitude weaker\sidenote{Especially when considering that the nuclear spins of interest, \W, have a weak gyromagnetic ratio}. 

However, the Hahn echo coherence of \Er in magnetically silent crystals is exceedingly long, $\sim23~ms$ in ensembles~\sidecite{ledantec_twentythreemillisecond_2021}, and in our system is limited by $2T_1$ (see \refsec{individual_er_ion_characterization}). As shown in the previous chapter, we are able to directly resolve individual nuclear spins with hyperfine couplings of 20~kHz. Consequently, the method demonstrated in the work of Stemp \textit{et al.}~\sidecite{stemp_scalable_2025} should be directly applicable, albeit with low fidelity. It is therefore appealing to develop an all-microwave technique that can be used to drive single- and two-qubit gates under these conditions. This is the objective of this chapter.

%But most important, both driving schemes require long electron coherence time to achieve high-fidelity two-qubit gates. This requirement is juxtaposed to the experimental needs of our experiment. Since the detection scheme measures the spin-dependent microwave fluorescence from the electron spin, a strong radiative decay is required. Therefore, a different driving scheme is required, one that leaves electron spin excited state unpopulated and can be implemented with microwave pulses.

However, the same weak coupling to the environment that enables long coherence also makes nuclear spins difficult to control and read out with high fidelity. As a result, most nuclear-spin-based quantum architectures rely on coupling to an ancillary electron spin that provides both control and a readout channel.

On the other hand electrically detected electron spins rely on charge conversion mechanisms to measure the state of the electron through contact electrodes or an STM tip~\cite{vincent_electronic_2012,pla_highfidelity_2013,pla_coherent_2014,willke_hyperfine_2018,asaad_coherent_2020}.

\section{Preparation and readout}

We extend the readout and preparation protocols from the previous chapter to the two-qubit system. Preparation into arbitrary nuclear spin states is done through a combination of solid-effect DNP (see \refsec{dnp_half}) and single-qubit gates (see next section). Solid-effect DNP is achieved by driving the ZQ/DQ transitions (represented as gray arrows in \reffig{8_level_system}) with a $\tau_{\mathrm{prep}}=3.5$~ms-long square pulse followed by $\tau_\text{w}=2$~ms of wait time, allowing the electron spin to decay. This sequence is then repeated $N_\text{prep}=50$ times to ensure preparation. This corresponds to a very large number of repetitions compared to those needed in \refsec{dnp_half}. Given that the measurement time is mostly dedicated to nuclear spin readout (see below), increasing the number of preparation pulses is not a significant time investment.

We chose $\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}$ as the target state for preparation. Therefore, during the preparation sequence we drive the ZQ transitions. To cover both Qb1 and Qb2 sidebands, the frequency of the pulse is chirped by 50 kHz around the expected sideband transition frequency (see Chapter \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w}). To prepare other states, we apply the appropriate single-qubit gates after initializing in $\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}$.

Qubit readout leverages the spectral separation of the EPR-allowed transitions $\omega_{\uparrow\uparrow}$, $\omega_{\uparrow\downarrow}$, $\omega_{\downarrow\uparrow}$ and $\omega_{\downarrow\downarrow}$ (blue, orange, green and red in \reffig{8_level_system}). The linewidth of the resonator $\kappa / 2\pi \approx 700$~kHz and the bandwidth of the SMPD $\sim300$~kHz are sufficient to span the four possible frequencies. \reffig{spectra_electron} shows four photon-counting spectra obtained by high-resolution spectroscopy with an $80$~\textmu s electron excitation pulse. The peak photon count occurs at a different frequency for each of the four nuclear spin states, which are clearly well-resolved. This enables single-shot readout of the nuclear spin register state by monitoring the spin-selective fluorescence.

The readout protocol is shown in \reffig{kmeans}a. Four $\pi$-pulses are consecutively applied, each of the pulses resonant with one of the EPR-allowed transitions. After each pulse, we measure the number of detector counts during an integration time $\tau_\mathrm{int}=1$~ms, repeated over $N_\text{RO}$ iterations. The total number of counts after each pulse is given by $C_{\uparrow \uparrow}$, $C_{\downarrow \uparrow}$, $C_{\uparrow \downarrow}$ and $C_{\downarrow \downarrow}$. We define 

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
   \Delta_x &= (C_{\uparrow \uparrow} + C_{\uparrow \downarrow}) - (C_{\downarrow \uparrow} + C_{\downarrow \downarrow}), \\
   \Delta_y &= (C_{\uparrow \uparrow} + C_{\downarrow \uparrow}) - (C_{\uparrow \downarrow} + C_{\downarrow \downarrow}).
\end{split} 
\end{equation}

The color indicates the prepared state: blue, orange, green and red correspond to $\ket{\uparrow \uparrow}$, $\ket{\uparrow \downarrow}$, $\ket{\downarrow \uparrow}$ and $\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}$, respectively. Background color indicates the state assignment regions calculated using a $k$-means algorithm.}
    \labfig{kmeans}
\end{marginfigure}

$\Delta_x$ ($\Delta_y$) effectively "traces out" the state of Qb2 (Qb1), while also cancelling the contribution of the dark counts to the photon-counting statistics. \reffig{kmeans}b plots the measured $\Delta_y$ versus $\Delta_x$ for each possible state of the nuclear spin manifold with $N_\text{RO}=200$ readout pulses. The color of the points represents the state in which the system was prepared before the measurement took place. There are four well-resolved distributions, each corresponding to a different prepared state of the two-qubit system. We use a $k$-means algorithm to separate the space corresponding to each distribution, displayed in the plot with the corresponding background color. The boundaries approximately correspond to the $x=0$ and $y=0$ curves as expected. The skewness in the boundaries can be attributed to inaccurate calibration of the driving frequencies $\omega_{\uparrow\uparrow}$, $\omega_{\downarrow\uparrow}$, $\omega_{\uparrow\downarrow}$ and $\omega_{\downarrow\downarrow}$, which would result in different counting statistics for each state. 

We note that the assignment of the prepared state is not perfect in \reffig{kmeans}b, which is indicative of non-negligible State Preparation and Measurement (SPAM) errors. These errors arise from non-idealities in the preparation and measurement procedures and ultimately limit the performance of any quantum computing platform. SPAM errors can be characterized and subsequently mitigated in complex measurements, such as state tomography. One such approach is the $T$-matrix SPAM correction technique \sidecite{lidar_quantum_2013}. The $T$ matrix has dimension $2^n \times 2^n$, where $n$ is the size of the quantum register; in our case, $n = 2$. Each column of the $T$ matrix corresponds to the measured probability distribution obtained after preparing a specific basis state. To correct for SPAM errors, the inverse of the $T$ matrix is applied to the results of any measurement. From the data in \reffig{kmeans}b, we extract the following $T$ matrix:

\begin{equation}
    T = \begin{pmatrix}
0.91 & 0.02 & 0.05 & 0.01 \\
0.01 & 0.92 & 0 & 0.06 \\
0.05 & 0 & 0.91 & 0.03 \\
0 & 0.05 & 0.01 & 0.94 
\end{pmatrix} 
\end{equation}

As discussed in \refsec{single_shot_ro_singlespin}, the main limiting factor of this scheme is the non-zero probability for the electron spin to relax through a nuclear spin flipping transition, which is proportional to $\left(A_\perp / 2\omega_I \right)^2$. These cross-relaxation events limit the number of readout pulses that can be applied before the readout becomes non-QND and are likely the main source of SPAM errors in our system.

\section{Stimulated Raman driving}
\labsec{stimulated_raman_ch4}

As discussed in \refsec{sota_ch4}, the nuclear spin driving schemes used in other solid-state platforms are not readily available. The limitations mainly arise from the enhanced electron spontaneous relaxation rate, which is required for readout. In addition, direct driving of the NMR-allowed transitions through rf pulses is not possible due to setup constraints. Therefore, we require a different method to control the nuclear spins, implemented with microwave drives.

As detailed in \refch{nuclea_spin_control}, the method of choice for coherent control of the \W nuclear spins is stimulated Raman driving. We derived the analytical Rabi frequency and discussed the origin of the \textit{differential AC-Zeeman shift}. We now briefly remind the reader of these concepts and introduce the subtleties that arise when a second \W qubit is strongly coupled to the \Er spin, which naturally leads to two-qubit gates.

Let us first consider a single \W qubit coupled to the \Er spin. To control the state of the nuclear spin through stimulated Raman driving, we simultaneously apply two detuned microwave pulses with amplitudes $\Omega_\text{A}$ and $\Omega_\text{B}$. The frequency detuning between the two pulses is defined as $\delta$ and the frequency detuning between drive A and the $\ket{\Downarrow \uparrow} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Uparrow \uparrow}$ transition is defined as $\Delta$, as depicted in \reffig{raman_scheme_v2}. The population between the two lower nuclear spin levels is coherently driven when $\delta = \omega_I^{(\Downarrow)} + \delta_\text{AC}$, where $\omega_I^{(\Downarrow)}$ is the bare NMR transition frequency and $\delta_\text{AC}$ corresponds to the differential AC-Zeeman shift due to the off-resonant driving of the microwave pulses given by Eq. \ref{eq:ac_zeeman}.

    \includegraphics{chapter4/figures/raman_scheme.pdf}
    \caption[Stimulated Raman Scheme]{\textit{(left)} Bare energy levels of an \Er spin coupled to a single \W nuclear spin qubit. We highlight the nuclear spin transition in the ground state of the \Er ground state with frequency $\omega_I^{(\Downarrow)}$. \textit{(right)} Two microwave pulses with amplitude $\Omega_\text{A, B}$ are applied on a four-level system. The detuning between drive A and the $\ket{\Downarrow \uparrow} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Uparrow \uparrow}$ is defined as $\Delta$. The frequency difference between the two pulses is defined as $\delta$. The bare energy levels (gray dashed lines) have been AC-Zeeman shifted by the strong off-resonance drives. The combined AC-Zeeman shift of the system results in an effective shift of the nuclear spin transition by $\delta_\text{AC}$}
    \labfig{raman_scheme_v2}
\end{figure}

We now return to the system with two \W nuclear spins. In the following, we concentrate on the control of Qb1, as the same methods apply to Qb2. We distinguish two cases, depending on the state of Qb2, highlighted in different colors in \reffig{2q_raman_scheme}a. The key difference between the two cases arises from the different Raman shifts $\Delta^{\uparrow, \downarrow}$ depending on the state of Qb2. The origin of this difference lies in the splitting of the EPR-allowed frequencies depending on the state of the nuclear spin. For the same stimulated Raman drives, $\Delta^{\uparrow} \approx \Delta^{\downarrow} + A_{\parallel}^{(2)}$.

\reffig{2q_raman_scheme}b shows the expected differential AC-Zeeman shift on the Qb1 transition as a function of $\Delta^{\uparrow}$ for both possible states of Qb2 and equal driving amplitudes $\Omega_\text{A} = \Omega_\text{B}$. The curves are calculated using Eq. \ref{eq:ac_zeeman} and only plot the region where $\Delta > \Omega_\text{Ram}$. As expected, the two quantities are similar but not equal. We define

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \delta_\mathrm{2qb} &\equiv \delta^\uparrow_\text{AC} - \delta^\downarrow_\text{AC}
\end{split}
\end{align}

shown in \reffig{2q_raman_scheme}c as a function of $\Delta^\uparrow$. Based on the value of $\delta_\mathrm{2qb}$ we can distinguish two regimes:

\newpage

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unconditional driving.} In this regime $\delta_\mathrm{2qb}\approx0$ and the Qb1 transition frequency is effectively independent of Qb2. It is obtained for $\Delta^{\uparrow} \approx \Delta_u \equiv -\omega_{I,1}/2 - A_\parallel^{(2)}/2$, where the driving speed can be maximized\sidenote{Experimentally, the unconditional regime applies when $|\delta_\mathrm{2qb}| \ll \Omega_\text{Ram}$. For $\Omega_\text{Ram}\approx0.1\,$kHz we observe tolerance for $\Delta^{\uparrow}$ variations of order $\sim 10\,$kHz while remaining unconditional.}.
    \item \textbf{Conditional driving.} For all other values of $\Delta^{\uparrow}$, the transition frequency for Qb1 is conditioned on the state of Qb2.
\end{itemize}

These two regimes are used for the single- and two-qubit gates, respectively. In the unconditional regime, the frequency of the NMR-allowed transitions does not depend on the state of Qb2, a crucial property for implementing single-qubit gates. On the contrary, conditional driving creates a native CNOT gate, which will be explored in detail in \refsec{cnot_gate}.

\subsection{Single-qubit gates}

We now perform spectroscopy and characterization of the two \W nuclear spins via stimulated Raman driving in the unconditional regime. We first perform Raman spectroscopy using the unconditional driving condition $\Delta/2\pi = -408.8$~kHz~$\approx \Delta_u/2\pi$, and $\Omega_A = \Omega_B$. The nuclear spin register is prepared in the $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ state, after which a Raman pulse is applied with detuning $\delta$, followed by nuclear spin readout. When the resonance condition is met, we observe a transfer of the nuclear spin population to either the $\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}$ or the $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$ state (see \reffig{spectra_and_rabi}a). The resonance linewidth is of order $100$~Hz or less, two orders of magnitude narrower than the electron spin linewidth, which confirms that the Raman driving occurs through virtual transitions of the electron excited states. The $\sim 2$~kHz splitting between the two NMR-allowed transitions reflects different values of $A_\parallel,A_\perp$ and $\omega_I$ for the two \W nuclear spins, due to their distinct positions relative to the \Er ion.

We now confirm the unconditional nature of the drive by studying the spectrum of Qb2 after preparation of Qb1 in either the $\ket{\downarrow}$ or the $\ket{\uparrow}$ state, as shown in \reffig{uncond_spectra}. At $\Delta \sim \Delta_u$, the two resonances occur at the same frequency, confirming the unconditional driving condition. Additionally, both curves are quantitatively reproduced by simulations based on the spin Hamiltonian performed by Z. Huang (see Appendix \ref{app:simulations}), using parameters determined by spectroscopic measurements.

Knowing the required drive frequencies, we then prepare the $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ state and sweep the drive duration, giving rise to coherent Rabi oscillations of each qubit, as shown in \reffig{spectra_and_rabi}b. This allows us to measure the Rabi Raman rate $\Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}}^{(1)} / 2\pi = 121(1)$~Hz and $\Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}}^{(2)} / 2\pi = 87(1)$~Hz for a specific microwave amplitude and calibrate the $\pi$-pulses for Qb1 and Qb2, respectively. 

As detailed in \refsec{driving_nuclear_spin_half}, the Rabi frequency of a stimulated Raman drive is proportional to the hyperfine coupling $A_\perp$. We can therefore use the measured values of $\Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}}^{(1,2)}$ to estimate $A_\perp$. In the unconditional regime with both drives having the same microwave amplitude, this relation is readily expressed as 

\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}} \approx \frac{\Omega^2_A A_\perp}{\omega_I^2}
\end{equation}

From the data presented in \reffig{spectra_and_rabi}b we obtain $A_\perp^{(1)} / 2\pi = 71(3)$~kHz and $A_\perp^{(2)} / 2\pi = 12.8(6)$~kHz. We note that there is a difference between the measured value of $A_\perp^{(1)}$ and the value inferred from time-trace statistics in the previous chapter (where we obtain $\sim$40~kHz). For the same reasons invoked in \refsec{sideband_driving_and_identification}, we consider the value measured by the Raman fit to be more precise, although limited by the precision in microwave amplitude.

In the process of running these experiments, we realized that the application of the microwave Raman driving pulses has an impact on the nuclear spin coherence. This is shown in the following experiment. We measured Raman Rabi oscillations extending over longer times than those previously shown. In \reffig{long_rabi}, we observe that the oscillations are damped with a characteristic time $T_{2, \mathrm{drive}} = 150(35)$ ms, much faster than $T_2^*$ for this qubit (see next section). We attribute this to an increase in the thermal population of the electron spin $\bar{n}_{\text{th}}$ during driving, which results in excess dephasing. The measurement data in \reffig{long_rabi} are fit to a simulation performed by Z. W. Huang (see Appendix \ref{app:simulations}), which yields a thermal population $\bar{n}_{\text{th}} = 9.5\times10^{-3}$, several orders of magnitude larger than the expected \Er excited state population at 10--20~mK. Due to time constraints, measurement of $T_{2, \mathrm{drive}}$ under different pulse amplitudes was not performed, which would be required to experimentally confirm the origin of this noise source.

\section{Nuclear spin coherence time}

We then proceed to measure the coherence properties of the nuclear spin qubits. We perform a Ramsey experiment on each qubit as shown in \reffig{ramsey_2qb} and extract $T_2^*$ times of 0.8(2)~s and 1.2(3)~s for Qb1 and Qb2, respectively. To our knowledge, these are the longest single-nuclear-spin Free-Induction-Decay times measured in a natural-abundance material by more than an order of magnitude. These times are on par with those measured in isotopically-enriched silicon~\sidecite{muhonen_storing_2014} and diamond~\sidecite{bartling_universal_2025}, which further confirms the interest of \Ca for spin-based quantum computing~\sidecite{ledantec_twentythreemillisecond_2021}. The residual decay is likely caused by a combination of $B_0$ drift and spectral diffusion due to nuclear spin bath reconfiguration. Coupled Cluster Expansion calculations, performed by Sen Lin and Ren-bao Liu, predict a decay time of $0.2$~s, shorter than the measurements. We interpret the longer experimental $T_2^*$ as being due to partial polarization of the $^{183}$W nuclear-spin bath by the pulses used to prepare the $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ state, an effect which has been observed in similar systems~\sidecite{taminiau_universal_2014, madzik_controllable_2020}. Additionally, the Larmor frequency of \W neighboring the \Er impurity experiences a paramagnetic shift due to the hyperfine coupling, which reduces the flip-flop interaction rate with the rest of the \W bath, also known as the frozen-core effect \sidecite{guichard_decoherence_2015}.

We performed Hahn echo measurements yielding $T_2$ times of 3.4(4)~s and 4.4(6)~s for qubits 1 and 2, respectively, as shown in \reffig{echo_2qb}. Coupled Cluster Expansion calculations predict a Hahn echo decay time of $10$~s due to nuclear spin bath dynamics. We believe the extra decoherence is due to residual excitations of the \Er ion, which occur at a rate $\bar{n} \Gamma_R$, $\bar{n} \approx 10^{-4}$ being the mean thermal occupation of the resonator mode, a value roughly consistent with the measured SMPD dark count rate. Energy relaxation of the two nuclear spin qubits likely occurs at a much lower rate, as indicated by ensemble measurements, which yield an upper bound of $\sim 10^{-6}~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ for the relaxation rate of $^{183}\mathrm{W}$ proximal to \Er:\Ca~\sidecite{wang_monthlonglifetime_2024}, and was not directly measured. Dynamically detuning the resonator from the \Er spin during pulse sequences while employing dynamical decoupling may offer a path to further extend these coherence times.


\section{Entangling the nuclear spins}
\labsec{cnot_gate}

We now proceed to implement two-qubit gates in our two-nuclear spin register. First we investigate the direct interaction between Qb1 and Qb2, which we find too small to perform entangling gates. We then proceed to demonstrate how to implement a CNOT by driving stimulated Raman transitions in the unconditional regime.

Being in close proximity, the two nuclear spins are coupled by the dipolar interaction $C_{zz} I_{z1} I_{z2}$. If strong enough, this interaction could be used to create a native two-qubit entangling gate. Consequently, we proceed by measuring $C_{zz}$ by performing a spin-echo double resonance (SEDOR) experiment~\cite{abobeih_atomicscale_2019}, as shown in \reffig{sedor}. This consists of a Hahn echo experiment performed on one nuclear spin qubit, the probe qubit, where either no pulse (red curve) or an additional $\pi$ pulse (green curve) is applied to the other qubit in the middle of the echo sequence. This yields a shift in frequency of the probe qubit equal to $C_{zz}/2$. We measure $C_{zz} / 2\pi = 0.8$~Hz, on the same order of magnitude as the qubit dephasing rate. Therefore, any gate implemented through direct nuclear--nuclear coupling will have a duration on the order of seconds, and it is desirable to find an alternative mechanism.

\section{Bell state tomography}
\labsec{bell_states}

We use this CNOT gate to generate the Bell states $\ket{\Phi^+} = \left(\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}+\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}\right)/\sqrt{2}$ and $\ket{\Psi^+} = \left(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}+\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\right)/\sqrt{2}$. \reffig{tomography_2qb} shows the preparation sequences as well as state tomography of the reconstructed density matrices of the two Bell states. For the purpose of characterization, we define the fidelity of a measured state $\rho$ with respect to a target state $\rho_t$ as \sidecite{nielsen_quantum_2010}

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F} = \left(\text{Tr}\left\{\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho_t} \cdot \rho \cdot \sqrt{\rho_t}}\right\}\right)^2
\end{equation}

SPAM error correction was applied during the reconstruction of the state tomography, which yields fidelities of 0.68 and 0.79 for the $\ket{\Phi^+}$ and $\ket{\Psi^+}$ states, respectively.

    \labfig{tomography_2qb}

We studied the contribution of the different coherent and incoherent error sources during Bell state preparation using the numerical simulations described in Appendix \ref{app:simulations}. We simulate the preparation sequence for $\ket{\Psi^+}$ including the required pulses for state tomography to account for the full experimental process. Then, we perform maximum likelihood estimation on the resulting set of measurement results to reconstruct the density matrix and extract the fidelity to the target state. 

When including only coherent errors (i.e. removing all decay and dephasing effects) the simulation yields a fidelity of 0.978; when including incoherent errors in addition, we find an expected fidelity of 0.976. The long coherence times of the two qubits ($\sim$ s) in comparison to the state preparation time ($\sim$ ms) explain the relatively small reduction in fidelity due to incoherent errors. Therefore, the identified coherent and incoherent errors are insufficient to account for the experimentally measured fidelity of 0.79. We attribute the discrepancy to the finite thermal population of the \Er electron spin under a driving field, an effect that was discussed in \refsec{stimulated_raman_ch4}. The increase in thermal population may enhance the dephasing rate due to the non-zero population of the \Er spin excited state. During tomography, the average pulse sequence was on the order of $30$~ms. Based on this and $T_{2, \mathrm{drive}}$ as measured in \refsec{stimulated_raman_ch4}, we expect approximately 15\% additional infidelity due to dephasing, which is roughly sufficient to account for the remaining error. Due to time constraints, measurement of $T_{2, \mathrm{drive}}$ under the exact conditions of the Bell state preparation sequence was not performed, and further investigation would be required to verify the origin of the reduced fidelity. 

\section{Decoherence-free subspace}

    \includegraphics{chapter4/figures/dfs.pdf}
    \caption[Decoherence-free subspace]{Decoherence-free subspace. Ramsey coherence measurement of the $\ket{\Phi^+}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}+\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}\right)$ (coral) state and the $\ket{\Psi^+}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}+\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\right)$ (purple) state, yielding $T_2^*$ values of $0.3(1)$ and $1.7(2)~$s, respectively. The final two $\pi/2$-pulses carry a phase $\phi$ as an artificial detuning, which gives rise to the oscillations. The data (dots) are fit to an exponentially decaying cosine (solid line).}
    \labfig{dfs}

As discussed in \refsec{sota_ch4}, both magnetic field fluctuations and dynamics of the nuclear spin bath are an important source of magnetic noise in solid-state systems. Neighboring nuclear spins experience the same field as well as correlated bath dynamics and a significant suppression of the correlated noise can be expected by preparing the state in specific Bell states~\sidecite{bradley_tenqubit_2019, bartling_entanglement_2022}. 

This improvement in coherence time can be traced to the magnetic properties of the prepared states; for example, the Bell state $\ket{\Psi^+}$ corresponds to a triplet state with a total spin of 1, and a magnetic projection of $m_S=0$. Compared to bare nuclear spin states, the energy difference between the $\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$ states is approximately 400 times smaller, which suppresses the effect of spatially correlated sources of magnetic field noise that affect both spins equally. As a result, $\ket{\Psi^+}$ forms an effective decoherence-free subspace, where quantum information can be preserved for a longer period of time.

We proceed to study this effect by measuring the coherence time of these multi-qubit states. We begin by preparing the system in $\ket{\Psi^+}$, followed by an evolution time $\tau$. Then, we project the state back to the single qubit basis with a $\pi/2$-pulse in each qubit, followed by readout of the populations. We define

\begin{equation}
    S = \left(P_{\downarrow \uparrow} + P_{\uparrow \downarrow}\right) - \left(P_{\downarrow \downarrow} + P_{\uparrow \uparrow}\right),
\end{equation}

which measures the coherence of the Bell state\sidenote{This quantity measures how much of the population remains in the $\{\ket{\downarrow \uparrow}, \ket{\uparrow \downarrow}\}$ manifold compared to the $\{\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}, \ket{\uparrow \uparrow}\}$. As the system decays into a mixed state, $S\rightarrow0$.}. \reffig{dfs} plots $S$ as a function of $\tau$. We measure a coherence time for $\ket{\Psi^+}$ of $1.7(2)~$s, which approximately doubles the individual qubit coherence time. In addition, we also measure the coherence of $\ket{\Phi^+}$, also a triplet state but with magnetic projection $m_S=1$. As expected, the measured coherence time for this state is $0.3(1)$, as correlated noise sources are enhanced due to the effective increase in gyromagnetic factor.

If we assume additive contributions of independent dephasing rates, we can consider that the coherence time for the two Bell states is given by

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \frac{1}{T_{2,\ket{\Psi^+}}^*} &= \sqrt{2}\pi\Gamma_\text{u} + \frac{1}{T_2}, \\
    \frac{1}{T_{2, \ket{\Phi^+}}^*} &= \sqrt{2}\pi\Gamma_\text{u} + 2\pi\Gamma_\text{c} + \frac{1}{T_2}.
\end{split}
\end{align} 

where $\Gamma_\text{u}$ and $\Gamma_\text{c}$ correspond to the dephasing rate due to spatially uncorrelated and correlated noise. For completely uncorrelated noise, the contribution to the decoherence rate for the two states is identical. However, correlated noise sources from the nuclear spin bath and $B_0$ fluctuations are suppressed to first order for $\ket{\Psi^+}$, as they couple identically to the two spins, due to their spatial proximity. From the measured coherence times, we extract the contributions of spatially correlated and uncorrelated noise, $\Gamma_\text{u} \approx 0.1 $ Hz and $\Gamma_\text{c} \approx 0.4 $ Hz. The amplitude of the correlated noise is in agreement with previous observations of magnetic field noise~\sidecite{wang_monthlonglifetime_2024}, pointing to this mechanism as the dominant term in correlated noise.

\section{Conclusion and outlook}

In conclusion, we have presented the building blocks for a quantum information processing platform using nuclear spins in the solid state as qubits. Primarily, we have demonstrated how to implement single- and two-qubit gates through stimulated Raman driving. This technique leverages the hyperfine-induced mixing of the electron and nuclear spin energy levels to drive coherent Raman transitions between the nuclear spin levels. We remark that this scheme requires the nuclear spin levels to be resolved and therefore long electron coherence times. In our particular case, the \Er spin coherence is $T_2^*=170$~\textmu s, and the Larmor frequency of the nuclear spins is readily resolved. This might not be the case in other systems with shorter electron spin coherence or weakly coupled nuclear spins. %In the case of NV centers and weakly coupled $^{13}$C nuclear spins, this is not 

%Compared to DD pulse sequences, this scheme can be readily implemented for systems with electronic spin with $S=1/2$ or with low coherence time. 

The fidelity of the gates demonstrated here remains significantly lower than the state of the art achieved in other color-center platforms, such as NV centers. A key limitation of the present scheme is that the application of the Raman drives substantially increases the nuclear spin decoherence rate.  We attribute this effect to an increased thermal occupation of the excited \Er state induced by the applied drives. To properly address this problem, an in-depth study would need to be performed, tracking the source of the problem, with the goal of developing strategies to suppress or mitigate their impact.

Readout fidelity is intrinsically limited by cross-relaxation events of the nuclear spin during the readout protocol. Increasing the quality factor of the superconducting resonator is a straightforward path to improve this figure. In addition, achieving a stronger Purcell rate would help address the long readout times of the experiment: readout of the joint 2-qubit state requires $\sim 1$~s per shot, which is long compared to a previous demonstration of $\sim20$~ms for optical QND readout of an \Er spin \sidecite{raha_optical_2020}. It should be possible to reduce the measurement time by orders of magnitude by increasing the Purcell-enhanced spin decay rate using a resonator with a parallel-plate capacitor design to enhance resonator-spin coupling~\sidecite{bienfait_reaching_2016,haikka_proposal_2017}. 

However, the specific parameter range for which this driving scheme is achievable is not wide: effective driving of the nuclear spin through a stimulated Raman process requires a low-quality resonator to avoid microwave filtering. Indeed, the Larmor frequency of the target nuclear spin must be on the same order of magnitude as the resonator's bandwidth $\kappa$. \W nuclear spins possess a low gyromagnetic moment, which yields a Larmor frequency of $\sim 800$~kHz at the operating magnetic field. Tuning the electron spin frequency with the resonator results in the two stimulated Raman drives being applied at $\omega_r \pm \omega_I / 2$. If the linewidth of the resonator were reduced by an order of magnitude, something desirable as it would enhance the Purcell factor, the necessary microwave amplitude of each drive to achieve the same Rabi rate would be $\sim 7$ times larger. This could be addressed by adapting the resonator design to have a tunable quality factor, as demonstrated in ref.~\sidecite{wen_addressing_2025}. One could envision a scheme where $\kappa$ is dynamically tuned to be wider during the pulse scheme and narrower during the readout sequence. 

If one were able to solve the aforementioned problems, the question of scaling the nuclear spin register becomes relevant. With rf access to the resonator, additional nuclear spin qubits could be interfaced using DDrf control techniques~\sidecite{bradley_tenqubit_2019, beukers_control_2025}. A possible resonator design would include a Bragg mirror in the form of a mismatched impedance transmission line, which would allow galvanic coupling of the resonator to the microwave lines~\sidecite{mahashabde_fast_2020}. However, this scheme would not be ideal, as the Purcell-enhanced relaxation rate of the \Er spin will ultimately limit its coherence time. This approach would be limited to around 10 qubits due to the weak dipolar coupling of \W spins to the \Er which decreases rapidly with distance. Distant \Er spins could be deterministically entangled using virtual photon exchange via the superconducting resonator~\sidecite{majer_coupling_2007, landig_virtualphotonmediated_2019}. Readout could be frequency multiplexed using multiple SMPDs, allowing multiple clusters of W spins coupled to \Er ancilla to be controlled via a single resonator. Beyond the \Er:\Ca system, many other crystal hosts and spin species could be investigated. Both the readout and control mechanisms are not limited by system-specific mechanisms, which opens the door to exciting new discoveries and developments in systems that are largely unexplored.
