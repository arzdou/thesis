\chapter{Detection and control of individual \W nuclear spins}
\label{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w}

Pushing the sensitivity of nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy to the single spin level would have a major impact in chemistry and biology and is the goal of intense research efforts~\cite{budakian_roadmap_2024,grob_magnetic_2019,mamin_nanoscale_2013}. Individual nuclear spins have been detected via their hyperfine coupling to an individual electronic paramagnetic system, itself measured by optical~\cite{neumann_singleshot_2010,taminiau_detection_2012,bourassa_entanglement_2020,uysal_coherent_2023} or electrical~\cite{vincent_electronic_2012,pla_highfidelity_2013,pla_coherent_2014,willke_hyperfine_2018,asaad_coherent_2020} means. These methods are however only applicable when suitable optical transitions or electron-spin-to-charge conversion mechanisms exist, and a more universal method is currently lacking. In this chapter, we report spectroscopic measurements of individual $^{183}\mathrm{W}$ nuclear spins in a CaWO$_4$ crystal via their hyperfine interaction with a neighboring $\mathrm{Er}^{3+}$ ion detected by microwave photon counting at millikelvin temperatures~\cite{lescanne_irreversible_2020,balembois_cyclically_2024,albertinale_detecting_2021,wang_singleelectron_2023}. We observe real-time quantum jumps of the nuclear spin state, a proof of their individual nature. We perform single-spin ELDOR-detected NMR spectroscopy~\cite{schosseler_pulsed_1994} by microwave driving the zero- and double-quantum transitions of the $^{183}$W--Er$^{3+}$ coupled system. By repeated driving of these transitions, we also achieve single-spin solid-effect dynamical nuclear polarization~\cite{abragama.proctorw._nouvelle_1958,abragam_principles_1978}. Relying exclusively on microwave driving and microwave detection, the methods reported here apply in principle to any nuclear spin coupled to a paramagnetic impurity, and therefore open the way to single-nuclear-spin spectroscopy in a large class of samples.

After identifying and characterizing individual \Er ions, we perform high-resolution spectroscopy by exciting the spin with an 80 \textmu s long Gaussian-shaped microwave $\pi$-pulse, with frequency $\omega$ followed by microwave fluorescence detection, sweeping $\delta = \omega - \omega_0$ over $2\pi \times 100$\,kHz. Each sweep is averaged 200 times to obtain the ensemble-averaged number of counts $\langle C \rangle (\omega)$. \reffig{quantum_jumps}.a shows such spectra measured repeatedly during 150 minutes for $\mathrm{Er}_1 - \mathrm{Er}_5$. Ion $\mathrm{Er}_1$ displays a single line, whose center frequency fluctuates on a timescale of several minutes, and on a frequency scale of $\sim 20$\,kHz. This spectral diffusion is attributed to a combination of $B_0$ drift, charge noise, and re-arrangements of the weakly coupled nuclear spin bath, and was observed for all measured ions although with a different intensity (see discussion below). Because the drift is slow, it can be compensated by various frequency-tracking strategies (see discussion below) which are used in all the measurements reported later in this chapter. In contrast, the electron spin frequencies of ions $\mathrm{Er}_2$, $\mathrm{Er}_3$, and $\mathrm{Er}_5$ display a clear telegraphic noise between two frequencies, four in the case of $\mathrm{Er}_4$. As demonstrated in the following, these jumps occur when one nearby $^{183}\mathrm{W}$ nuclear spin changes state. Quantum jumps are a hallmark of the measurement of individual quantum systems, and have been observed for trapped ions~\sidecite{nagourney_shelved_1986,bergquist_observation_1986,sauter_observation_1986}, photons in a high-Q microwave cavity~\sidecite{gleyzes_quantum_2007,guerlin_progressive_2007}, superconducting circuits~\sidecite{vijay_observation_2011}, and various nuclear spin systems~\sidecite{neumann_singleshot_2010,pla_highfidelity_2013,thiele_electrical_2013}; here, we report their observation on individual $^{183}\mathrm{W}$ nuclear spins by microwave fluorescence detection.

The transition frequency of each spectrum $\tilde{\delta}(t)$ is obtained from a Lorentzian fit, shown as a histogram in \reffig{quantum_jumps}.b for the five ions. Each histogram presents a different number of resolved distributions. For $\mathrm{Er}_2$, $\mathrm{Er}_3$ and $\mathrm{Er}_5$, the two resolved distributions indicate the presence of a strongly coupled neighboring nuclear spin. In contrast, $\mathrm{Er}_4$, shows four resolved distributions which correspond to two strongly coupled nuclear spins. The pair will be referred as $\mathrm{Er}_4^{(1)}$ and $\mathrm{Er}_4^{(2)}$ in the following. We note that the standard deviation of the individual distributions is significantly different for each ion, indicating that part of the drift originates from their local environment, likely from unresolved nuclear spin jumps.

\subsection{Time trace analysis}

The energy level structure presented in \refsec{w_er_hamiltonian} dictates that the difference in frequency between the two EPR-allowed transitions is a good estimate of the parallel hyperfine coupling $|A_\parallel|$ in the high-field regime. To extract this value we measure the difference in frequency between each time window 

\begin{equation}
    \Delta \tilde \delta (\tau) = \tilde \delta (\tau) - \tilde \delta (\tau - \Delta t), 
\end{equation}

which is plot in \reffig{trace_analysis}.a as a function of time for Er$_2$. Depending on the number of strongly coupled nuclear spins, the value distribution of $\Delta \tilde \delta$ will show different number of peaks (see \reffig{trace_analysis}.b).  The peak centered at $0$ arises when no resolved jump occurs in-between two consecutive traces. A Gaussian fit yields a Full-Width-Half-Maximum varying from 4.5 to 10 kHz, indicating that the short-term noise (at the minute scale) also varies significantly from ion to ion. The extra peaks observed for $\mathrm{Er}_{2-5}$ arise from resolved nuclear spin quantum jumps. Gaussian fits to these peaks yield the mean frequency jump size which corresponds to $|A_\parallel|$. The measured values are shown in \reftab{hyperfine_parallel_meas}. We note that the standard deviation of the satellite peaks is similar in value to the central peak, as expected.

The direct relaxation rate of $^{183}$W nuclear spins proximal to \Er impurities in \Ca is exceedingly slow at 10\,mK, with an upper bound of $\sim 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$~\sidecite{wang_monthlonglifetime_2024}. Therefore, we attribute this effect to cross-relaxation events, during which the electron spin relaxes through the weakly allowed ZQ and DQ transitions. This enables to determine the cross-relaxation probabilities introduced in \refsec{cross_relaxation_half} for the ZQ and DQ transitions,

\begin{equation}
    p_\text{ZQ, DQ} \approx \frac{\Gamma_{ZQ, DQ}}{\Gamma_R} \approx \frac{1}{1 + 4\tfrac{\omega_I^2}{\kappa^2}} \, \frac{A_\perp^2}{\omega_I^2}.
    \label{eq:cross_relaxation_prob}
\end{equation}

These probabilities can be measured by counting the number of jumps relative to the number of electron excitation events. The total number of jumps for the ZQ and DQ transitions $N_\text{ZQ, DQ}$ is straightforward to obtain from the histograms presented in \reffig{trace_analysis}.b as each event under the satellite peaks corresponds to a single cross-relaxation event. The number of electron excitations $N_\text{ex}$ depends on the step-size of the frequency sweep. Because detuned $\pi$-pulses still drive the electron spin when they fall within the linewidth, every pulse contributes with a weight set by the lineshape. Thus, the total excitation count per sweep can be estimated by summing over all applied pulses, each weighted by a Lorentzian profile with the fitted linewidth. The cross-relaxation probability is given by

\begin{equation}
    p_\text{ZQ, DQ} = \frac{N_{ZQ, DQ}}{N_\text{ex}}
\end{equation}

Table I shows the cross-relaxation probabilities for the coupled nuclear spins of $\mathrm{Er}_{2-5}$. The measured values were extracted from longer traces, this is particularly important when the cross-relaxation rate is low as significant statistics require long measurement time. We note that, for $\mathrm{Er}_{4}^{(1)}$, there is significant difference between $p_{ZQ}$ and $p_{DQ}$. This is consistent with the nuclear spin spending a considerably longer amount of time in one of the states compared to the other, as noted through visual inspection of \reffig{quantum_jumps}. A large detuning with respect to the resonator center can induce this effect, as the Purcell enhancement of the two transitions is equal only when $\omega_S=\omega_r$. However, the measured detuning is not enough to explain the large disagreement between the two cross-relaxation rates. Further studies are needed to understand the origin of this difference, but a working hypothesis is the existence of an extra, non-radiative relaxation channel mediated through another \Er spin. Finally, from Eq. \ref{eq:cross_relaxation_prob} we estimate $A_\perp$ by averaging both probabilities, which is presented alongside with $A_\parallel$ in \reftab{hyperfine_parallel_meas}.

\subsection{Tracking procedure}

\begin{marginfigure}
    \includegraphics{chapter3/figures/rough_tracking.pdf}

The two traces are separated by $\Delta t \sim 2$ minutes and the data (dots) are fit to a Lorentzian (line). The red arrow indicates the drift in the EPR-allowed transition after the waiting time.}
    \labfig{rough_tracking}
\end{marginfigure}

As discussed in the previous section, the frequency drift on the EPR-allowed frequencies is on the same order of magnitude as the linewidth of the transition. Without active tracking and compensation of the drift, the fluorescent intensity of the transition will not be constant, resulting in a reduced SNR. However, the noise has a characteristic timescale of minutes, which is slow when compared to the duration of our pulse sequences. Therefore, it is possible to track the drift in between averages, which allows to compensate it during the next sequence. 

A rudimentary but straightforward tracking protocol can be implemented by performing high-resolution spectroscopy with a small sweeping range around the electron transition frequency\sidenote{To account for nearby nuclear spins, we perform dynamical nuclear polarization before tracking (see \refsec{dnp_half})}. The ensemble averaged number of counts $\langle C \rangle (\delta)$ is then fitted to a Lorentzian, as shown in \reffig{rough_tracking} The center of the fit yields the new electron spin transition frequency and the pulses are updated for the next measurement. This algorithm is straightforward and reliable, but the main drawback is the time it takes to obtain a reasonable fit, approximately 1 minute. Therefore, can only be realistically run a limited number of times, resulting in poor tracking.

Due to the limitations of the direct tracking procedure, we envisioned a second algorithm focused on speed rather than fine control. This method exploits the properties of a Ramsey measurement as frequency sensor. Let's begin by considering a Ramsey measurement, where the spin is sent to the equatorial plane of the Bloch sphere by a $\pi/2$-pusle where it freely evolves during a time $\tau$ before another $\pi/2$-pulse is applied and the population of the spin state is measured. In our case, the measurement is performed via the SMPD and results in a number of counts $C$. 

During the free evolution period, the electron spin accumulates a phase proportional to the transition frequency and the evolution time. Any drift $\delta \omega$ in the transition frequency therefore appears as an additional phase shift\sidenote{As measured before, the drift in our system has a timescale of minutes, while the coherence of the electron is on the hundreds of \textmu s. It is therefore reasonable to assume that the transition frequency is constant during the evolution time of the Ramsey sequence.} $\phi = (\omega_S + \delta \omega) \tau$. Since $\omega_S \gg \delta\omega$, directly resolving small changes in $\phi$ is difficult. To address this, the second Ramsey pulse includes an artificial phase that effectively compensates most of the rapid oscillation due to $\omega_S$, leaving only the slower drift-dependent component.

One possible strategy for tracking the drift would be to measure the full Ramsey fringe and fit the oscillation frequency, but this method will again be limited by the data acquisition time. Instead, the tracking can be greatly accelerated by probing only a single time point on the Ramsey curve, chosen for maximal sensitivity. This point corresponds to the zero crossing of the Ramsey oscillation, where the population of the electron spin is linear to $\delta \omega$. For this single-point Ramsey sensor, sensitivity is maximized when $\tau\sim T_2^*$ as it balances long phase accumulation time with low information loss due to decoherence.

    \includegraphics{chapter3/figures/tracking.pdf}

    \textbf{a.} \textit{(top)} Simulated Ramsey measurement under phase cycling. The signal was generated with a dephasing time of 50~\textmu s and artificial detuning of 20~kHz and 21~kHz (red and gray respectively).     \labfig{tracking_ramsey}

In practice, fluctuating dark counts introduce a varying offset in the measured counts $C$. We remove this offset by phase cycling: the Ramsey sequence is repeated with an added $\pi$ phase shift on the second pulse, producing a second measurement $\bar C$. Subtracting the two, $C - \bar C$, cancels the offset and yields a signal that is linear in $\delta\omega$, provided that $\tau \ll 2\pi / \Delta\omega$ (see \reffig{tracking_ramsey}a).

The quantity $C - \bar C$ can be used as the process variable of a Proportional-Integral (PI) loop with a setpoint at zero, integrated within the FPGA logic. In practice, we define the variable $Y_i$ which carries a short memory by filtering the values of the sensor output for every iteration $i$ of the PI loop,

\begin{equation}
Y_i = \sum_{j=0}^{i} (C_{j} - \bar{C}_{j})\, e^{-(j-i)/f} \approx (1 - 1/f)\, Y_{i-1} + (C_{i} - \bar{C}_{i}),
\end{equation}

where $f = 2000$ is the memory of the system. Finally, the PI controller calculates the frequency correction to be applied on the pulses through a proportional and an integral channel,

\begin{equation}
\delta\omega_{i} = P\, Y_{i} + I \sum_{j=0}^{i} Y_{j}.
\end{equation}

The values for P and I were set by manually optimizing the convergence time after artificially detuning the pulse frequency. \reffig{tracking_ramsey}b shows a block diagram with the control loop.

The second tracking procedure is used to compensate for the drift in all measurements presented in this chapter and the next, unless explicitly stated.

\section{Single-Shot readout}

We leverage the ability to spectrally resolve the different allowed transition frequencies of the electronic spin to perform a projective, quantum-non-demolition measurement of the nuclear spin state (see \reffig{single_shot_readout_pulses}.a). We apply sequences of frequency-resolved excitation pulses at $\omega_S - A/2$ and $\omega_S + A/2$. The pulses are Gaussian-shaped, 80~\textmu s-long and spaced by an integration time $\tau_{\mathrm{int}}$ during which the fluorescence counts are measured; repeating this $N_\mathrm{RO}$ times yields the number of counts after each sequence $C_\uparrow$ and $C_\downarrow$. \reffig{single_shot_readout_pulses}.b shows the described pulse sequence.

We continuously record $C_\uparrow$ and $C_\downarrow$ for ion $\mathrm{Er}_2$ over a 7-hour period using $N_{\mathrm{RO}}=10^3$ readout pulses per sequence. The two time traces (see \reffig{single_shot_readout}.a) are strongly anti-correlated. The photon-counting histograms for each trace show two well separated distributions. We note that the distribution centers are not identical, and we measure a lower count signal for $C_\uparrow$. We attribute this difference to imperfect tuning of the SMPD center frequency and the resulting bias in detector efficiency.

After reaching a maximum value, the probability exponentially decreases with $N_\mathrm{RO}$, due to the finite cross-relaxation probability $p_{ZQ, DQ}$ at each readout step. \paragraph{SNR of the measurement}

We consider a nuclear spin prepared in $\ket{\downarrow}$. Since the spin is completely polarized, the measured counts $C_\uparrow$ will arise solely from detector dark-counts and can be modelled as a Poissonian random variable with rate $N_\mathrm{RO} \, \Gamma_\mathrm{DC}\, t_\mathrm{d}$ where $\Gamma_\mathrm{DC}$ is the detector  dark-count rate and $t_\mathrm{d}$ is the duration of each detection window. 

In contrast, the measured counts $C_\downarrow$ contain both detector dark counts and real fluorescence clicks. We model the fluorescence clicks as a binomial variable with sample size $N_\mathrm{RO}$ and success probability $\eta$ (the spin-to-click efficiency). The total distribution for $C_\downarrow$ is obtained from direct addition of these two distributions which is not trivial. However, when the Poissonian rate and the binomial sample size are large, both distributions can be approximated to Gaussian, and the sum is simply a Gaussian with mean and variance given by the sum of the means and variances respectively. 

The distributions for $C_\uparrow$ and $C_\downarrow$ are then

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    &C_\uparrow \sim \mathcal{N} (\mu_{\uparrow}, \sigma^2_{\uparrow}) \\
    &\mu_{\uparrow} = \sigma^2_{\uparrow} = N_\mathrm{RO} \, \Gamma_\mathrm{DC} \, t_\mathrm{d}
\end{split} 
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    &C_\downarrow \sim \mathcal{N} (\mu_{\downarrow}, \sigma_{\downarrow}^2) \\
    &\mu_{\downarrow} = N_\mathrm{RO} \left(\eta + \Gamma_\mathrm{DC}\, t_\mathrm{d} \right) \\
    &\sigma^2_{\downarrow} = N_\mathrm{RO} \left(\eta \, (1 - \eta) +  \Gamma_\mathrm{DC} \, t_\mathrm{d} \right)
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The quantity used for single-shot readout corresponds to the difference in counts $\delta C = C_\downarrow - C_\uparrow$, which is given by 

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    &\delta C \sim \mathcal{N} (\mu_{\delta}, \sigma_{\delta}^2) \\
    &\mu_{\delta} = \mu_{\downarrow} - \mu_{\uparrow} =   N_\mathrm{RO} \eta \\
    &\sigma^2_{\delta} = \sigma^2_{\downarrow} + \sigma^2_{\uparrow} = N_\mathrm{RO} \left(\eta \, (1 - \eta) + 2 \Gamma_\mathrm{DC} \, t_\mathrm{d} \right)
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The threshold-based nuclear spin state assignment translates to an integral of the probability density function of the $\delta C$ distribution from the threshold (here taken as 0 since we do not include detector bias in the model) and $\infty$ and the probability of measuring the prepared state is

\begin{equation}
    p_\mathrm{SNR}(\downarrow | \downarrow, N_\mathrm{RO}) = \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{\sigma_\delta \sqrt{2\pi}} \, e^{-\frac{(x - \mu_\delta)^2}{2\sigma^2_\delta}} dx = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 - \text{Erf}\left[\frac{\text{SNR}}{\sqrt{2}}\right]\right)
\end{equation}

where SNR corresponds to the Signal-to-Noise Ratio,

\begin{equation}
    \text{SNR} = \frac{\mu_\delta}{\sigma_\delta} = \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\eta \cdot (1 - \eta) +  2\Gamma_{DC}\, t_D}} \cdot \sqrt{N_{RO}}.
\end{equation}

The SNR is proportional to $\sqrt{N_\mathrm{RO}}$ which leads to $\mathcal{F}_\mathrm{SNR}$ with $N_\mathrm{RO}$. The prefactor contains detector specific parameters which can be measured from the fluorescence curve of the spin as detailed in \refsec{individual_er_ion_characterization}, which for this precise measurement yield $\eta = 0.18$, $\Gamma_\mathrm{DC} = 150$ s$^{-1}$, $t_\mathrm{d} = 2.6$ ms.

\paragraph{Population evolution due to cross-relaxation}

The cross-relaxation events introduce a dynamic change in the population of the nuclear spin states. The population evolution can be modeled through a system of discrete rate equations by considering the finite probabilities of cross-relaxation $p_\mathrm{ZQ,DQ}$,

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \frac{\Delta P_\downarrow}{\Delta N_\mathrm{RO}} \: = \: &p_\text{DQ} P_\uparrow - p_\text{ZQ} P_\downarrow, \\
    \frac{\Delta P_\uparrow}{\Delta N_\mathrm{RO}}  \: = \: &p_\text{ZQ} P_\downarrow - p_\text{DQ} P_\uparrow, \\
    P_\uparrow + &P_\downarrow = 1.
\end{split}
\end{equation}

However, $p_\mathrm{ZQ,DQ} \ll 1$ and the dynamics of the system are much slower than the step size of $N_\mathrm{RO}$, and it can be approximated to $\frac{\Delta P_\downarrow}{\Delta N_\mathrm{RO}} \approx \frac{d P_\downarrow}{d N_\mathrm{RO}}$. We solve the equations by considering the initial condition $P_\downarrow(0) = P_0 < 1$ which yields

\begin{equation}
    P_\downarrow(N_\mathrm{RO}) \: = \: (P_0 - \frac{p_\text{DQ}}{p_\text{DQ} + p_\text{ZQ}})\cdot e^{-(p_\text{DQ} + p_\text{ZQ})\cdot N_\mathrm{RO}} + \frac{p_\text{DQ}}{p_\text{DQ} + p_\text{ZQ}}.
\end{equation}

The populations exponentially converge to a stable state which depends on the relative cross-relaxation probabilities. In the following, we consider that both cross-relaxation rates are equal $p_\text{ZQ} \approx p_\text{DQ} = p_\text{x}$, and complete polarization of the nuclear spin state at the beginning of the sequence $P_0=1$ and the equation can be simplified to

\begin{equation}
    P_\downarrow(N_\mathrm{RO}) \: \approx \: \frac{1}{2}\left( 1 + e^{- 2 p_\text{x} \, N_\mathrm{RO}}\right), 
\end{equation}

And the system converges to a balanced mixed state when $N_\mathrm{RO} \gg p_\text{x}$; larger $p_x$ gives faster deterioration of the prepared nuclear population and thus limits the number of usable readout pulses.

\paragraph{Total fidelity of the measurement} The final readout fidelity is set by a combination of the two processes described above, with different scaling on $N_\mathrm{RO}$. On one hand the SNR of the measurement improves with repetitions and larger values of $N_\mathrm{RO}$ result in better discrimination between the two states ($\mathrm{SNR} \propto \sqrt{N_{\mathrm{RO}}}$). On the other hand, each pulse carries a small probability to flip the nuclear spin through a cross-relaxation event, which accumulates and drives the nuclear population towards a mixed state with an exponential dependence on $N_{\mathrm{RO}}$.

Because these two effects act approximately independently on the assignment probability, the overall probability to correctly read out a prepared state can be well approximated by weighting the discrimination success by the population remaining in the prepared level:

\begin{equation}
    p(\downarrow \; \mid \; \downarrow, N_{\mathrm{RO}}) \approx p_{\mathrm{SNR}}(\downarrow \; \mid \; \downarrow, N_{\mathrm{RO}})\; P_{\downarrow}(N_{\mathrm{RO}}),
\end{equation}

This model only has one fitting parameter, $p_x$, and an equivalent expression can be found for the complementary process when preparing into $\ket{\uparrow}$. Fitting the model to the experimental data of \reffig{single_shot_vs_NRO} returns $p_x \approx 2.9\pm0.3\times 10^{-4}$ as an average over preparations in $\ket{\downarrow}$ and $\ket{\uparrow}$.

Using the relation between the cross-relaxation probability and the perpendicular hyperfine coupling given by Eq. \ref{eq:cross_relaxation_probability_half}, the measured value of $p_x$ corresponds to a perpendicular hyperfine coupling $A_\perp/2\pi \approx 71 \pm 7$~kHz. \red{compare to other measurement}

The model also yields an upper bound on the achievable single-shot readout fidelity by optimizing $N_{\mathrm{RO}}$ to balance SNR gain and cross-relaxation loss. For the parameters of our detector and the extracted $p_x$, this optimal average fidelity is

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_\mathrm{RO} =\frac{1}{2} \left[p\left(\downarrow \; \mid \; \downarrow, N^{(\max)}_{\mathrm{RO}}\right) + p\left(\uparrow \; \mid \; \uparrow, N^{(\max)}_{\mathrm{RO}}\right) \right] = 0.93.
\end{equation}

The fidelity of the readout is ultimately limited by the nuclear spin cross-relaxation rate. To further increase the performance of the scheme, two strategies are possible. First, enhancing detector sensitivity will yield an increase of SNR, in the ideal limit of unit efficiency and vanishing dark counts, a single electron excitation would suffice for nuclear spin single shot readout where the probability of cross-relaxation, and thus the fidelity of the scheme, is $1/p_x$. However, there is an intrinsic trade off with coherent control of the nuclear spin. As discussed in the following sections, a larger perpendicular hyperfine coupling increases control speed but also increases $p_x$, therefore degrading readout fidelity. Consequently, improving readout quality requires pursuing schemes that reduce cross-relaxation. For example, active control of the resonator's bandwidth has been demonstrated in the context of ESR spectroscopy \red{yutian reference}. Reducing the bandwidth of the resonator during the nuclear spin readout will also suppress the Purcell enhancement of the ZQ and DQ transitions, and thus reducing the cross-relaxation probability $p_x$.

\section{Sideband driving and nuclear spin identification}

Nuclear spin spectroscopy is achieved by driving the forbidden transitions, in a single-spin version of the Electron-Double-Resonance-detected NMR (ELDOR-detected NMR) experiment ~\sidecite{schosseler_pulsed_1994}. Following preparation in state $\ket{\downarrow}$ (resp. $\ket{\uparrow}$) (see \refsec{dnp_half}) a flattop-shaped pulse is applied at a frequency $\omega_S + \delta$, The maximum amplitude of the pulse, $\Omega$, is quantified by the Rabi frequency of the allowed transitions. A reduction (resp. increase) in the nuclear spin state probability $P_\Downarrow$ is observed when $\omega_S + \delta$ matches the forbidden DQ and ZQ transitions. Data are shown in \reffig{sb_spectroscopy}.a \& b for $3$ values of $\Omega$, showing a frequency shift and power-broadening.

\section{Conclusion and outlook}

A logical next step will be to extend these methods to nuclear spins more weakly coupled to the electron spin, using methods demonstrated with NV centers in diamond~\sidecite{taminiau_detection_2012,cujia_tracking_2019,du_singlemolecule_2024}. This would open the way to $^{183}\mathrm{W}$-nuclear-spin-based quantum registers operated and read-out via the $\mathrm{Er}^{3+}$ spin, generalizing recent $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ quantum registers demonstrations operated by a NV center in diamond~\sidecite{bradley_tenqubit_2019,abobeih_faulttolerant_2022}. Perhaps the most interesting aspect of the methods reported here is their applicability to a large range of nuclear spin systems in a large variety of samples, crystalline or not. Indeed, individual paramagnetic centers can be detected by microwave fluorescence provided their Purcell relaxation rate $\Gamma_R$ can be increased above $\sim 10^3 \mathrm{s}^{-1}$, and above their non-radiative relaxation rate at $10$\,mK. These conditions can likely be met in a variety of paramagnetic systems, ranging from organic radicals to transition-metal ions in metallo-enzymes, which could be deposited on top of the resonator constriction in the form of small molecular crystals or even frozen solutions. Applying the techniques demonstrated here may then yield their hyperfine couplings to individual ligand nuclear spins with a higher spectral resolution than reached with ensemble measurements.
