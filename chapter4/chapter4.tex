\section{Introduction}

Nuclear spins in the solid-state are attractive candidates for quantum computing, offering long coherence times~\cite{steger_quantum_2012,pla_highfidelity_2013a, zhong_optically_2015, maurer_roomtemperature_2012a} and high density. However, the typically weak coupling of nuclear spins to their environment that enables such long coherence times makes their control and readout challenging. Single-shot QND readout and control of the electron spin of rare earth ion defects in the solid state via their optical transitions using nanophotonic cavities has been demonstrated\cite{raha_optical_2020a, kindem_control_2020a}. Furthermore, a frequency-erasing photon detection scheme was recently used to herald remote entanglement between up to three such ions\cite{ruskuc_scalable_2024}. However, nuclear spin qubits are usually addressed through their hyperfine coupling to an electron spin ancilla~\cite{pla_coherent_2014a, madzik_precision_2022, abobeih_onesecond_2018a, waldherr_quantum_2014}, which then needs to be detected. Electrical detection of electron spin donors in silicon enabled the demonstration of long-lived single-$^{31}$P nuclear spin qubits in isotopically-enriched silicon~\cite{muhonen_storing_2014a}, the operation of a two-$^{31}$P quantum gate~\cite{madzik_precision_2022}, and the control~\cite{fernandezdefuentes_navigating_2024} and preparation of a $^{123}$Sb $7/2$-nuclear spin in a Schr\"odinger-cat state~\cite{yu_creation_2024}. Electrical detection was used also for TbPc$_2$ molecular quantum dots, enabling the manipulation and readout of a spin-3/2 $^{159}$Tb nuclear spin~\cite{thiele_electrically_2014a} and the implementation of Grover's search algorithm on a single $^{159}$Tb nucleus~\cite{godfrin_operating_2017}. Optical detection of individual NV center electron spins in diamond enabled the operation of a register of up to ten $^{13}$C nuclear spin qubits~\cite{bradley_tenqubit_2019}, the fault-tolerant operation of a logical qubit utilizing five $^{13}$C nuclear-spin qubits~\cite{abobeih_faulttolerant_2022b}, and the demonstration of high-fidelity single- and two-qubit gates with a $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ nuclear spin~\cite{bartling_universal_2024}. Individual nuclear spin control has also been demonstrated using other color centres in diamond~\cite{beukers_control_2025, maity_mechanical_2022, nguyen_quantum_2019}, rare earth ions~\cite{uysal_coherent_2023a} and SiC~\cite{babin_fabrication_2022}.

In this chapter, we build upon the results highlighted in the previous chapter and propose a hybrid architecture to control and read out individual nuclear spin qubits via an electron spin defect ancilla, in turn magnetically coupled to a high-Q superconducting resonator. Requiring relatively straightforward fabrication techniques, this approach permits a single resonator to control numerous coherent spin defects. The techniques used are also readily applicable to a wide range of paramagnetic defects with nearby nuclear spins. Individual all-microwave qubit control on a timescale of milliseconds is achieved by driving a stimulated Raman transition. Two-qubit gates are achieved by taking advantage of induced AC-Zeeman shifts on the energy level structure of the electron and nuclear spin system. We use this to construct a CNOT gate and generate Bell states of the two nuclear spin qubits. We further show that certain Bell states exist in a so-called decoherence free subspace and are inherently resilient to magnetic field noise, extending $T_2^*$ from $0.8(2)~$s and $1.2(3)~$s for the individual qubits to $1.7(2)~$s for the two-qubit protected state.

\section{Readout and preparation}

We extend the readout and preparation protocols from the previous chapter to the two nuclear spin Er$_4$ system. Preparation into arbitrary nuclear spin states is done through a combination of sideband pumping (see \refsec{dnp_half}) and single-qubit gates (see next section). Sideband pumping is achieved by driving the electron-nuclear transitions (represented as gray arrows in \reffig{8_level_system}) with a $\tau_{\mathrm{prep}}=3.5$~ms length square pulse followed by $\tau_\text{w}$ = 2~ms of wait time, allowing the electron spin to decay. This sequence is then repeated $N_\text{prep}=50$ times to maximize the preparation efficiency. To prepare $\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}$ we drive the high frequency sidebands. To cover both Qb1 and Qb2 sidebands, the frequency of the pulse is chirped 50 kHz. To prepare other states, we apply the appropriate single-qubit gates after initializing in $\ket{\downarrow \downarrow}$.

The data was taken with an integration time of $\tau_\text{int}=1$~ms}
    \labfig{spectra_electron}

Readout exploits the spectral separation of the electron allowed transitions $\omega_{\uparrow\uparrow}$, $\omega_{\uparrow\downarrow}$, $\omega_{\downarrow\uparrow}$ and $\omega_{\downarrow\downarrow}$ (blue, orange, green and red in \reffig{8_level_system}). The linewidth of the resonator $\kappa \approx 700$~kHz and the bandwidth of the SMPD $\sim200$~kHz are sufficient to span the four possible frequencies and the measured spin coherence $T_2^*=170$~\textmu s, allows us to frequency-resolve them. \reffig{spectra_electron} shows four photon-counting spectra obtained by high-resolution spectroscopy with an $80$~\textmu s electron excitation pulse. Each trace was acquired after deterministically preparing the nuclear spins in one of the four possible nuclear spin configurations.  We see that the peak photon count occurs at a different frequency for each of the four nuclear spin states, and that the peaks are all clearly distinguishable, allowing for high fidelity readout of the nuclear spin state.

Each point corresponds to the value of $\Delta_x$ and $\Delta_y$ measured after preparing in a specific state.     \labfig{kmeans}

To measure the state of the nuclear spin we use the photon counting statistics obtained after sending a $\pi$-pulse at each of the four resonant frequencies. We define $C_{\uparrow \uparrow}$, $C_{\downarrow \uparrow}$, $C_{\uparrow \downarrow}$ and $C_{\downarrow \downarrow}$ as the accumulated number of counts after applying a $\pi$-pulse with frequency matching each of the electron spin transitions respectively. We then introduce 

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
   \Delta_x &= (C_{\uparrow \uparrow} + C_{\uparrow \downarrow}) - (C_{\downarrow \uparrow} + C_{\downarrow \downarrow}), \\
   \Delta_y &= (C_{\uparrow \uparrow} + C_{\downarrow \uparrow}) - (C_{\uparrow \downarrow} + C_{\downarrow \downarrow}).
\end{split} 
\end{equation}

$\Delta_x$ ($\Delta_y$) effectively "traces out" the state of Qb2 (Qb1) as well as cancelling the contribution of the dark counts to the photon counting statistics. \reffig{kmeans} plots the measured $\Delta_y$ versus $\Delta_x$ for each possible state of the nuclear spin manifold with $N_\text{RO}=200$ readout pulses. The color of the points represents the state in which the system was prepared before the measurement took place. There are four well resolved distributions, each corresponding to a different prepared state of the two-nuclear-spin system. We use a $k$-means algorithm to separate the space corresponding to each distribution, displayed in the plot with the corresponding background color. The boundaries approximately correspond to the $x=0$ and $y=0$ curves as expected. The skewness in the boundaries can be attributed to inaccurate calibration of the driving frequencies $\omega_{\uparrow\uparrow}$, $\omega_{\downarrow\uparrow}$, $\omega_{\uparrow\downarrow}$ and $\omega_{\downarrow\downarrow}$, which would result in different counting statistics for each state. The measurement probability after preparation is of 0.91, 0.93, 0.92 and 0.95 to be in the desired state $\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}$, $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$, $\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$, respectively. As discussed in the previous chapter, the main limiting factor of this scheme is the non-zero probability for the electron spin to relax through a nuclear spin flipping transition, which is proportional to $\left(A_\perp / 2\omega_I \right)^2$ (see \red{section ref}). These cross-relaxation events limit the number of readout pulses that can be applied before the readout becomes non-QND.

\section{Stimulated Raman driving.}

A straightforward method to implement single-qubit gates on the qubits would be to directly drive the nuclear transitions in the radiofrequency regime. However, we are constrained by the experimental setup, since the electron spins are addressed via a resonator, filtering any excitation outside its bandwidth. Therefore, we require a different method to control the nuclear spins, which can be implemented with microwave pulses. As discussed in \refch{nuclea_spin_control}, the method of choice used for coherent control of the \W nuclear spins is stimulated Raman driving.

In \refsec{raman_driving} we introduce the principle of stimulated Raman driving, derived the analytical Rabi frequency and discussed the concept of the \textit{differential AC-Zeeman shift}. We now proceed to briefly remind the reader the concept of stimulated Raman driving in the case of a single qubit and introduce the subtleties that arise when a second \W qubit is strongly coupled to the \Er spin, from which we naturally implement two-qubit gates.

Let us first consider a single \W qubit coupled to the \Er spin. To control the state of the nuclear spin through stimulated Raman driving, we simultaneously apply two detuned microwave pulses with amplitudes $\Omega_\text{A}$ and $\Omega_\text{B}$. The frequency detuning between the two pulses is defined as $\delta$ and the frequency detuning between the drive A and the $\ket{\Downarrow \uparrow} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Uparrow \uparrow}$ is defined as $\Delta$, as depicted in \reffig{raman_scheme_v2}. A coherent transfer of population between the two lower nuclear spin levels is activated when $\delta = \omega_I^{(\Downarrow)} + \delta_\text{AC}$, where $\omega_I^{(\Downarrow)}$ is the bare NMR transition frequency and $\delta_\text{AC}$ corresponds to the differential AC-Zeeman shift due to the off-resonant driving of the microwave pulses.

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics{chapter4/figures/raman_scheme.pdf}
    \caption[Stimulated Raman Scheme]{\textit{(left)} Bare energy levels of an \Er spin coupled to a single \W nuclear spin qubit. We highlight the nuclear spin transition in the ground state of the \Er ground state with frequency $\omega_I^{(\Downarrow)}$. \textit{(right)} Two microwave pulses with amplitude $\Omega_\text{A, B}$ are applied on a four-level system. The detuning between drive A and the $\ket{\Downarrow \uparrow} \leftrightarrow \ket{\Uparrow \uparrow}$ is defined as $\Delta$. The frequency difference between the two pulses is defined as $\delta$. The bare energy levels (gray dashed lines) have been AC-Zeeman shifted by the strong off-resonance drives. The combined AC-Zeeman shift of the system results in an effective shift of the nuclear spin transition by $\delta_\text{AC}$}
    \labfig{raman_scheme_v2}
\end{figure}

We now return to the system with two \W nuclear spins. In the following, we concentrate on the control of Qb1, as the same methods apply to Qb2. We differentiate two cases, depending on the state of Qb2, highlighted in different colors in \reffig{2q_raman_scheme}a. The key point in the diagram comes from the different Raman shifts $\Delta^{\uparrow, \downarrow}$ depending on the state of Qb2. The origin of this difference lies on the split of the electron allowed frequencies and is evident that for the same Raman drive, $\Delta^{\uparrow} \approx \Delta^{\downarrow} + A_{\parallel}^{(2)}$.

Using Eq. \ref{eq:ac_zeeman} we can calculate the expected differential AC-Zeeman shift on the Qb1 transition for both possible states of Qb2 and equal driving amplitudes $\Omega_\text{A} = \Omega_\text{B}$ (see \reffig{2q_raman_scheme}b). Stimulated Raman driving is a second order process that requires $\Delta \gg \Omega_\text{Ram}$ and the figure only plots $\delta^{\downarrow, \uparrow}_\text{AC}$ where this condition is met. As expected, the two quantities are similar but not equal, and we naturally introduce the following quantities:

\begin{align}
\begin{split}
    \delta_{2qb} &\equiv \delta^\uparrow_\text{AC} - \delta^\downarrow_\text{AC}, \\
    \bar{\delta}_\text{AC} &\equiv (\delta^\uparrow_\text{AC} + \delta^\downarrow_\text{AC})/2,
\end{split}
\end{align}

\reffig{2q_raman_scheme}c shows $\delta_{2qb}$ as a function of $\Delta^\uparrow$. Based on the value of $\delta_{2qb}$ we can distinguish two regimes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unconditional driving.} In this regime the Qb1 transition frequency is effectively independent of Qb2. It is obtained for $\Delta^{\uparrow} \approx \Delta_u \equiv -\omega_{I,1}/2 - A_\parallel^{(2)}/2$, where the driving speed is also close to its maximum. Experimentally, this is realized when $|\delta_{2qb}| \ll \Omega_\text{Ram}$. For $\Omega_\text{Ram}\approx0.1\,$kHz we observe tolerance for $\Delta^{\uparrow}$ variations of order $\sim 10\,$kHz while remaining unconditional. \red{appendix with derivation?}
    \item \textbf{Conditional driving.} For all other values of $\Delta^{\uparrow}$, the transition frequency for Qb1 is conditioned on the state of Qb2.
\end{itemize}

These two regimes respectively define the single- and two-qubit gates. To implement a single-qubit gate, the frequency of the nuclear spin transition should not depend on the state any other qubit of the register, which is preserved in the unconditional regime. On the contrary, the conditional driving creates a native CNOT gate, as we discuss in the following sections.

\subsection{Single-qubit gates}

We now perform spectroscopy and characterization of the two \W nuclear spins via stimulated Raman driving in the unconditional regime. We first perform Raman spectroscopy using the unconditional driving condition $\Delta/2\pi = -408.8$~kHz~$\approx \Delta_u/2\pi$, and $\Omega_A = \Omega_B$. The nuclear spin register is prepared in the $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ state, after which a Raman pulse is applied whose detuning $\delta$ is swept, followed by nuclear spin readout. When the resonance condition is met, we observe a large change in the nuclear spin population to either the $\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}$ or the $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$ state (see \reffig{spectra_and_rabi}a). The resonance linewidth is of order $100$~Hz or less, two orders of magnitude narrower than the electron spin linewidth, which confirms that the Raman driving occurs through virtual transitions of the electron excited states. The $\sim 2$~kHz frequency separation between the two ground-state nuclear spin transitions is due to the different values of $A_\parallel,A_\perp$ and $\omega_I$ for each \W nuclear spin, due to their different position relative to the \Er ion. 

\begin{marginfigure}
    \includegraphics{chapter4/figures/uncond_spectra.pdf}
    \caption[Unconditional spectroscopy]{Spectroscopy of Qb2 with unconditional pulse conditions, $\Delta / 2\pi=-404.8~\text{kHz} \approx \Delta_u/2\pi$ with Qb1 prepared in state $\ket{\downarrow}$ (top) or $\ket{\uparrow}$ (bottom). Solid line is the result of a simulation using the QuTiP package, based on the measured spin Hamiltonian parameters.}
    \labfig{uncond_spectra}
\end{marginfigure}

We now confirm the unconditional nature of the drive by studying the spectrum of Qb2 after preparation of Qb1 in either the $\ket{\downarrow}$ or the $\ket{\uparrow}$ state \reffig{uncond_spectra}. At $\Delta \sim \Delta_u$, the two resonances occur at the same frequency, confirming the unconditional driving condition. Additionally, both curves are quantitatively reproduced by simulations based on the spin Hamiltonian, using parameters determined by spectroscopic measurements.

Knowing the required drive frequencies, we then prepare the $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ state and sweep the drive duration, giving rise to coherent Rabi oscillations of each qubit, as shown in \reffig{spectra_and_rabi}b. This allows us to measure the Rabi Raman rate $\Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}}^{(1)} / 2\pi = 121(1)$~Hz and $\Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}}^{(2)} / 2\pi = 87(1)$~Hz. for a specific microwave amplitude and calibrate the $\pi$-pulses for Qb1 and Qb2, respectively. 

As detailed in section \refsec{raman_driving}, the frequency of the Rabi oscillations of a nuclear spin under a Raman drive is proportional to the hyperfine coupling $A_\perp$. Consequently, we can use the measured values of $\Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}}^{(1,2)}$ to estimate $A_\perp$. In the unconditional regime with both drives having the same microwave amplitude, this relation is readily expressed as 

\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{\mathrm{Ram}} \approx \frac{\Omega_A\Omega_B A_\perp}{\omega_I^2}
\end{equation}

From the data presented in \reffig{spectra_and_rabi}b we obtain $A_\perp^{(1)} / 2\pi = 71(3)$~kHz and $A_\perp^{(2)} / 2\pi = 12.8(6)$~kHz. We note that there is a difference between the measured value of $A_\perp^{(1)}$ compared to the measurement using time trace statistics presented in the previous chapter (where we obtain $\sim$40~kHz). This difference is readily explained by the nature of the time trace measurement, for which rapid changes in the nuclear spin state will be hidden by averaging artifacts. Therefore, the measurement of large values of $A_\perp$ will be biased towards a lower estimation if the SNR limit of the measurement is reached. We take the value measured by the Raman fit to be more precise, although limited by the precision in microwave amplitude.

\section{Coherence measurements}

We then proceed to measure the coherence characteristics of the nuclear spin qubits using unconditional Raman driving. We perform a Ramsey experiment on each qubit as shown in  \reffig{ramsey_2qb} and extract $T_2^*$ times of 0.8(2)~s and 1.2(3)~s for Qb1 and Qb2, respectively. To our knowledge, these are the longest single-nuclear-spin Free-Induction-Decay times measured in a natural-abundance material by more than an order of magnitude. These times are on par with those measured in isotopically-enriched silicon~\sidecite{muhonen_storing_2014} and diamond~\sidecite{bartling_universal_2024}, which further confirms the interest of \Ca for spin-based quantum computing~\sidecite{ledantec_twentythreemillisecond_2021}. The residual decay is likely caused by a combination of $B_0$ drift and spectral diffusion due to nuclear spin bath reconfiguration. Coupled Cluster Expansion calculations predict a decay time of $0.2$~s, shorter than the measurements. We interpret the longer experimental $T_2^*$ as being due to partial polarization of the $^{183}$W nuclear-spin bath by the pulses used to prepare the $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ state, an effect which has been observed in similar systems~\sidecite{taminiau_universal_2014, madzik_controllable_2020}.

We performed Hahn echo measurements yielding $T_2$ times of 3.4(4)~s and 4.4(6)~s for qubits 1 and 2, respectively, as shown in \reffig{echo_2qb}. Coupled Cluster Expansion calculations predict a Hahn echo decay time of $10$~s due to nuclear spin bath dynamics. We believe the extra decoherence is due to residual excitations of the \Er ion, which occur at a rate $\bar{n} \Gamma_R$, $\bar{n} \approx 10^{-4}$ being the mean thermal occupation of the resonator mode, a value roughly consistent with the measured SMPD dark count rate. Energy relaxation of the two nuclear spin qubits likely occurs at a much lower rate, as indicated by ensemble measurements, which yield an upper bound of $\sim 10^{-6}~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ for the relaxation rate of $^{183}\mathrm{W}$ proximal to \Er:\Ca~\sidecite{wang_monthlonglifetime_2024}, and was not directly measured. Dynamically detuning the resonator from the Er spin during pulse sequences while employing dynamical decoupling may offer a path to further extend these coherence times.


\section{Entangling the nuclear spins}

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics{chapter4/figures/sedor.pdf}
    \caption[SEDOR measurement]{Spin-echo double resonance (SEDOR) measurement (dots) performed on Qb1, where sequence 1 and sequence 2 (as shown above) yielded the red and green curves respectively. The fits (solid lines) yield a nuclear-nuclear coupling rate $C_{zz}/2\pi = 0.8$~Hz.}
    \labfig{sedor}
\end{figure}

Being in close-proximity, the two nuclear spins are coupled by the dipolar interaction $C_{zz} I_{z1} I_{z2}$. If strong enough, this interaction could be used to create a native two-qubit entangling gate. Consequently, we proceed by measuring $C_{zz}$ by performing a Spin Echo DOuble Resonance (SEDOR) \red{explain in the intro} experiment~\cite{abobeih_atomicscale_2019}, as shown in \reffig{sedor}. This consists of a Hahn echo experiment performed on one nuclear spin qubit, the probe qubit, where either no pulse (red curve) or an additional $\pi$ pulse (green curve) is applied to the other qubit at the middle of echo sequence. This yields a shift in frequency of the probe qubit equal to $C_{zz}/2$. We measure $C_{zz} / 2\pi = 0.8$~Hz, on the same order of magnitude as the qubit dephasing rate. Therefore, the coupling is insufficient to implement a two-qubit entangling gate, and we instead use the conditional AC-Zeeman shifts of the Raman drive.

We now study the spectrum of Qb2 after preparation of Qb1 in either the $\ket{\downarrow}$ or the $\ket{\uparrow}$ state  in the unconditional regime with the objective of implementing a two-qubit entangling gate. For this purpose, we try to maximize the value of $\delta_{2qb}$. We set $\Delta/2\pi=110$~kHz and $\Omega_B = 5\Omega_A$. This asymmetry is introduced to help preserve the Raman regime ($\Omega_A \ll \Delta$ and $\Omega_B \ll \Delta+\delta_0$) when the value of $\Delta$ is reduced. \reffig{cond_spectra}a shows the two spectra of Qb2 depending on the state of Qb1, from which we measure $\delta_{2qb}/2\pi=244(8)$~Hz. Additionally, both curves are quantitatively reproduced by simulations based on the spin Hamiltonian, using parameters determined by spectroscopic measurements.

After preparing the $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ or $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$ states, we drive Qb2 using the $\Delta/2\pi = 110$~kHz conditional driving condition at 810.13~kHz, as shown in \reffig{cond_spectra}b. For a 6.72~ms drive pulse, the $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ state is driven to the $\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}$ state, while the same pulse has no effect on the $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$ state. Thus, this pulse directly enacts an inverse CNOT gate.

\subsection{Bell state tomography}

We use this inverse CNOT gate to generate the Bell states $\ket{\Phi^+} = \left(\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}+\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}\right)/\sqrt{2}$ and $\ket{\Psi^+} = \left(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}+\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\right)/\sqrt{2}$. We perform state tomography and extract the reconstructed density matrices of the two Bell states, shown in \reffig{tomography_2qb}. For the purpose of characterization, we define the fidelity of a measured state $\rho$ with respect to a target state $\rho_t$ as \sidecite{nielsen_quantum_2010}

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F} = \left(\text{Tr}\left\{\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho_t} \cdot \rho \cdot \sqrt{\rho_t}}\right\}\right)^2
\end{equation}

However, to accurately estimate the fidelity of the generated state, it is necessary to account for State Preparation And Measurement (SPAM) errors. These errors arise from non-idealities in the preparation and measurement procedures and ultimately limit the performance of any quantum computing platform. SPAM errors can be characterized and subsequently mitigated in complex measurements, such as state tomography. One such approach is the $T$-matrix SPAM correction technique \sidecite{lidar_quantum_2013}. The $T$ matrix has dimension $2^n \times 2^n$, where $n$ is the size of the quantum register; in our case, $n = 2$. Each column of the $T$ matrix corresponds to the measured probability distribution obtained after preparing a specific basis state. To correct for SPAM errors, the inverse of the $T$ matrix is applied to the results of any measurement. From the data presented in \reffig{kmeans}, we extract the following $T$ matrix:

\begin{equation}
    T = \begin{pmatrix}
0.91 & 0.02 & 0.05 & 0.01 \\
0.01 & 0.92 & 0 & 0.06 \\
0.05 & 0 & 0.91 & 0.03 \\
0 & 0.05 & 0.01 & 0.94 
\end{pmatrix} 
\end{equation}


SPAM error correction was applied during the reconstruction of the state tomography shown in \reffig{tomography_2qb}. After SPAM correction, we measure fidelities of 0.68 and 0.79 for the $\ket{\Phi^+}$ and $\ket{\Psi^+}$ states, respectively.

%Further study of the errors at play in the system will require the use of randomized benchmarking, which was not performed in the current experiment due to time constraints.

Numerical simulations were used to estimate the contribution of different error sources. When simulating the generation and tomography of the $\ket{\Psi^+}$ state while including only coherent errors, we obtain a final state fidelity of 0.978. When incoherent errors are also included (excluding spurious electron-spin excited-state population), the expected fidelity is reduced slightly to 0.976. The remaining discrepancy between the simulated and experimentally measured fidelities may be attributed to an increased thermal population of the Er spin during microwave driving.

\subsubsection{Simulation details}

The simulation tool is based on the QuTiP and Dynamiqs packages to simulate the system Hamiltonian under the influence of driving and $T_1$ and $T_2^*$ decay. The simulation considers an electron spin coupled to two nuclear spins and does not include a cavity or background spin bath. The spin system is described by

\begin{align}
\hat{H}_{0} = &\ \omega_S \hat{S}_{z} + \omega_{I,1} \hat{I}_{z,1} + \omega_{I,2} \hat{I}_{z,2}  \notag \\
          &+ A_\parallel^{(1)} \hat{S}_z \cdot \hat{I}_{z,1} + A_\perp^{(1)} \hat{S}_z \cdot \hat{I}_{x,1} \notag \\
          &+ A_\parallel^{(2)} \hat{S}_z \cdot \hat{I}_{z,2} + A_\perp^{(2)} \hat{S}_z \cdot \hat{I}_{x,2}
\end{align}

All parameters used in $\hat{H}_{0}$ are experimentally determined. An additional driving term with frequency $\omega$ is modeled by the Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_d = \frac{\hbar\Omega_d}{2}(\hat{S}_{-}e^{i\omega t} + \hat{S}_{+}e^{-i\omega t}),
\end{equation}

where $\Omega_d$ is the pulse amplitude, $\hat{S}_{+}$ and $\hat{S}_{+}$ are the lowering and raising operators for the Er spin respectively. The pulse amplitude used in the simulation is calibrated using the measured electron spin Rabi frequency and pulse durations are matched to those used in the experiment.

The simulation results follow from solving the master equation with the total time-dependent Hamiltonian

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{dt} = \frac{1}{i \hbar} \left[ \hat{H}_{\text{tot}}, \hat{\rho} \right] + \sum_k \left( \hat{L}_k \hat{\rho} \hat{L}_k^\dagger - \frac{1}{2} \left\{ \hat{L}_k^\dagger \hat{L}_k, \hat{\rho} \right\} \right),
\end{equation}

where $\hat{H}_{\text{tot}} = \hat{H}_{\text{0}} + \hat{H}_{\text{d}}$. The electron energy relaxation process is modeled by the Lindblad operators $\hat{L}_{s_-} = \sqrt{\Gamma_{1} (\bar{n}_{\text{th}} + 1)} \, \hat{S}_{-}$ and $\hat{L}_{s_+} = \sqrt{\Gamma_{1} (\bar{n}_{\text{th}})} \, \hat{S}_{+}$, where $\Gamma_{1} = \frac{1}{T_1}$ is the electron relaxation rate. Dephasing of the nuclear spins is modeled by $\hat{L}_{n_\varphi,i} = \sqrt{\Gamma_{\varphi}} \, \hat{I}_{z,i}$ for $i=1,2$, where $\Gamma_{\varphi} = \frac{1}{T_2^*}$ is the nuclear dephasing rate. The parameter $\bar{n}_{\text{th}}$ models the finite thermal population of the electron spin. The effect of this term was observed experimentally on Qb1 as an increased dephasing rate under the influence of driving as shown in \reffig{long_rabi}. We observe Rabi oscillations decaying with a characteristic decay time $T_{2 \rho} = 150(35)$ ms, much faster than $T_2^*$ for this qubit. This may be attributed to an increase in $\bar{n}_{\text{th}}$ during driving which results in excess dephasing. A value of $\bar{n}_{\text{th}} = 9.54\times10^{-3}$ was used in the simulation in \reffig{long_rabi} to match the observed decay rate.

These simulations were used to investigate the origin of coherent and incoherent errors during Bell state preparation. We simulate the Bell state preparation sequence that yielded the $\left(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}+\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\right)/\sqrt{2}$ Bell state shown in \reffig{tomography_2qb}. We include subsequent basis rotations used for tomographic reconstruction and project the results into the $\{\ket{\uparrow},\ket{\downarrow}\}$ measurement basis to simulate the full experimental tomography process. We then perform maximum likelihood estimation on the resulting set of measurement results to reconstruct the density matrix and extract the fidelity to the target state. When including only coherent errors (i.e. removing all decay and dephasing effects) the simulation yields a fidelity of 0.978; when including incoherent errors in addition we find an expected fidelity of 0.976. The long coherence times of the two qubits ($\sim$ s) in comparison to the state preparation time ($\sim$ ms) explains the relatively small reduction in fidelity due to incoherent errors. However, the coherent errors are insufficient to account for the experimentally measured fidelity of 0.79. We attribute the remaining error to the finite thermal population of the Er electron spin under a driving field causing a reduced dephasing time as discussed previously and illustrated in \reffig{long_rabi}. Due to time constraints, measurement of $T_{2 \rho}$ under the exact conditions of the Bell state preparation sequence was not performed. During tomography, the average pulse sequence was on the order of $30$~ms. Based on this and $T_{2 \rho}$ as measured in \reffig{long_rabi}, we expect approximately 15\% additional infidelity due to dephasing, which is roughly sufficient to account for the remaining error. Further investigation would be required to verify this. We also note that this analysis only considers Markovian effects and non-Markovian terms, such as spin-bath dynamics, could be an extra source of decoherence. 


\subsection{Decoherence free subspace}

\begin{figure}[b]
    \includegraphics{chapter4/figures/dfs.pdf}
    \caption[Decoherence free subspace]{Decoherence free subspace. Ramsey coherence measurement of the $\ket{\phi^+}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}+\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}\right)$ (coral) state and the $\ket{\psi^+}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}+\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\right)$ (purple) state, yielding $T_2^*$ values of $0.3(1)$ and $1.7(2)~$s respectively. The data (dots) are fit to an exponentially decaying cosine (solid line).}
    \labfig{dfs}
\end{figure}

In \reffig{dfs}, we measure the coherence of the $\ket{\Phi^+}$ (coral) state and the $\ket{\Psi^+}$ (purple) state, where the quantity plotted on the $y$-axis is a linear combination of the populations of the four possible nuclear spin qubit states. This quantity measures the coherence of the Bell states. Both are triplet states with a total spin of 1, and differ in the projection of the angular momentum on the z-axis, $m=1$ and 0 respectively. We see that the $\ket{\Psi^+}$ state has a significantly longer $T_2^*$ than the $\ket{\Phi^+}$ state; indeed, it also exceeds that of either individual nuclear spin qubit. This is due to the fact that the energy difference between the $\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$ states is approximately 400 times smaller than the energy between single nuclear spin transitions, which suppresses the effect of spatially correlated sources of magnetic field noise that affect both spins equally~\sidecite{bartling_entanglement_2022}. The coherence time of the state $\ket{\Psi^+}$ can be expressed as $T_{2,\ket{\Psi^+}}^* = ( \sqrt{2}\pi\Gamma_\text{u} +1/T_2)^{-1}$, where $\Gamma_\text{u}$ is the dephasing rate due to spatially uncorrelated noise, which we attribute to fluctuations in the nuclear spin bath. In contrast, state $\ket{\Psi^+}$ is doubly sensitive to spatially correlated noise compared to single nuclear spin states; $T_{2, \ket{\Phi^+}}^* = (\sqrt{2}\pi\Gamma_\text{u} +2\pi\Gamma_\text{c} +1/T_2)^{-1}$, where $\Gamma_\text{c}$ is the dephasing rate due to spatially correlated noise. From the measured coherence times, we extract the contributions of spatially correlated and uncorrelated noise, $\Gamma_u \approx 0.1 $ Hz and $\Gamma_c \approx 0.4 $ Hz. The amplitude of the correlated noise is in agreement with previous observations of magnetic field noise \sidecite{wang_monthlonglifetime_2024}, pointing to this mechanism as the dominant term in correlated noise.

\section{Conclusion and outlook}

In conclusion, we have presented a new platform for quantum information processing utilizing nuclear spins in the solid state. We use an SMPD to detect the fluorescence of an \Er spin coupled to a superconducting resonator, and use this to perform single-shot QND readout on two nearby nuclear spins. We utilize stimulated Raman drives to coherently control the nuclear spins and generate an entangled state. A key limitation of the experiment was measurement time: readout of the joint 2-qubit state requires $\sim 1$~s per shot, in comparison to a previous demonstration of $\sim20$~ms for optical QND readout of an Er$3+$ spin \sidecite{raha_optical_2020}. It should be possible to reduce the measurement time by orders of magnitude by increasing the Purcell-enhanced spin decay rate using a resonator with a parallel-plate capacitor design to enhance resonator-spin coupling~\sidecite{bienfait_reaching_2016,haikka_proposal_2017}. Furthermore, readout fidelity is intrinsically limited by cross-relaxation events of the nuclear spin during the readout protocol. Increasing the quality factor of the superconducting resonator is a straightforward path to improve this figure. To scale to larger system size, additional nuclear spin qubits could be interfaced using dynamical decoupling control techniques~\cite{bradley_tenqubit_2019, beukers_control_2025}; this approach would be limited to around 10 qubits due to the weak dipolar coupling of $^{183}$W spins to the \Er which decreases rapidly with distance. Distant \Er spins could be deterministically entangled using virtual photon exchange via the superconducting resonator~\cite{majer_coupling_2007, landig_virtualphotonmediated_2019}. Readout could be frequency multiplexed using multiple SMPDs, allowing multiple clusters of W spins coupled to Er ancillae to be controlled via a single resonator. Multiple such resonators could be coupled together, or interfaced with standard superconducting qubits. Alternatively, utilizing the optical telecom C-band transitions of \Er defects would open up the possibility of long-distance communication between clusters of nuclear spin qubits. Beyond the \Er:\Ca system, many other crystal hosts and spin species could be investigated. The techniques demonstrated here are not limited to quantum information processing and may find utility in quantum sensing or electron paramagnetic resonance. The potential to explore a range of alternative systems and leverage the techniques described for new applications promises exciting new discoveries and developments in a platform that is largely unexplored.
