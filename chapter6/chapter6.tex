\chapter{Conclusion and future directions}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section*{Summary}

The objective of this thesis has been to demonstrate an all-microwave platform for the detection of nuclear spins in the solid state using an electronic spin as an ancilla, and to show how this system can be used for quantum information and precision spectroscopy.

In Chapter \ref{ch:detection_and_control_of_individual_w}, we establish the basis for nuclear spin detection by monitoring the fluorescence of a coupled electronic spin. This demonstration relies on several key technologies. First, the Single Microwave Photon Detector provides the sensitivity required to count individual photons in the microwave regime. Second, Purcell enhancement of radiative spin transitions of \Er ions in \Ca boosts the microwave emission beyond the phonon relaxation rate. By combining these two technologies, the group had previously detected single \Er spins. Using high-resolution EPR spectroscopy, we resolve the hyperfine splitting of the \Er spin coupled to a neighboring \W. We exploit the frequency differences of the EPR transitions, which depend on the \W nuclear spin state, to define a nuclear spin readout protocol. In addition, we demonstrate solid-effect dynamical nuclear polarization of the nuclear spin environment surrounding the \Er.

Chapter \ref{ch:individual_nuclear_spins_quantum_information} builds on these techniques to construct a two-qubit register composed of two \W nuclear spins in close proximity to an \Er. We demonstrate coherent control using microwave stimulated Raman scheme and define single- and two-qubit gates by exploiting microwave-induced energy level shifts. We show that, owing to the low magnetic noise environment of \Ca, nuclear spins achieve coherence times on the order of seconds. These coherence times can be further extended by preparing a near-zero magnetic dipole Bell state that is inherently protected from correlated magnetic noise.

Finally, in Chapter \ref{ch:nb_nuclear_spin}, we chemically and structurally identify a \Nb impurity neighboring an \Er spin using the techniques developed in the previous chapters. Leveraging the long nuclear spin coherence, we measure the NMR transitions with hertz-level precision and fit them to a Hamiltonian model. We observe a significant shift in the nuclear quadrupole interaction depending on the electronic spin state, which we attribute to a previously unobserved form of coupling term between the electron spin and the nuclear quadrupole in the solid state. This interaction originates from the magnetically induced electric dipole of the \Er spin and is mediated by the crystal lattice. Finally, we use high-resolution data to place an upper bound on the hexadecapole interaction of the \Nb that is four orders of magnitude tighter than previous solid-state NMR results.

\section*{Perspectives}

The procedures and techniques demonstrated in this thesis are a proof-of-concept that can be greatly expanded in many directions. 

\subsection*{Improving SNR with new designs}

The experiments presented in this manuscript rely on a single-shot readout protocol of the nuclear spins. However, achieving sufficiently high readout fidelity requires approximately 200 electron excitation pulses. Given that each electron spin readout takes approximately 1 ms, the total readout sequence becomes relatively long, on the order of 200~ms. Increasing the SNR of the photon counting measurement is therefore a primary requirement for reducing the overall duration of each experiment, which opens the door to faster data acquisition and the implementation of more complex quantum control and feedback protocols.

As discussed in \refch{spins_coupled_to_a_cavity}, the design of the superconducting spin resonator tries to maximize the coupling to the \Er spins in the \Ca substrate. This is done by minimizing its impedance by creating large interdigitated capacitors, shunted by a small nanowire. For convenience, the resonator is capacitively coupled to the driving lines, but this design choice forbids radiofrequency pulses from ever reaching the spins, and direct nuclear spin driving is therefore unachievable. There are two straightforward, although technically challenging, improvements in the design

By reducing the impedance of the resonator the coupling to the spins is proportionally increased. Given the relaxation rate of the electron spins is proportional to the square of the coupling, a significant increase Purcell effect is expected. This can be achieved by fabricating parallel plate capacitors separated by a thin dielectric membrane, a design that maximizes capacitance and therefore minimizes impedance for the same frequency range. Finite element simulations predict an increase of an order of magnitude in the coupling, yielding a Purcell factor of $10^{16}$.

Directly connecting the resonator to the driving lines through a metallic bond would allow one to send RF pulses to the resonator and therefore resonantly drive the nuclear spins. Additionally, DC pulses can be used to dynamically tune the resonance frequency by leveraging the response of the nanowire's kinetic inductance to a DC current. To avoid direct losses to the line, the resonator would be protected by a transmission line with mismatched impedance sections, which plays the role of a microwave Bragg mirror.

\subsection*{Connecting nuclear spin registers}

One key limitation of the quantum computing platform presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:individual_nuclear_spins_quantum_information} is scalability: the number of nuclear spins strongly coupled to a single \Er impurity is very limited. Within the first unit cell, there are only ten \W atoms, of which only approximately 14\% possess nuclear spins. One possible route to overcome this limitation is to use the resonator as a mediator between different \Er impurities. By implementing the design improvements described previously, reaching the strong-coupling regime between \Er spins and the reasonator should be feasible. In this regime, spatially separated \Er spins could be entangled via virtual-photon exchange mediated by the resonator.

Moreover, rare-earth ions are promising candidates for the implementation of quantum communication protocols due to their intrinsic spin-photon interface and optical transitions, which lie close to or within the telecom C-band, where fiber losses are minimal. By leveraging this spin-photon interface, distant \Er impurities could communicate with one another, enabling long-distance entanglement between distinct nuclear-spin clusters.

\subsection*{Probing new substrates}

So far, the only substrate and paramagnetic impurity combination that has been studied using single microwave photon counting at the single ion level is \Er:\Ca. The choice to focus on this combination is motivated by the excellent properties of the system: the substrate has low microwave losses and low nuclear spin density. Additionally, the \Er in \Ca has a strong gyromagnetic factor, which yield a large coupling strength to the resonator. However, microwave photon counting is a technique that could, in principle, be applied to many electron spin systems, in a wide variety of samples, ranging from organic radicals to transition-metal ions in metallo-enzymes. One can also envision a setup in which the samples are deposited on top of the resonator, instead of being present on the substrate, on the form of nanoparticles or a frozen solution. 

Currently, the group is working with erbium doped Y$_2$O$_3$ nanoparticles which have been deposited on top of the nanowire with the use of a micromanipulator tweezer. The complex spin signals observed in the sample promise a rich and interesting system and lay the path forward to state-of-the-art analysis of new samples.
